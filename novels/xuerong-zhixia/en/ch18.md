# Chapter Eighteen　Empty Bottles

## 1. Masks

Su Tang logged on at eight every morning.

The customer-service back end was a web page: a column of conversations on the left, the chat box on the right, and along the top a row of red numbers, the unanswered messages. She propped her phone on a stack of textbooks on her desk; the computer was her dad’s old laptop, which took three minutes to boot and whose fan sounded like a hairdryer when it got going. While her two brothers were in their online classes, the computer was hers.

The messages on Town Matters were different from when the epidemic was at its worst. Fewer requests for help, more questions. Which barber’s had reopened, when could you book a driving test, which corner had the old man who sold vegetables outside the estate moved to, could someone post a transfer, a pram, ninety per cent new, baby grew too fast.

And there was one woman who sent a message at ten every morning on the dot: Still no eating in today?

Every day Su Tang replied: Not yet~ waiting for the notice~

Every day the woman replied with a crying face.

Later Su Tang found out the woman ran a noodle shop opposite the county hospital, had run it for eleven years, and the pot of bone broth at the door had never once been off the boil. Now the fire had been out for two months.

---

Her parents’ attitude to this job started out as “signing a contract is a good thing,” and slowly turned into something else.

When her mum came home from work every day—the textile mill had reopened at half capacity last week, and her mum was on the day shift—the first thing she said coming in was “Tired today?”, and the second was “How’s your boss doing?” When her dad watched the news and saw some company laying people off, some factory stopping work, he called her over and pointed at the TV and said, see, your company’s doing all right, isn’t it?

“It’s all right.”

“Good. You work hard there, get on well with the boss.”

“I know.”

“So young, holding up a whole show like that on his own,” said her dad. “Don’t just take the wages, if there’s anything you can help with, help.”

And then they started stuffing things into her bag.

First a pot of steamed flower rolls her mum made; her mum said she’d made too many, they couldn’t finish them, take them to your boss. Then two cases of milk her dad’s work had handed out; her dad said he didn’t like milk, take them. Then a jar of spicy pickled cabbage her grandma had made when she was alive, the bottom of the crock, the very last of it; her mum hesitated for ages, then packed one container of it after all.

“Tell him not to wear himself out.” Her mum pressed the lid of the container down, then pressed it again. “Young people staying up all night, they don’t feel it now, but it all has to be paid back later.”

“Mum, he doesn’t listen to me.”

“Then say it a few more times.”

“…”

“And,” said her mum, “when school starts, no letting it get in the way of lessons. Work is work, study is study. If you drop out of the top fifty in the midterm, you’re giving up this job.”

“I know.”

“If you know, what are you standing there for? Go.”

---

On the bridge over the river, now the checkpoint was gone, you had to wear a mask to cross, and at the end of the bridge stood an old man in a red armband who shouted at anyone without one. Su Tang rode her old bike with the flower rolls and the cabbage in the basket and a case of milk tied to the back with string, her mask on tight, and as she crossed the bridge the wind got in round the edges of the mask and made her eyes stream.

There were a few more people on Back Street than last week. The copy shop was open; the braised-meat stall wasn’t out yet. The internet café’s curtain was hooked up, and two boys stood at the door in masks smoking, masks pulled down under their chins: a drag, mask up, another drag, mask down.

On the cat café door was a sheet of A4 in Ye Zhixia’s writing, very large:

**Dine-in suspended. Cats are well. Don’t worry.**

Underneath was a drawing of a cat, recognizably Rice Cake, because it was drawn very round.

Su Tang went up, knocked, and the door opened. The moment she was inside she took off her mask, and the smell of cat fur and cat litter and the dry heat baked out by the electric heater all came rushing at her.

Ye Zhixing was sitting behind the bar counter, facing his computer.

He was wearing a grey hoodie, the same one he had on when she came the day before yesterday, a tuft of cat fur stuck to the cuff, which had been stuck there the day before yesterday too. On the counter were three instant-noodle tubs stacked one inside another, the fork still stuck in the top one. He had headphones on, in a meeting; when he heard the door he raised one hand and waved at her, then put it down again, his eyes not leaving the screen.

“…Right, the button on the second version goes bottom right, not bottom left, there aren’t many left-handers… No, I’m not discriminating against left-handers…”

Su Tang put the things on the shoe cabinet by the door, and crouched to untie the string round the case of milk from the back of the bike.

At the start of the school year, she had seen Ye Zhixing. Not at the cat café, but on the No. 8 High sports field, back in the make-up military training. She was in Class Three then, he was in Class Seven, two companies apart. She remembered him standing at attention all crooked, the instructor yelling at him and him not caring, one earbud in his ear and the other hanging on his chest, swinging. He was given ten laps as punishment, and when he’d finished he wasn’t even out of breath; he sat down on the ground, unscrewed a bottle of water, tipped his head back and gulped it, and when he’d gulped it he was still laughing and joking with the people next to him.

Back then she had thought: so this is the kind of person people talk about. Rich family, nothing to be afraid of.

She carried the milk in and put it beside the bar counter. Ye Zhixing was still in his meeting; halfway through a sentence he stopped, gave a very long yawn, and when he’d finished yawning carried on.

## 2. Chen Xi’s Classes

Chen Xi had made a fair bit of money.

He didn’t say exactly how much, but one day he transferred his mum two thousand, and she stood in the kitchen stunned for ages, then came out and asked whether he had stolen it or robbed someone. He said he had earned it teaching. She didn’t believe him, so he showed her a screenshot of the QQ group class, with over a hundred and thirty people in the group. His mum turned his phone over and over in her hands, and in the end transferred a thousand of the two thousand back.

“A thousand’s enough,” she said. “Keep the rest yourself. Don’t waste it.”

He didn’t listen.

With the rest of the money he bought a new pair of trainers, bought three game season passes, and the moment the internet café opened, topped up five hundred on a membership. He also treated the internet café gang to barbecue twice; the barbecue stall had only come out at the end of March, and the owner wore his mask hooked over one ear and coughed as he grilled, coughed so much that Huang Yingjun shifted his stool back half a metre.

“Xi the Great’s treat,” said Abiao, holding up a skewer of kidney.

“Say that again and I’ll kick you.”

“You kick people from the group, not from the barbecue stall.”

Chen Xi thought about it, decided that was fair, and said nothing more, and picked up a chicken wing.

“Forget logarithms for now,” he said. “Let me ask you: what is addition?”

## 3. The Basement

It was Su Tang’s first time at the place where Gu Xuerong lived.

She knew Xuerong had a “place,” Lin Xiaoman knew, everyone knew, but nobody had ever been. The trip to the urban village at New Year had been to the Gu family’s. This place was Gu Xuerong’s own.

Gu Xuerong had sent the location herself. The night before, in MC, Su Tang had said hasn’t your place gone a long time without a tidy, and Gu Xuerong said “don’t know,” and Su Tang said then I’ll come and help you tidy tomorrow, and after a long gap Gu Xuerong typed two words in the chat bar: “if you like.”

Then she sent a location.

Two turns in from Back Street, the side of an old building. Su Tang stood there looking at the seven steps going down; at the bottom was an iron door, most of its paint gone, rusted out in reddish-brown patches. Beside the door was a little window with its sill level with the pavement; she looked down at it, and the glass was coated in a layer of dust, so she couldn’t see inside.

She had her mask on and was carrying a big plastic bag with bin bags, rubber gloves, cloths, a bottle of washing-up liquid, a bottle of 84 disinfectant, and a bag of tangerines her mum had pushed on her.

She went down the steps and knocked on the door.

It was a long time before the door opened. Gu Xuerong stood inside, in that grey thermal set—the one Lin Xiaoman gave her—with a very old sweater over it, her hair not tied, a tangled mess round her shoulders.

“…You came.”

“I came!” said Su Tang, her voice a bit muffled through the mask.

She went in and took off her mask.

Then she stood in the doorway, stunned.

---

It was very dark inside, only a little grey light from the small window. Gu Xuerong pressed the switch on the wall; the fluorescent tube on the ceiling flickered twice, came on, and hummed.

The room wasn’t big, ten-odd square metres. A single bed against the wall, the quilt on it bundled into a heap. A table, with a computer on it, and beside the computer an electric pot with half a pot of noodle soup of unknown age in it, a layer of white fat set on top. In the corner was something covered with a cloth, square, the cloth dark, so you couldn’t tell what was underneath. Against the wall stood the little fridge, and stuck on the fridge door was a sticky note in handwriting Su Tang recognized, Ye Zhixing’s: Don’t unplug.

And then there were the bottles.

Bottles all over the floor. Coke, Sprite, Fanta, iced tea, orange juice, bottle after bottle, empty, lying every which way on the floor, leaning in the corners, piled at the foot of the bed, under the table too, some with the caps screwed on, some not, some with a bit left in the bottom gone a strange colour. Instant-noodle tubs and packets were mixed in among the bottles, some of the seasoning sachets torn open, some not.

“Xuerong…” said Su Tang, “these… you drank all these?”

“Mm.”

“Over how long?”

“New Year till now.”

Su Tang looked down at a Coke bottle by her foot. Two-litre, empty, the body crushed halfway and sprung back a little.

“How many bottles a day do you drink?”

“Don’t know,” said Gu Xuerong. “When I’m thirsty I drink.”

“Do you drink water?”

“This is water.”

Su Tang opened her mouth, and nothing came out.

She put the plastic bag on the floor, took out a pair of rubber gloves, put them on, took out a big black bin bag, and shook it open.

“Let’s go!”

---

Halfway through the tidying, Su Tang found a plastic bag under the bed.

The bag was tied shut, tied very tight; she undid it and looked inside: a duck carcass, every scrap of meat long gone from the bones, dried and yellowed, and beside it a few pear cores, shrivelled into little blackish-brown lumps.

“What’s this?”

Gu Xuerong was pulling bottles out from under the table, heard, and poked her head out to look.

“New Year’s Eve dinner.”

“…What?”

“What I ate at New Year,” said Gu Xuerong. “Instant noodles, roast duck, pears.”

“Where did the roast duck come from?”

“The braised-meat stall on Back Street. Bought it the day before the lockdown. It was his last day out, he was selling cheap, twenty for a whole one.”

“Just you, on your own?”

“Mm.”

“Then you…” Su Tang crouched by the bed, holding the plastic bag, “pears at New Year? Pear, li, sounds like parting, you’re not supposed to eat pears at New Year, sharing a pear means splitting up—”

“They were cheap,” said Gu Xuerong. “One-fifty a jin.”

For a moment Su Tang didn’t know what to say.

Gu Xuerong reached an iced-tea bottle out from under the table and threw it into the bin bag, where it clanged against the other bottles.

“I haven’t got anyone to part from,” she said.

Su Tang tied the plastic bag up again, tied it very tight, and threw it into the bin bag.

---

The bottles filled four big bags. The noodle tubs filled one. Su Tang poured the half pot of noodle soup down the toilet and flushed it, scrubbed the electric pot three times, until the black scorched mark on the bottom had at last faded a little. She swept, mopped, diluted the 84 with water and wiped down the corners and under the bed.

The thing under the cloth, Su Tang didn’t touch. When she got there with her cloth, she went round it.

Gu Xuerong was tidying too, but very slowly. Bend once, pick up a bottle, straighten up, rest a while, bend again. Su Tang watched her and said nothing, only went a little faster herself.

When they had finished it was nearly noon. The two of them sat on the edge of the bed; the sheet had been changed, for the one Su Tang had brought, an old sheet her mum had dug out, blue-and-white check, washed a little pale.

Su Tang peeled a tangerine and gave half to Gu Xuerong.

Gu Xuerong took it and held it, and didn’t eat.

“Xuerong.”

“Mm.”

“Before…” said Su Tang, “before, when you watched all that scary stuff, those games, those films. The first time I watched I threw up, remember? I’ve always wanted to ask you, how come you’re not scared?”

Gu Xuerong looked down at the half tangerine in her hand.

“When I was in middle school,” she said, “she showed me films.”

“Who?”

“Auntie.”

“…She showed you horror films?”

“Not horror films,” said Gu Xuerong. “The kind… about hell.”

She spoke very slowly, a sentence at a time, as if thinking how to put it.

“There’s a kind of painting called the transformations of hell. It shows people after they die, going down to hell, the Ten Kings, judged by one court after another. Mountains of knives, pots of boiling oil, tongues pulled out, sawn in half. Painted in a lot of detail. She bought a big one at a temple fair and stuck it on the kitchen wall. Right across from my bed.

“And discs. From the temple fair too, ten yuan for three. About karma, about what you owed someone in a past life, you have to pay back in this one. One was about karmic creditors. It said there are four kinds of children: the ones come to repay kindness, the ones come for revenge, the ones come to collect a debt, the ones come to pay a debt. The debt-collecting kind are born just to take money; in a past life you owed them, so in this life they’re born into your family and take your family’s money cent by cent, and when they’ve taken it all, they leave.”

Su Tang’s hand stopped in mid-air.

“She made you watch that?”

“Mm,” said Gu Xuerong. “She said I’m the debt-collecting kind.”

“…”

“She said, look, ever since you came, this family’s had bad luck. Your dad’s business lost money, he can’t pay back what he borrowed, that’s all you collecting.”

Su Tang put down her half tangerine.

“But… but that was your dad himself—”

“I know,” said Gu Xuerong. “He bought the enzyme drink himself.”

She broke a segment off her half tangerine, put it in her mouth, chewed, swallowed.

“That painting stayed up there,” she said. “Every night when I went to sleep, the moment I opened my eyes there it was. I looked at it for three years. After that, nothing scared me. Those things in the games, next to that painting, they’re all quite good-looking.”

Su Tang said nothing. She looked at Gu Xuerong’s profile, at the way she chewed the tangerine, very slowly, one bite after another, as if chewing something perfectly ordinary.

“She believes in another thing too,” said Gu Xuerong. “It’s called Three Redemptions Christ.”

“…What?”

“Three Redemptions Christ,” said Gu Xuerong. “Some people call it the Disciples’ Association. A few women from the village meet at one of their houses every week, door shut, chanting, singing. They say when you’re ill you don’t need medicine, pray and you’ll get better. They say in a few years there’ll be a great flood, believers will be saved and everyone else will drown.”

“Isn’t that… isn’t that a cult?”

“Mm.”

“One’s Buddhist and one’s Christian,” said Su Tang. “How can she… believe both?”

“She believes all of them,” said Gu Xuerong. “Whichever one says it’ll help her have a son, she believes that one.”

Su Tang’s mouth hung open.

“That time I had a fever,” said Gu Xuerong, “in the second year of middle school, over thirty-nine. She wouldn’t let me take medicine, she made me kneel in the kitchen and listen to her chant. She chanted all night.”

“…And then?”

“Then it went down,” said Gu Xuerong. “She said the prayers worked.”

She ate another segment of tangerine.

“And now she’s pregnant,” said Su Tang, her voice shaking a little, “if she won’t go to hospital—”

“She will,” said Gu Xuerong. “It’s a son in there.”

---

Su Tang sat on the edge of that bed for a long time.

She thought of her mum. Her mum believed in things a bit too: burned incense at New Year, went to the temple on the first and fifteenth to kowtow. When she was little and had a fever, her mum went to the temple for a charm too and stuck it at the head of her bed. But after she got the charm, her mum still carried her to the county hospital, went to A&E, sat up all night in the corridor, and the next day her eyes were swollen like two peaches.

Her mum had never said she’d come to collect a debt.

“Xuerong,” she said, “um…”

“Su Tang.”

“Hm?”

“Why do you bother with me?”

Su Tang was taken aback.

Gu Xuerong put the remaining few segments of tangerine on the sheet in a row, one segment against the next.

“Just leave me like this,” she said. “Look, I’m quite fine like this. I’ve got food, I’ve got drink, I’ve got a computer, I’ve got somewhere to sleep.”

“This is quite fine?”

“Mm.”

“Bottles all over the floor is quite fine?”

“You threw all the bottles away.”

Su Tang was stumped.

“I know what you want to say,” said Gu Xuerong. “You want to say, be a bit happier.”

She moved the row of tangerine segments, lining them up straighter.

“But being happy seems like a really hard thing,” she said. “I don’t know how to do it. I look at you lot, you, Xiaoman, Chen Xi, it’s like you were born knowing how. You laugh, you cry, you argue, you’re happy. I watch, and I feel…”

She thought for a moment, looking for the word.

“Envious,” she said. “I envy you. Maybe people are just born different.”

“That’s not—”

“Maybe,” said Gu Xuerong. “Maybe my whole life I’m just a heap of mud.”

Su Tang’s nose stung all at once.

“Don’t say that…”

“I’m not sad,” said Gu Xuerong. “I mean it. I don’t know what scared is, and I don’t know what hurting is. It’s like… I can’t feel those things. The feelings other people talk about, that love, I can’t feel it either. I don’t know what they’re like.”

She turned her head and looked at Su Tang.

“So you don’t need to bother with me,” she said. “If you do, I won’t feel it anyway.”

Su Tang’s eyes were already red, tears going round in them, round twice, not falling. She sniffed, grabbed the row of tangerine segments in one hand, and pushed them back into Gu Xuerong’s hand.

“Eat them,” she said.

“…”

“Eat them!”

Gu Xuerong looked down at the tangerine in her hand and ate a segment.

“Xuerong, listen to me,” said Su Tang. Her voice was shaking, but she didn’t let it shake too much. “I don’t know what you mean by not being able to feel. I’m stupid, I don’t understand those things. But I know one thing: however hard it gets, you have to keep living.”

Gu Xuerong chewed her tangerine and made no sound.

“Between people,” said Su Tang, “whatever happens, you have to help each other. You help me, I help you. If you can’t help me now, that’s fine, you help later. If you can’t help later either, that’s fine, I still help you. That’s just what I think. Whatever the problem, it can be solved in the end. I believe that.”

“…You believing it,” said Gu Xuerong, “doesn’t mean it can be solved.”

“Then I’ll keep believing it, until it’s solved!”

Gu Xuerong didn’t answer.

Su Tang sniffed, wiped her eyes with her sleeve, and then—she didn’t know how herself—split into a grin. A very ugly grin, the tears still hanging there, her nose bright red.

“Smile a bit,” she said. “You smile a bit too.”

“…”

“Stop watching all that scary stuff, OK? That stupid painting’s not across from your bed any more. What’s across from your bed now?”

Gu Xuerong turned her head and looked across from the bed. Across from the bed was the little window, its sill level with the pavement, and outside it a pair of feet went by in pink trainers, flap, flap.

“…The window.”

“Yes! The window!” said Su Tang. “People walk past outside! There’s sun! That painting’s gone! Just feel it a bit, there are good things in this world, there’s love, there are people who are good to you—”

“You,” said Gu Xuerong.

Su Tang stopped dead.

“…Huh?”

“People who are good to me,” said Gu Xuerong. “You said. That’s you.”

Su Tang’s tears finally fell, two big ones at once, splashing onto the blue-and-white checked sheet.

“Yes,” she said, “it’s me. And Xiaoman, and Chen Xi, and… and loads of people.” She wiped her eyes again. “Whatever happens, I’m here. I said so. Remember it.”

Gu Xuerong looked at her.

At her red nose, at the tears hanging on her lashes, at that ugly wide grin.

“When you cry,” said Gu Xuerong, “your nose runs.”

“…Xuerong!!”

## 4. A Body Full of Cats

That afternoon, Su Tang carried the four big bags of bottles and threw them one by one into the rubbish point at the end of Back Street. Crouched beside the rubbish point was an old man who collected scrap, in a grey cloth mask, and when he saw her throwing bottles away he jumped up and said don’t throw them, don’t throw them, give them to me.

So Su Tang gave him all the bottles.

The old man stamped them flat one by one, crunch, crunch, and when he’d stamped them he put them into his own woven sack. Four big bags took him nearly twenty minutes. When he was done, he took two yuan out of his pocket and tried to give it to her.

“No, no—”

“Take it!” said the old man. “That many bottles, they’re worth something!”

Su Tang stood at the rubbish point holding the two yuan, not knowing what to do. In the end she put the two yuan in her pocket, thinking next time she’d buy Xuerong a bottle of water. Real water.

---

When she got to the cat café, it was nearly six.

Ye Zhixia opened the door. She was wearing the beige apron, covered in cat fur, holding a lint roller, and when she saw Su Tang her eyes lit up, then dimmed.

“Sister Su Tang.” She lowered her voice. “You’ve come at just the right time.”

“What is it?”

Ye Zhixia jerked her chin toward the bar counter.

Ye Zhixing was still sitting there. Same grey hoodie, same position, facing his computer, the light of the screen on his face, which was a bluish white. The noodle tubs on the counter had gone from three to four.

“He hasn’t slept,” said Ye Zhixia, “since yesterday morning.”

“…What?”

“He got up yesterday morning and started on it, worked till the middle of the night, when I went to sleep he was working, when I got up he was still working,” said Ye Zhixia. “At noon I made him a bowl of noodles, he ate two mouthfuls and left it, it’s all mush now. I tell him to sleep and he says ‘mm, in a minute,’ he’s said it eight times.”

Su Tang went over.

Close up, she could see his eyes were full of red veins, two dark bluish-black marks under them. The screen had several windows open: one of code, one a spreadsheet, one a chat app with a dozen conversations flashing, and one the Town Matters back end, the number in its top right corner red.

“Ye Zhixing.”

“Mm.” He didn’t look up. “Su Tang, you’re here. I ate the spicy cabbage, it’s good. Thank your mum for me.”

“That was this morning.”

“Hm?”

“The spicy cabbage was this morning. It’s six in the evening now.”

Ye Zhixing raised his head and glanced out of the window. Outside it was already dark, the tiles on the roof opposite black.

“…Oh.”

“How long since you slept?”

“Not long.”

“Zhixia says you haven’t slept since yesterday morning.”

Ye Zhixing glanced over at the sofa. Ye Zhixia, holding Rice Cake, stood by the sofa with a so-I-told-on-you-what-of-it face.

“That hotpot place,” said Ye Zhixing, “they’ve changed what they want again, they want to see the first version next Monday. Big Liu’s putting new levels into the mini-game, and Huang Yingjun’s stuff needs me to look at it. Town Matters has had a jump in posts these last two days, lots of places are reopening, they all want to post jobs, and the review side—”

“Sister Xiaowen’s reviewing.”

“Xiaowen can’t keep up on her own—”

“I was reviewing this afternoon too,” said Su Tang. “At Xuerong’s, on my phone. I did over forty.”

Ye Zhixing stopped short.

“…Weren’t you going to help her tidy her place?”

“Finished,” said Su Tang. “When I finished I reviewed. I’m fast.”

Ye Zhixing opened his mouth as if to say something, and didn’t. He turned back to the screen and put his hands on the keyboard, but didn’t type.

“Ye Zhixing,” said Su Tang, “are you really not tired?”

“I am.”

“Then sleep.”

“Can’t sleep.”

“You haven’t even lain down, how do you know you can’t sleep?”

Ye Zhixing ignored her, swiped a finger on the touchpad, and dragged one window behind another.

“Ye Zhixing.”

“…”

“I’m talking to you.”

“You don’t understand at all.”

When he said it, his voice wasn’t loud, and he didn’t look at her.

Su Tang stood there, taken aback.

“It’s that I don’t dare,” said Ye Zhixing. “I don’t dare let go of that breath. The moment I let go, something falls. The company falls, Town Matters falls, Big Liu’s family, Xiaowen, the two street promoters, you, Xuerong, Huang Yingjun, I brought all of them in. The moment I let go, they—”

He stopped.

“I don’t understand,” said Su Tang, “but you never told me either.”

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer.

“You don’t say anything,” said Su Tang. “You don’t talk about your family, you don’t talk about three years ago, you don’t say you’re tired, you don’t say when you’re hurting. You just keep going and keep going, getting everything right, thinking of everything. You think people can’t see?”

“So what if they see,” said Ye Zhixing. “Real life isn’t solved by people seeing it.”

He took his hands off the keyboard, leaned back in his chair, tipped his head back, and looked at the ceiling. The pendant lamp on the ceiling had a rattan shade Uncle He had bought when the cat café opened, now covered in a layer of cat fur.

“I live like a machine,” he said, “think of everything, look after everyone, work out every step.” He pulled up one corner of his mouth. “Not because I’m good at it. Because I don’t want to get it wrong again.”

“Again?”

“…”

“You got it wrong before?”

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer.

He kept his head tipped back, staring at that fur-covered lamp, until Su Tang thought he had fallen asleep with his eyes open. Su Tang stood beside him and didn’t hurry him. Ye Zhixia, holding Rice Cake, stood by the sofa, and didn’t make a sound either.

“I don’t know how to say it,” he said at last. “And if I said it you’d…” He shook his head. “Forget it. A life is long. And pretty dull. You walk alone, you work alone. That’s it.”

When he’d said it, he seemed to think himself that it was too gloomy, sat up, and reached for the mouse again.

Su Tang slammed his laptop shut.

Smack.

Ye Zhixing looked at the closed laptop, then at her.

“…I hadn’t saved.”

“It autosaves.”

“How do you know?”

“I don’t,” said Su Tang. “But you’ve been at it a day and a night, the bit you didn’t save, do it again tomorrow.”

She grabbed his arm and pulled him up out of the chair. He was a head taller than her, but pulled like that he actually came up, swayed, and steadied himself on the bar counter.

“See,” said Su Tang, “you can’t even stand.”

“My legs have gone to sleep.”

“Then lie down and let them sleep.”

She dragged him over to the grey sofa and gave him a push. He sat down, and once he was down his whole body tipped back against the sofa, his eyes half open.

“Just lying down for a bit,” he said. “Twenty minutes. You wake me.”

“Yes, yes, twenty minutes.”

“Really wake me.”

“Really, really.”

He put his legs up on the sofa and lay down, banging his head on the armrest as he went down, thunk; he hissed, didn’t open his eyes, and just used the armrest as a pillow.

In less than a minute he was asleep.

Then the cats came.

Rice Cake first. It wriggled free of Ye Zhixia’s arms, jumped onto the sofa, trod a few circles on Ye Zhixing’s stomach, so that he grunted in his sleep, and then settled down, curled into a ginger-and-white ball, weighing on the pit of his stomach.

Then Calico, down from the back of the sofa, burrowing into the gap between his arm and his body, squeezing until the gap was a bit bigger, and lying in it.

Then the Devon. The Devon had always felt the cold, and in winter went looking for the warmest spot. It sniffed at the side of Ye Zhixing’s neck, smelled something, its big ears twitched, and then it curled up in the hollow of his neck, its head resting on his collarbone.

Blue-and-White jumped up and lay on his shins. The American Shorthair lay on his feet. The British Blue turned three circles on his knees, couldn’t find a spot, and in the end lay down on top of Blue-and-White, who didn’t object.

Your Highness came last. It walked out through the sliding door to the back room—which it had opened itself—came to the sofa, looked up, looked at the pile of cats, looked at the person underneath, and then jumped up onto the back of the sofa and lay down in the spot above Ye Zhixing’s head, its long tail hanging down so it rested exactly across his forehead.

Ye Zhixing slept deeply, not moving at all.

“…He’s warm,” Ye Zhixia whispered. “They always go for warm places.”

“How many are there?”

Ye Zhixia counted. “Seven,” she said. “Briquette won’t come, Briquette only cuddles Sister Xuerong. And the three strays, they’re timid, they don’t dare come over.”

Su Tang crouched by the sofa, looking at the person buried under seven cats. His face was showing, half hidden by the Devon’s big ears, Your Highness’s tail across his forehead. The two bluish-black marks under his eyes showed even more clearly in the electric heater’s orange-red glow.

“Sister Su Tang,” said Ye Zhixia, “it’s been twenty minutes, are you going to wake him?”

“No.”

“You promised him.”

“I promised to wake him,” said Su Tang. “I didn’t say when.”

Ye Zhixia thought about it, and decided the logic was sound.
