# Chapter Eleven　The Sand Table

## 1. Midnight

Lately Ye Zhixing had often been waking in the middle of the night.

Not from a nightmare, and not because some noise woke him. He was simply awake: his eyes opened, and he was as clear-headed as in daytime, and there was no getting back to sleep. He glanced at his phone, a little after two, or just past three, the light of the screen stinging his eyes, then turned the phone face down on the bedside table, lay there and looked at the ceiling.

There was nothing on the ceiling. White, flat, with the heat from the radiator floating against it, invisible, but he could feel it, a layer of dry, faintly hot air pressing on his face.

After lying there a while he would get up, walk barefoot to the planted aquarium and sit down on the floor in front of it.

The tank lights had long since gone off, leaving only the blue night light in one corner. In the dark the plants were black, clump after clump, like a shrunken forest. The neon tetras were asleep, hanging motionless in the gaps between the plants, the fluorescent blue line along their sides still faintly glowing in the dark, like a row of tiny indicator lights someone had forgotten to switch off. The CO₂ bubble counter had stopped too, on its timer. The whole tank was as still as a photograph.

He just sat there and watched.

Watched what, he couldn’t really have said himself. The piece of dragon stone, the small patch of moss growing at its foot, a fish that gave its tail one small flick in its sleep and then was still again.

He had built this tank in his second year of middle school. It was the first winter after everything was over, he couldn’t sleep night after night, so he went online and looked up how to keep fish, how to grow aquatic plants, how to adjust the water’s hardness, its pH, the concentration of CO₂. It took him three weeks to build it, and half a year more before it was stable. A batch of plants died, a batch of fish died, the algae bloomed twice, green as a tank of pea soup. He adjusted it bit by bit, looked for the cause bit by bit, and in the end it came alive.

Once it was alive, it stayed like this. Two years now: every day he changed a little of its water, trimmed a little of its plants, fed it a little, and it just went on living like this, no more, no less.

He liked it because it was a closed thing. All the numbers added up. How much light, how much CO₂, how much the fish excreted, how much the plants absorbed, what came in and what went out, in the end it balanced. If even one number was off, something would go wrong; and as long as you were willing to look, you could find which number was off.

The world outside wasn’t like that.

In the world outside, a lot of numbers didn’t add up. And some of the numbers that didn’t, you could see perfectly well, and there was still nothing you could do to touch them.

---

He had started saying strange things in the daytime, too.

Once at the shop, Chen Xi was explaining a physics problem to Gu Xuerong, and got to conservation of energy. Ye Zhixing was behind the counter going over the accounts, and without looking up, he cut in:

“Money isn’t conserved.”

Chen Xi stopped for a second. “…What?”

“Energy is conserved, money isn’t,” said Ye Zhixing. “Money can be made out of nothing, and it can vanish into nothing. A bank lends you a million, and that million didn’t exist before; it only appears the second you sign. If one day you can’t pay it back, it’s gone again in a second. But that million that’s gone, somebody has already used it to put up buildings, buy cement, pay wages, and those things are real.”

He turned a page of the ledger.

“So when the money disappears, what disappears isn’t the money. It’s the things the people who got the money thought they owned.”

Chen Xi looked at him for a good while, then turned his head and said to Gu Xuerong, “…He didn’t sleep well today.”

Gu Xuerong looked at Ye Zhixing.

“He hasn’t slept well for days,” she said.

Ye Zhixing looked up at her. She had already turned back to her problem, her pencil drawing a block on the scrap paper, and under the block an inclined plane, drawn very carefully.

---

Another time, Su Tang was brushing the Ragdoll, and partway through she sighed and said her mum wanted her to apply to a teachers’ college later, teachers’ college was good, stable, after graduating she could come back to the county and be a teacher and never have to worry for the rest of her life.

“Do you want to be a teacher?” Ye Zhixing asked.

“Don’t know,” said Su Tang. “Never thought about it. If my mum says it’s good, then it’s good, I guess.”

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer. After a while, he said:

“This thing called stability, it’s only stable because nobody moves.”

Su Tang stopped the brush and looked up at him.

“A table with four legs, whether it’s steady or not doesn’t depend on whether the legs are strong, it depends on whether the ground is level,” he said. “If the ground tilts, it doesn’t matter how strong the legs are.”

“…What do you mean?”

Ye Zhixing opened his mouth, as if he wanted to explain, and in the end shook his head.

“Nothing,” he said. “Keep brushing. Your Highness is about to start swearing again.”

Sure enough, the Ragdoll let out a curse. Su Tang lowered her head and went on brushing, brushed a few strokes, then looked up again and glanced at him.

That glance stayed a little long.

## 2. The Recommendation

The Ye family had made their start in building materials.

More than twenty years ago, Ye Shilong opened a shopfront by the highway on the north side of the county town, selling cement, selling rebar, selling tiles, with a second-hand Dongfeng truck parked out front that he drove himself to make deliveries. Back then the tallest buildings in the county town were only six storeys, and his cement went up them one sack on someone’s shoulder at a time, until it had carried up the county town’s first eight-storey building, its first housing estate with lifts, its first pedestrian street.

Later he no longer just sold materials. He started acquiring land himself and putting up buildings himself.

These past few years had been the best years. The year before last the county had been upgraded to a city, land prices doubled every year, and the flats in a building sold out before it was even topped off. The Ye family had a development each in the east and the south of town, and had acquired another plot in the west, where piles were being driven now. The family’s building-materials company had grown from one shopfront into a whole street of warehouses, from one Dongfeng truck into a fleet.

It was on a Sunday afternoon that Ye Zhixing put the thing on his father’s desk.

He had made it himself. A dozen-odd pages, printed out, held together with a black binder clip. In it were the county’s population figures for the past five years, the resident population smaller every year; the sale prices and sell-through times of the county town’s main developments, the sell-through times longer every year; the debts on the Ye family’s projects, from accounts he had wheedled and pestered out of Uncle He and worked through item by item; and a few pages he had clipped from the news, where the phrase “houses are for living in, not for speculation” had been circled three times in red pen.

The last page had only one sentence:

> Recommendation: Stop acquiring new land. Slow down the west-side project. Gradually reduce debt.

Ye Shilong sat behind the desk and turned the pages one by one.

He was in his early fifties, his hair combed back very neatly, already grey at the temples. He wore a dark cashmere cardigan and an old-style gold watch on his wrist, the band a little loose, so that it slid back and forth on his wrist as he turned the pages. He turned them slowly, read every page, and where there were numbers he would pause, and tap the number with one finger.

Ye Zhixing stood on the other side of the desk and waited.

The study was large. Three walls were bookshelves, and most of the books on them came in sets, hardbacks with gilt spines that hardly anyone had ever opened. The other wall was a big window, and outside it was the osmanthus tree in the courtyard, its leaves all fallen, a layer of snow lying along its branches, and when the wind blew the snow off, it fell with a soft rustle.

Ye Shilong turned the last page, closed the stack, clipped it back together with the binder clip and set it at the corner of the desk.

“Not bad work,” he said.

Ye Zhixing said nothing.

“The data is right,” said Ye Shilong. “People are leaving, flats aren’t moving, debt is rising. Everything you’ve said here, I already know.”

“Then why—”

“Because the things you’ve calculated,” Ye Shilong raised his head and looked at his son, “aren’t what matters most in this county town.”

His voice wasn’t loud, and it wasn’t hurried, and when he spoke there was a slight smile at the corners of his mouth, but the smile stopped at his mouth and never reached his eyes.

“Take this thing of yours to the city, to the province, show it to the banks, to the people who do research, and they’ll all say your figures are right. But here,” he tapped the desktop once with a finger, “in this place, whether buildings go up or not, whether land gets taken or not, doesn’t depend on data. It depends on people.”

“Which people?”

Ye Shilong didn’t answer. He stood up, walked over to the coat stand, took down the black overcoat hanging there and draped it over his arm.

“Don’t go to school tomorrow afternoon,” he said. “Come with me to meet someone.”

## 3. The Sand Table

At three the next afternoon, Ye Zhixing followed his father into the city government building.

It was an old building, put up in the eighties, five storeys, its outer walls faced with cream-coloured tiles, a few of which had already fallen off, showing the grey cement underneath. At the entrance were two pillars, and on the pillars hung signs, black characters on white, where the word “County” had been painted out and the word “City” written in beside it, its colour a little newer than the rest.

The mayor’s office was on the fourth floor.

The mayor was in his early forties, not tall, but full of energy, his hair cut very short, his face carrying the kind of keyed-up sheen that only people who are chronically short of sleep have. He wore a white shirt with the sleeves rolled to the forearm and no tie, and when he saw Ye Shilong come in he strode over, took Ye Shilong’s hand in both of his and shook it hard a few times.

“Old Ye, Old Ye, you’re here, you’re here.” He turned to look at Ye Zhixing, and his eyes lit up. “So this is your boy— I’ve heard about him, I’ve heard about him, top of his year, competition student, right? Good, good. The young will surpass us.”

He shook Ye Zhixing’s hand too. His hand was hot, dry, his grip very strong.

There were several other people in the office.

All middle-aged men, sitting on a row of sofas against the wall, in much the same dark jackets, some fat, some thin, and when they saw Ye Shilong come in they all stood, smiling and greeting him as “Mr. Ye”. Ye Zhixing recognized two of them, the bosses of two other small developers in the county, one named Liu, one named Ma, who had come to the house at New Year to pay their respects, carrying Moutai.

They were all smiling. Smiling very happily, as if they had just heard some good news.

In the middle of the office stood a very big table, and on the table was a sand table model.

---

The sand table was as big as a ping-pong table.

The whole county town was on it, shrunk thousands of times over, made out of plastic, foam and wood. The river on the east side, the two bridges over the river, the old street in the town centre, the schools, the hospital, the railway station, building after building, tree after tree, all made in fine detail. Ye Zhixing found No. 8 High on it, found Back Street behind No. 8 High, and on Back Street the little two-storey building, as small as a sugar cube.

On the north side of the sand table, a large area was empty.

Not actually empty: it was covered with a clear acrylic dome. The area under the dome was different from everywhere else on the sand table: the buildings were new, white, lined up block after block, perfectly neat, in a great square formation; in the middle of the formation was a plaza, and in the plaza a fountain; north of the plaza was a very tall, very wide building, with a tiny red flag stuck on its roof.

Ye Zhixing knew what that area used to be.

The north of town, the railway freight yard and the old grain depot. A few rows of red-brick warehouses, a disused railway line, a patch of self-built single-storey houses, several hundred households living in them, most of them old people. And the building-materials market, where Gu Jianguo carried cement, was right on the edge of it.

“This is our new district,” said the mayor, standing at the edge of the sand table, his hand resting on the acrylic dome. “The North City Area.”

His voice suddenly rose, as if he were in a meeting, or giving a speech.

“This city of ours has been upgraded from a county for two years now. But look,” his hand swept a circle over the sand table, “where’s our city centre? Still in the old town. And what’s the old town like? Narrow roads, old buildings, nowhere even to park. A city without a decent city centre, how is it supposed to attract business? Attract investment? Keep young people here?”

His hand moved to the area under the acrylic dome.

“So the city has decided: the North City Area will be completely redeveloped. The old warehouses, the old single-storey houses, all of it comes down. New housing estates, new schools, new hospitals, a new commercial centre.” He paused and looked around the room. “The land in this area, Mr. Ye, Mr. Liu, Mr. Ma, and a few others, it’s already been acquired.”

The men on the sofas smiled even wider.

Ye Zhixing looked at the sand table, at that stretch of white, neat new buildings. He thought of his own report, a dozen-odd pages, of those population figures, smaller every year.

He spoke up.

“Mayor,” he said, “how many people live in that northern area right now?”

The mayor turned to look at him, the glow of the speech still in his eyes. “About… eight hundred-odd households. There’s a plan for demolition compensation, everyone will be properly resettled.”

“And the new housing estates, how many households are planned?”

“Three thousand in phase one, another two thousand in phase two.”

“Five thousand households,” said Ye Zhixing. “The county town— the city has been losing more than ten thousand residents a year these past few years. Half the flats in the developments in the east and south still haven’t sold. Five thousand households. Who’s going to live there?”

The office went quiet for a moment.

Someone on the sofas gave a dry cough. Ye Shilong stood on the other side of the sand table and said nothing, watching his son, his expression very calm, as if he had known all along that he would ask this.

The mayor looked at Ye Zhixing for a while, then suddenly laughed.

The kind of laugh that was very generous, very indulgent, like an adult listening to a child talk.

“Good question, young Ye,” he said. “We’ve considered that question too. You’re right, if you just put up buildings and nobody comes to live in them, that won’t do.”

He reached out and pointed under the acrylic dome, at the tallest, widest building, the one with the little red flag.

“That’s why this, here,” he said, “is the new civic centre.”

Ye Zhixing’s gaze stopped on that building.

“The city government, the city Party committee, the People’s Congress, the Political Consultative Conference, and the court, the procuratorate, the public security bureau, the tax bureau, the education bureau, the health bureau,” the mayor counted them off one by one, his finger tapping one by one on the ring of small buildings around the big one, “all of them move over. The government leads the way, the public institutions lead the way, several thousand civil servants and public-sector employees, along with their families, all move to the North City. The schools follow, the hospitals follow, business follows.”

He drew his hand back and rested it on the acrylic dome, very steadily.

“That’s where the people come from.”

---

Ye Zhixing said nothing more.

He looked at the building with the little red flag.

All at once he understood all of it.

This land wasn’t a development. It was a project. A project that had to be built. The city government moving over there was the biggest thing of the mayor’s term, something to be written into reports and shown to the people above. It couldn’t not be built, couldn’t be built slowly, couldn’t be built halfway and stopped.

So where would the money to build it come from?

From the people who had taken this land.

The demolition compensation, they would pay. The infrastructure, they would provide. The schools, the hospitals, the roads, the pipe networks, they would front the money. The new civic building, too, they might have to “build on the government’s behalf”, and once it was built, the government would “buy it back in instalments”— how many instalments, starting when, would be settled when the time came.

They would stake everything they had on it. And once it was staked, they could never pull it out again. Because once the buildings started going up, they couldn’t stop; if they stopped, everything put in before would be lost. They could only keep putting more in, until the day the buildings were finished, or until the day they couldn’t put in any more.

And the mayor, the mayor’s term had a number on it. Three years, five years. If the buildings were finished, he would be the man who “built the new district”, and he would move up. If they weren’t finished, he would still move up, or move somewhere else, and what would be left behind was a stretch of dug-up ground and a crowd of developers trapped inside it.

He took his eyes off the building and looked at the men on the sofas.

Mr. Liu was smiling. Mr. Ma was smiling. The other two, whose names he didn’t know, were smiling too, and one of them was saying something in a low voice to the man beside him, and when he got to the good part, slapped his thigh.

They were all far smaller developers than the Ye family. The Ye family had two developments, east and south; they had one each, and some of them hadn’t even sold out that one. In taking the land in the North City, they had staked their whole fortunes, their whole lives.

They were smiling because they thought they had bet right.

When the civic centre moved over, land prices would rise, house prices would rise, this land was gold.

Ye Zhixing looked at them, at the flushed, excited shine on their faces in the office the radiators had heated until it was scorching.

He opened his mouth.

In the end he said nothing.

---

When they came out of the city government building, it was almost dark.

It had started snowing again, very fine, slanting through the light under the streetlamps. Uncle Chen brought the car round to the foot of the steps, Ye Shilong got in first, and Ye Zhixing stood on the steps and looked back once at the old cream-coloured building. The window on the fourth floor was still lit, and on it were the shadows of several people, moving back and forth.

He ducked into the car.

The car went a fair way, and Ye Shilong said nothing the whole time, looking out of the window.

After a long while, he spoke.

“You understand now.”

It wasn’t a question.

Ye Zhixing looked at the hands on his knees.

“…Why take it?” he asked. “You know perfectly well.”

Ye Shilong brought his gaze back from the window and looked at the wipers sweeping across the windscreen in front of them, back and forth.

“If I don’t take it,” he said, “the pre-sale permits for the two developments in the east and south won’t get approved next month.”

The wipers swept across, and swept back.

“You think that’s a piece of land,” said Ye Shilong. “It’s an admission ticket.”

## 4. The Banquet

The dinner was three evenings later, at the best hotel in the county town, in a private room on the top floor.

Ye Zhixing hadn’t wanted to go. His father said go, just sit there, you don’t have to say anything. So he went.

The room was large, with a big round table in the middle that could seat twenty. In the middle of the table was a motorized lazy Susan, already covered with cold dishes, ring after ring, arranged like a flower. On the wall hung a landscape painting of the Welcoming Pine on Mount Huang, with four characters inscribed beneath the pine: “Guests feel at home.” The light was a crystal chandelier, very bright, so bright that there was no shadow on anyone’s face.

A lot of people came.

All the developers from the North City came. Mr. Liu, Mr. Ma, and the two whose names he didn’t know. The director of the cement plant came, the boss of the steel company, the manager of the concrete mixing plant, the window-and-door people, the lift people, the waterproofing people. The president of a bank came and sat beside the seat of honour, in gold-rimmed glasses, saying little and smiling the whole time. A deputy director from the demolition office came, and a section chief from the housing and construction bureau.

Ye Shilong sat in the seat of honour.

Ye Zhixing sat on his father’s left, in the third seat, next to the director of the cement plant, a very fat middle-aged man with a thick gold chain round his neck.

The liquor was Moutai. A waitress carried in a case, opened it and set the bottles one by one on the lazy Susan.

Then the toasting began.

---

Ye Zhixing sat there and watched.

He watched his father stand, glass in hand, and toast them one by one, starting from the seat of honour. The bank president, the deputy director, the section chief, Mr. Liu and Mr. Ma, the director, the manager, the bosses. With each person, a few words, a clink of glasses, head back, glass drained. His father could hold his drink; seven or eight glasses in, his face wasn’t the least bit red, his voice hadn’t changed in the slightest, and that slight smile stayed at the corners of his mouth.

He watched the others stand one by one and toast his father back, toast other people, toast each other. The lazy Susan went round and round, and the dishes changed course after course. Lobster, abalone, sea cucumber, a steamed star-spotted grouper, its eyes white, bulging, staring at the ceiling.

He watched Mr. Liu get drunk, his face as red as a slab of pig’s liver, his arm round Mr. Ma’s shoulders, saying with a thick tongue, “Brother Ma, once the North City takes off, you and me, we go in together, take another plot, another plot…”

He watched the bank president, glass in hand, say something in a low voice to Ye Shilong, a sentence and then a smile, and Ye Shilong nodded and smiled too.

He watched the deputy director from the demolition office take out his phone, open a map, point at an area on it, and gesture to the section chief from the housing bureau. “This area, three months, within three months, all cleared. Work starts after Spring Festival.”

The section chief said, “There are a lot of old people in that area. Probably a fair few holdouts.”

The deputy director said, “There are ways.”

He watched the director of the cement plant, sitting beside him, pick up a big chopsticksful of sea cucumber and drop it into his bowl, then clap him on the shoulder and say, “Young Ye, your dad says your family’s building the new high school too. You’ll be studying in a school your own family built, how about that.”

Ye Zhixing looked down at the piece of sea cucumber in his bowl, black, soft, with a glistening layer of glaze on it.

“The new high school?”

“You didn’t know?” The director was surprised, and lowered his voice, his breath full of liquor in Ye Zhixing’s face. “Over in the North City, they’re building a new high school, a city key school, three times the size of No. 8 High. The government says your family builds it on their behalf. Once it’s built, No. 8 High’s senior section moves over there.”

“When will it be built?”

“Three years,” said the director, picking up a piece of abalone. “If all goes well, three years.”

He stuffed the abalone into his mouth, chewed twice, and added, indistinctly:

“…If it doesn’t go well, then it’s hard to say.”

---

Ye Zhixing sat there right to the very end.

He didn’t drink a drop, and he hardly ate anything. He looked at the faces, one after another, flushed and shining, listened to the “bottoms up”, “Mr. Ye can really hold his drink”, “once the North City takes off we all get rich together”, watched the dishes taken away half eaten from the lazy Susan, watched the waitresses go in and out topping up the liquor, looked at the Welcoming Pine on the wall.

He felt something was wrong.

Not that the data was wrong. He had worked the data out long ago. It was another kind of wrong, one he couldn’t put into words, like a number in the aquarium that didn’t add up: you know perfectly well it’s there, and you just can’t find where.

He looked at his father.

Ye Shilong was clinking glasses with the bank president. Then head back, glass drained, glass set down, a dab at the corners of his mouth with his napkin, and he turned his head and met his son’s eyes.

Father and son, one seat apart, looked at each other for a second.

Ye Shilong’s eyes were clear. Not the least bit drunk, clear as glass just washed.

He gave his son a very small nod.

Then he turned back and went on talking to the others.

## 5. Downstairs

That weekend, at the cat café, Ye Zhixing told them about it.

He didn’t start with it. At first he said nothing at all, sitting on the grey sofa under the big screen with a controller in his hands, and on the screen was that rain-soaked corridor from Nightmare Raid, his character standing in the middle of the corridor, not moving.

Gu Xuerong sat beside him, holding a controller too. Her character stood beside his character, not moving either.

The two characters just stood like that, in that corridor where the rain kept falling, for nearly ten minutes.

Su Tang finished brushing the Ragdoll, came over, looked at the screen, then at the two of them.

“What are you doing?”

“Waiting,” said Gu Xuerong.

“Waiting for what?”

Gu Xuerong didn’t answer, and turned her head to look at Ye Zhixing.

Ye Zhixing put the controller down.

---

He told it very slowly, in fits and starts. About his father, about the report, about the mayor, about the sand table, about the building with the red flag, about that dinner, about that piece of sea cucumber, about the North City that would be demolished in three months, about the new high school that wouldn’t be finished for three years.

He didn’t talk about the numbers, didn’t talk about debt, didn’t talk about sell-through times, didn’t say “an admission ticket”. He only told what he had seen and what he had heard.

Chen Xi had come too. He had come straight from the internet café, and halfway through he stopped playing on his phone and leaned against the arm of the sofa, listening.

Su Tang sat on the floor with her back against the sofa, hugging her knees.

Gu Xuerong kept looking at him the whole time.

When he finished, the room was very quiet. Calico yawned on the windowsill, and halfway through the yawn, seeing that nobody was moving, closed her mouth again. In the kitchen Old Zhou’s griddle sizzled, in bursts, sounding very far away.

“I don’t really understand,” Su Tang spoke first, very quietly. “Debt, civic centres… I don’t really understand.”

She hugged her knees, her chin resting on them, and thought for a moment.

“But where we live, across the river, the year before last they said it was getting demolished too,” she said. “They said they were building some kind of wetland park. They painted the red ‘demolish’ character on the walls, a circle with ‘demolish’ inside it, painted everywhere. There was one on our wall too. Back then my mum did sums every day, how much money we’d get if it was demolished, how many flats we could swap it for, said that way the money for me to go to university would be sorted.”

She paused.

“It still hasn’t been demolished. That ‘demolish’ has faded, it’s turned pink. When it rains, the colour runs down the wall, in streaks. My mum doesn’t do the sums any more.”

She looked down at her trainers.

“…I just think, things people say they’ll do, they don’t necessarily do. And things they do, they don’t necessarily finish.”

Ye Zhixing looked at her.

Su Tang didn’t look up, picking with a finger at a bit of rubber on the side of her trainer, picked at it a while, then stopped.

---

Chen Xi scratched the back of his head.

“I don’t get this stuff either,” he said. “Property or whatever, nobody in my family has touched it in eight generations.”

He thought for a while.

“But I’ve only got one question,” he said. “Who’s buying?”

Ye Zhixing looked at him.

“Our village,” said Chen Xi, “when I was little, more than three hundred households. Now there’s fewer than a hundred left, and the ones left are all old people and kids. The young ones have all gone, Guangdong, Zhejiang, Shanghai. They come back for a few days at New Year, and after New Year they leave again. When they leave, some of them lock up their houses, lock them for a year, two years, lock them until the houses fall down and still don’t come back.”

He shoved his phone into his pocket.

“The county town’s the same. Forty-seven people in our class, go and ask, how many want to stay in the county town later? Not a single one. Everyone wants out. Out through university, out through work, out one way or another.”

He paused, then grinned, a slightly dry grin.

“Me too. I’m not staying here. I’m leaving, as far as I can get. Beijing, Shanghai, abroad, anywhere. As long as it’s not here.”

He looked at Ye Zhixing.

“So when you say they’re building five thousand homes,” he said, “all I want to ask is, who’s buying? And who lives in them once they’re bought?”

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer.

He didn’t need to. He had asked the same question in front of that sand table. The mayor’s answer was: move the government over there, and the people will follow.

But those several thousand people the government would move had already been living in town. When they moved from town to the North City, town would have several thousand flats emptied out. The total didn’t change. There weren’t more people, they were just shifted from here to there.

A closed tank: scoop the water from the left side to the right, and there’s no more water than before.

---

Gu Xuerong had said nothing the whole time.

She sat beside Ye Zhixing, still holding her controller. Her character was still standing in that rain-soaked corridor, not moving, the rain on the screen falling and falling.

Ye Zhixing turned his head and looked at her.

She looked back at him. She looked for a long time before she spoke.

“Then will you be buried in it?”

Ye Zhixing froze.

Su Tang and Chen Xi froze too, both turning their heads to look at her.

Gu Xuerong’s face was as flat as ever, as if she were asking for the answer to a problem. She looked at Ye Zhixing, her eyes very dark, very still, not blinking once.

“Those buildings,” she said. “Half built, and then they can’t go on. The pits they dug underneath.” She paused. “Will you be in there?”

The room was very quiet.

Ye Zhixing looked at her, his mouth opened, and nothing came out.

He found he had no answer.

He wanted to say no, he had his own company, his money wasn’t in there, he wouldn’t be buried. But he thought of what his father had said in the car, “It’s an admission ticket.” Thought of that nod his father had given him at the dinner. Thought of the director saying, “Your family’s building the new high school too, you’ll be studying in a school your own family built.”

He thought of three years ago.

Back then, he had also thought he was standing outside it.

Only later did he learn that the person standing at the very front is the one furthest inside.

“…I don’t know,” he said.

Gu Xuerong looked at him for a while.

Then she turned back and took a proper grip on her controller again.

“Then don’t be in there,” she said.

Her character moved on the screen, took one step forward, stopped, and looked back at his character, as if waiting for him.

## 6. The Mini-Program

That night, he woke in the middle of the night again.

Two-forty.

He didn’t go and sit in front of the aquarium. He turned on his computer.

The screen lit up, its bluish-white light on his face. He logged in to a back end.

The home page of the back end was a chart, one line, climbing from the bottom left to the top right, not climbing fast, but climbing the whole way. Under the chart were a few numbers: active users yesterday, total registered users, new posts yesterday, this month’s revenue from pinned posts and ads.

That was his mini-program.

It was called “Town Matters”. He had picked the name offhand in his last year of middle school, a very plain, folksy name, and later couldn’t be bothered to change it. At first it was just a local noticeboard: whose dog was lost, who wanted to rent out a flat, who wanted to sell a second-hand e-bike, post it up and everyone could see. Later he slowly divided it into sections: jobs, second-hand, homes to rent and buy, shops for transfer, carpools, lost and found, tutoring, public notices.

He didn’t do anything else with it. No food delivery, no errand running, never touching money passing from one person’s hands to another’s. It was just a board, a board a bit bigger and a bit cleaner than the telegraph poles and the noticeboards.

Now, almost everyone in the county town who used a smartphone had this mini-program on their phone. Labour contractors looking for workers for building sites posted jobs on it, restaurant owners looking for dishwashers posted jobs on it, university graduates just back in the county town looking for work scrolled through it post by post. When someone was moving away, they put their fridge and washing machine up, and within half a day someone came to haul them off; when someone’s child had outgrown a little bicycle, they put it up for twenty yuan, and underneath came a row of comments asking if it was still available. The woman who ran the copy shop had once put up the shopfront for transfer, left it up for a week and then took it down again, saying that when she thought about it, she couldn’t bear to let it go. Sister Mei had found a tutor for her child on it. Old Zhou had tried to hire a kitchen hand on it, didn’t find one, and said young people these days don’t like going into kitchens.

The thing made money.

The money came from three places. One was pinning: a post you put up would get pushed down by every new post after it, and if you wanted it to stay at the top, you paid, by the day, a few yuan a day. Two was ads: on the home page and in the middle of every section there were a few business ads slotted in, renovation companies, driving schools, cram schools, wedding photography, and each one had a small grey box in the top right corner, and in the box, one word: “Ad”, very small, but always there. Three was that some posts themselves cost money: the sections for jobs, shop transfers and home sales charged a tiny bit per post, while ordinary people selling an old fridge or looking for a lost dog paid nothing.

Ads had to be labelled: that was the very first rule he had set, earlier even than the line written on the wall when he opened the shop. Once a company selling health supplements came to him, offering five times what anyone else paid, on condition that it not be labelled “Ad” and made to look like an ordinary post. He turned it down.

It wasn’t much, but it kept earning. Bit by bit, added up, it was a very thin stream of water that had never once run dry.

This was his.

Not his father’s, not the Ye family’s, not a script someone had handed him. It was written by him line by line of code, negotiated by him business by business, fixed by him bug by bug.

---

He looked at that number for a long time.

Then he opened another file.

It was a spreadsheet. It listed the Ye family’s businesses: the building-materials company, the logistics fleet, the two developments, the plot in the west, and the North City. After each row were some marks he had made.

He was looking.

Looking for the places that could be plugged in.

The two developments in the east and south still had several hundred flats unsold, and every month they ran ads in the papers, on the TV station, on the sides of buses— those ads could go on “Town Matters”, on the home page, long term, labelled “Ad”. The Ye family’s building-materials company had hundreds of renovation crews and small contractors downstream, hiring all year round— those job posts could all go through “Town Matters”, charged per post. The developments’ property-management notices, water and power cuts, the flats owners wanted to sublet or resell, could also be gathered in the “homes to rent and buy” section. And if that new district in the North City really did get built, and several thousand households moved in, the ones looking for work, selling old furniture, looking for tutors, would all be on this board.

All of it was legal. All of it was normal business cooperation. The Ye family’s companies would pay to buy ad space and posting services from his company, the services were real, the prices were fair, the ads were all labelled “Ad”, the accounts were clean.

But he knew what he was doing.

He was carrying things out of the house. Bit by bit, by legal means, drawing nourishment from the big tree that was the Ye family into his own little tree. While the big tree was still standing, still flourishing, before anyone knew its roots had begun to rot.

He thought of what Chen Xi had said: “I’m leaving, as far as I can get.”

He couldn’t leave.

He looked like the freest person in this county town. He skipped class and nobody did anything; he didn’t wear the uniform and nobody stopped him; he ran a cat café, he wrote programs, he did whatever he wanted. But he couldn’t leave. He was Ye Shilong’s son, and his name was tied to those two developments, to that land in the North City, to that building with the red flag on the sand table. That rope was thicker than any he could see.

All he could do was put a little more by for himself before the rope pulled tight.

---

Outside the window, it was still dark.

The blue night light in the corner of the aquarium was on. The fish in the tank were asleep, hanging in the gaps between the plants, the blue lines along their sides flickering.

He closed the spreadsheet, opened a new page in the back end and began writing a partnership proposal. The title was “Annual Advertising and Job-Posting Partnership Proposal for Ye’s Building Materials and the East and South Developments”.

He wrote very fast, line after line, like doing a problem whose answer he had known all along.

Halfway through, his phone buzzed.

It was Gu Xuerong.

> still up

It wasn’t a question.

He looked at the message for a while, and replied with one word:

> mm

After a while, from the other end:

> me too

> what are you doing

> writing something

> im mining

After it came a screenshot. Stardew Valley, the mines, level seventy-three. Her character stood among a patch of rocks glowing purple, her backpack stuffed full, her health bar down to a sliver.

> stop mining youre about to die

> mm

> go back to bed

> you too

He looked at those few words.

He put the phone down, looked at the half-written proposal on the screen, looked out of the window, looked at the aquarium.

He saved the proposal, left the computer on, and the screen dimmed by itself.

He went back to bed and lay down. After a while he sat up again, opened “Town Matters” on his phone, and, with no particular purpose, just scrolled down. Jobs, second-hand, rentals, a few pinned posts hanging at the top, on a yellow background, a renovation company’s ad tucked in the middle, the small grey box in its top right corner saying “Ad”.

When he scrolled to the “tutoring” section, he stopped.

A post put up at eight-forty that evening, not pinned, already pushed down to fifth place by the posts after it:

> Girl in first year of high school, can tutor primary school Chinese and maths, good with kids, patient. Free weekend daytimes. 15 yuan an hour, negotiable. Contact: Su (student).

Views: 3.

The phone number left at the bottom, he recognized. It was Su Tang’s.

He looked at that post for a long time. His finger rested on the screen, and right beside it was a “Pin” button; he was the admin of the back end, one tap and it wouldn’t cost a thing.

He didn’t tap it.

He put the phone back on the bedside table, screen down.

Outside the window, it had begun to snow.
