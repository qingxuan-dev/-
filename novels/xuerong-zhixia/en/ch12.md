# Chapter Twelve　Home Cooking

## 1. The Box

That Saturday afternoon, Ye Zhixing came up the stairs carrying a polystyrene box.

The box was big, the kind seafood comes in, white, a few chunks knocked off its four corners, wrapped round several times with wide tape. He carried it very carefully, both hands under the bottom, taking the stairs one at a time, much more slowly than usual. Something in the box was moving: click, click, like someone tapping on the polystyrene with a fingernail.

The copy-shop owner poked her head out of the glass door. “Young Ye, what have you got this time?”

“A crab.”

“A crab? In the middle of winter, where’d you get a crab?”

“Kept it at home.”

She craned her neck to try and see into the box, but the lid was on and she couldn’t see a thing, only hear click, click inside. She drew her head back in, muttered “rich people’s kids, always up to something,” and went back to her copying.

---

There weren’t many people at the shop that day. It was snowing, the roads were slippery, and only two groups came: one ate and left, and the other was three middle-school girls lying on the open floor by the window, playing with Calico.

Su Tang was in the back room blow-drying the Devon. Gu Xuerong sat on the grey sofa doing the accounts, the ledger open on her knees, her pen twirling between two fingers. Chen Xi was there too, just come from the lab, still in his down jacket, squatting under the cat tree, locked in a contest of wills with Rice Cake over a feather wand. Rice Cake sat on the second level of the cat tree, eyes narrowed, watching the feather swing back and forth without moving at all, as if watching a not especially bright human put on a show.

Ye Zhixing set the polystyrene box on the floor by the counter and lifted the lid.

A cold breath rose out of it, briny, with a hint of seawater.

Chen Xi was the first one over. Gu Xuerong looked up and closed the ledger. In the back room the hairdryer stopped, and Su Tang came out holding the Devon wrapped in a towel, leaning over to look into the box.

The box was lined with a layer of soaking-wet sponge, and on the sponge sat a crab.

A very big one.

So big it didn’t look like a crab; it looked like a blue-grey plate someone had left in a box. The shell was flat and wide, a good bit wider than a grown man’s palm, its edge lined with a row of serrations, each one pointed, like a blunt saw blade. The shell was a deep blue-green, green gone dark, nearly ink, and under the warm yellow lights of the shop it had a wet, oily sheen. Its walking legs were green too, covered in patches of a pale, net-like pattern.

Its two claws were drawn in front of its body, folded one over the other, like two clenched fists. The claws were thick, out of all proportion to its body, the two together wider than the shell. They were a different colour from the shell, a bluish green, like the glaze on old porcelain, smooth on the surface. The row of teeth on the inside of each claw was orange-red, one tooth after another, thick as pebbles, opening and closing: click, click.

Its two eyes stood up at the front edge of the shell like two short matchsticks, and turned round to face them.

“Holy crap,” said Chen Xi.

## 2. The Claws

“What kind of crab is that?” Chen Xi was squatting at the edge of the box, his eyes frighteningly bright. “That big?”

“A mud crab,” said Ye Zhixing. “From the sea. This one’s a bit over two kilos.”

“Two kilos?” Su Tang’s voice went shrill. “One crab, two kilos?”

“That’s about as big as they get,” said Ye Zhixing. “The biggest on record for this kind is only around three kilos. Over two, and they’ve pretty much stopped growing. People by the sea are used to them; here, inland, you don’t see them much.”

“You kept it at home?”

“Mm. A big storage tub, sea salt mixed in, plus an air pump,” said Ye Zhixing. “I brought it back from the coast last summer. It was small then, under a kilo.”

“So what does it eat?”

“Shellfish, mostly,” said Ye Zhixing. “Clams, whelks, oysters, anything with a shell is its favourite, one pinch of the claw and it’s open. Now and then a few prawns, a few bits of fish.”

“…It eats better than I do,” said Chen Xi.

“It won’t be eating for much longer,” said Ye Zhixing.

They all looked at him.

“Crabs have to moult to grow. Once they’re this big, every moult is a walk past death’s door, and the bigger they are, the harder it is. My storage tub, the water’s mixed up, and however much I adjust it, it isn’t the sea.” He looked at the crab lying motionless in the box. “At my place, it won’t hold out much longer. Rather than let it slowly stop moving in a tub, I thought I’d bring it over, let you have a look, have some fun.”

---

Su Tang put the Devon down on the sofa and came over too, squatting at the edge of the box, a little further back than Chen Xi. She tilted her head and looked for a long time, at the crab lying motionless on the sponge, its two eyes turning this way and that, and it didn’t seem all that scary, so she shuffled forward a bit.

And a bit more.

She reached out one finger, wanting to touch its shell.

One of the crab’s claws suddenly opened.

Not slowly; all at once, snap, opened very wide, wide enough to fit an egg. The inside of the claw was white, row upon row of little teeth, like a mouth. Once it was open, it lunged toward Su Tang’s finger—

“Ah!”

Su Tang flung herself backwards and landed on her bottom on the floor. She snatched her finger back and clutched it in her other hand, clutched it tight.

The claw paused in the air for a moment, then shut with a click, closing on nothing.

“It, it, it—” Su Tang sat on the floor pointing at the box, barely able to get the words out. “It tried to pinch me!”

“It was scaring you,” said Ye Zhixing. “Before you touch it, it opens its claw first to show you. If it really wanted to pinch you, it wouldn’t open that wide, it would just pinch.”

“Then why’s it scaring me?”

“Because you were going to touch it.”

Su Tang glared at the crab. The crab glared back, its two matchstick eyes turning, and drew its claw back in front of its body, folded it neatly, as if nothing had happened.

“…It’s so fierce,” said Su Tang.

“It’s not fierce,” said Ye Zhixing. “It just doesn’t want to be touched.”

Su Tang sat on the floor, hugging her knees, and looked at the crab for a while.

“Then it’s like Briquette,” she said.

Ye Zhixing glanced down at her.

She was still sitting on the floor, her bun knocked to one side by that fall, her face showing a little leftover fright and a little embarrassed pink. When she finished saying it, she seemed startled herself, as if she hadn’t expected to say that.

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer, and turned back to the crab.

---

Gu Xuerong hadn’t made a sound the whole time.

She sat on the sofa staring at the crab, so intently she didn’t notice the Devon rolling over beside her. Then she got up, came over and squatted by the box.

She squatted very close. Closer than Su Tang had, closer than Chen Xi too. Her knees almost touched the edge of the polystyrene box.

The crab’s eyes turned to face her.

She looked back at it.

After a while, she reached out her hand.

Not one finger: her whole hand, palm open, slowly, reaching into the box. Her sleeve slid back a little, showing a very thin stretch of wrist, and against it the cuff of that grey thermal top.

“Xuerong—” Su Tang’s voice rose.

The crab’s claw opened.

Same as before, all at once, snap, very wide.

Gu Xuerong’s hand stopped. It stopped about a fist’s width from the claw. She looked at the open claw, at the row of little white teeth on its inside, very intently, as if looking at a really interesting problem.

Then her fingers moved.

Very lightly, they waved once in front of the claw. Left, right, left again. Like teasing a cat.

The crab’s claw followed her fingers. Left, right, left again.

“What are you doing?” Chen Xi’s voice was very low.

“It’s following me,” said Gu Xuerong. There was something in her voice, very faint, very hard to catch, like— happiness.

She moved her fingers a little further forward.

The claw snapped shut.

## 3. Hands

In the instant the claw closed, one hand was faster.

Su Tang’s.

She threw herself across from the floor, grabbed Gu Xuerong’s wrist and yanked it back, yanked hard, her whole body falling backwards with it, and the two of them landed on the floor together.

Click.

The claw closed, on the place where Gu Xuerong’s fingers had just been, on nothing.

The shop went suddenly quiet. The three middle-school girls by the window turned round and stared this way, wide-eyed. On the cat tree, Rice Cake pricked up its ears.

Su Tang sat up, still gripping Gu Xuerong’s wrist, gripping it so hard her knuckles went white. Breathing hard, she looked down at the hand: the fingers, the back, the palm, one finger at a time, twice over, making sure there was no cut, no blood.

Then she raised her other hand and slapped the back of Gu Xuerong’s hand.

Not hard. But very loud.

“Don’t you want your hand any more?”

Gu Xuerong froze.

She sat on the floor, looking at Su Tang, then looked down at the back of her own hand. There was a faint red patch on it, from that slap.

“A claw that big,” Su Tang’s voice was shaking, and her eyes went red all at once, “didn’t you see it? A two-kilo crab, one pinch and your finger’s gone. Do you know that? Just gone. How would you play games then? How would you do the accounts? How would you—”

She couldn’t go on, and bit her lip, forcing the wetness in her eyes back down.

“…Why do you touch everything?”

Gu Xuerong looked at her.

She looked at Su Tang’s hand gripping her wrist, at Su Tang’s reddened eyes, at Su Tang’s lip, bitten white. Her expression was the same as always, very flat, very still, but her eyes moved. Not to look elsewhere; they looked a little deeper, as if carefully trying to make out something she didn’t quite recognize.

“It wouldn’t really pinch me,” she said.

“It just did!”

“It missed.”

“That’s because I pulled you!”

Gu Xuerong said nothing more.

She looked down at Su Tang’s hand on her wrist. Su Tang’s hand was warm, much warmer than her own, the palm a little damp, sweat from the scare just now.

“…Mm,” she said.

After a while, she said again:

“Sorry.”

Su Tang glared at her for several seconds, and finally let go. She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand, stood up, brushed the dust off her trousers, turned and walked away, over to the window, and stood there for a while with her back to everyone.

---

Ye Zhixing had been standing by the counter the whole time, not moving.

From Gu Xuerong reaching out, to Su Tang throwing herself across, to the slap, to Su Tang walking to the window, he had just stood there.

He had wanted to move, too. When Gu Xuerong’s hand went forward, his foot had already taken half a step. But Su Tang had been faster.

He looked at the back by the window. The bun knocked askew, three black hair ties round her wrist, her shoulders slightly hunched, rising, falling, as she got her breath back. Outside the window was snow, a white blur, the branches of the plane tree weighed down under a thick layer of it, and her shadow lay on the frosted glass, indistinct.

He watched for a while, then bent down and put the lid back on the polystyrene box.

## 4. Chopsticks

“But seriously,” Chen Xi broke the silence, “how strong is it?”

He pulled a disposable chopstick from the holder on the counter, still unopened, still in its paper sleeve. He lifted the lid of the box a crack and poked the chopstick in, right in front of the crab’s claw.

The crab’s claw opened, and pinched.

Crack.

A very crisp sound, like snapping a dry twig.

Chen Xi pulled the chopstick out. It had broken in two, the break slanted and ragged, and even the paper sleeve had been pinched through.

He stared at the two halves for several seconds.

“Holy crap,” he said. “That’s savage.”

He pulled out another one and poked it in. Crack. Broken again.

Another. Crack.

“Chen Xi!” Su Tang turned round from the window. “Stop wasting chopsticks!”

“I’m just testing.” Chen Xi laid the three broken chopsticks in a row on the counter, like exhibits. “One chopstick, how much force does a normal person need to snap it? Let me work it out…”

He actually got out his phone and started calculating.

Ye Zhixing put the lid back on properly.

“You like this kind of thing too?”

“Course I do.” Chen Xi didn’t look up. “I like all the weird stuff. Crabs, snakes, lizards, turtles, anything.” He paused. “Just can’t afford to keep them.”

“Don’t you catch fish in the river?”

“That’s little fish and shrimp,” said Chen Xi. “When I was little I lived in the countryside at my grandma’s, the fields, the river, you could catch anything. Then I came back to the county town to live with my parents, and we’re just an ordinary family, sixth floor, two bedrooms and a living room, my dad drives haulage, my mum works at a supermarket. All we’ve got at home is a little fish tank, fifty centimetres long, and I catch a few crucian carp and a few river shrimp and keep them in it, feed them fish food at two yuan a packet. The carp have died on me batch after batch, and the shrimp keep jumping out of the tank, and the next morning they’ve dried into a dried shrimp on the floor.”

“Is your tank aerated?” Ye Zhixing asked.

“No. Just a little filter hanging on the side.”

“That’s it, then,” said Ye Zhixing. “A fifty-centimetre tank, a few carp and a few shrimp crammed in, overfeed them and the leftovers rot on the bottom, the water goes bad, and the oxygen in the water drops right along with it. The fish come up to the surface and gulp for air, one gulp after another; that’s called piping. The shrimp can’t stand the suffocation, so they jump out.”

Chen Xi froze.

“…I always thought they wanted to go back to the river.”

“They weren’t trying to escape,” said Ye Zhixing. “They couldn’t breathe in the tank.”

He put his phone back in his pocket and looked at the polystyrene box.

“Stuff like that, I can only ever look at.”

“Then don’t just look,” said Ye Zhixing. “It’s yours.”

Chen Xi looked up. “…Whose?”

“Yours.” Ye Zhixing tilted his head toward the sofa. “And hers.”

Gu Xuerong was squatting on the other side of the polystyrene box, peering in through the gap under the lid. Hearing this, she raised her eyes.

“Keep it here at the shop, in the back room,” said Ye Zhixing. “Storage tub, air pump, sea salt, I’ll bring them over tomorrow. You’re in charge of buying clams and whelks at the market, she’s in charge of changing the water. How to mix the water, I’ll write it down for you.”

“Keep it at the shop?!” Su Tang turned round by the window. “There are eleven cats in this shop!”

“Put the lid on properly, weigh it down with a brick.”

“And if it gets out?”

“If it gets out,” Ye Zhixing thought about it, “then that’s the cats’ problem.”

Su Tang glared at him, speechless. Rice Cake had come down from the cat tree at some point and was crouching beside the polystyrene box, neck stretched out, nose pressed to the gap under the lid, sniffing for a long time, tail flicking back and forth.

Gu Xuerong looked at the ginger cat, then at the box.

“I’ll change it,” she said.

---

Ye Zhixing looked at him for a while, then got out his phone, swiped a few times and held it out.

“Here’s something even more savage.”

On the screen was a video. A very big aquarium, at least two metres long, its water a pale tea colour, its bottom covered with a layer of fine sand, a few pieces of driftwood resting on the sand. The light was very dim, shining down from above into the water, where it turned into swaying shafts of light.

A fish swam out from behind the driftwood.

Sixty or seventy centimetres long. Its body was round, like a thick, springy tube, almost the same width from head to tail. Its head was flat, like a snake’s. Its mouth was huge, the lower jaw jutting forward, the corners of the mouth turned down, as if it couldn’t care less about anyone.

It was black all over. But not pure black: the black ground was covered in markings, spots of white and pale yellow, large and small, scattered from head to tail, like stars in the night sky. Between the spots ran a few dark red streaks, half-hidden, and as the fish swam, the red streaks brightened and dimmed with the rise and fall of its body, like sparks not quite gone out under the charcoal. Its dorsal and anal fins were very long, running nearly the length of its body, thin, edged with a faint band of orange, rippling in the water wave after wave, like two flags in the wind.

It swam very slowly. It came up to the glass of the tank, stopped, turned its head, and its eyes faced the camera for several seconds.

Chen Xi held his breath.

“What is that?”

“A Black Devil,” said Ye Zhixing. “A kind of thunder dragon. My dad keeps it.”

“Thunder dragon?”

“Snakehead. A distant relative of those black snakeheads in your river, same family,” said Ye Zhixing. “But this one’s from Southeast Asia; its scientific name is Channa marulioides, and they say in the wild they can get over a metre. Every one has a different pattern: white spots, yellow spots, some with red. Fierce, very territorial, you have to be really careful keeping it with others; put a small one in and it becomes its fish food.”

“How much does a thing like that cost?”

“Cheaper than a golden or red arowana,” said Ye Zhixing. “Snakeheads have only started getting hyped in the last few years, and the Black Devil is in the expensive tier, only about—” he named a figure.

Chen Xi’s mouth fell open and stayed open for a long time.

“…Only about?”

Ye Zhixing ignored him and swiped the screen again.

“This is my dad’s real treasure.”

---

The second video was set in an office.

A broad solid-wood desk, on it a purple-clay tea tray, a tea set, a brush rest with a few calligraphy brushes standing in it, and on the wall a scroll of calligraphy that read “Great virtue bears all things.” Across from the desk, directly facing the door, stood an aquarium.

The aquarium was long, one metre eighty, and very tall, set into a surround of solid-wood cabinets. The back of the tank was covered by a whole sheet of imitation-rock backdrop, dark grey, with crack after crack, like a cliff face soaked in water for many years. The bottom was covered in pale blue gravel, with a few big stones on it, the stones streaked with blue-green and rust. The light shone down from above, very bright.

There was more than one fish in the tank. At the bottom lay a white-bellied shovelnose catfish with long whiskers and a red tail, lazily hugging the gravel; two smaller fish swam back and forth in the middle of the water.

But what Ye Zhixing wanted to show him was the one at the very top.

It was long, flat-sided, like a blade drawn from its sheath. It was a red arowana, but at first glance it wasn’t red all over. The base colour of its body was a silver grey with a touch of blue in it, like polished tin. But every scale— the scales were large, one after another, each like a copper coin, lined up perfectly— every scale was edged with a ring of orange-red, as if someone had taken a fine brush and traced a line round the edge of every coin. Square after square of red frames joined up, so that the whole fish seemed to wear a red net. Its gill covers were red, a shining red.

Its mouth turned upward, and from its lower jaw grew two short barbels that trembled slightly in the water. Its pectoral fins, pelvic fins and tail fin were all red, more translucent than the red frames on its body, like a few pieces of red silk with light shining through them, and the edge of the tail fin was lined with a band of deeper, darker red.

“Why isn’t it red?” Chen Xi asked.

“Red arowana is a general term,” said Ye Zhixing. “Same as a golden arowana isn’t necessarily gold. Within red arowanas there’s chilli red, blood red, orange red, and when they’re small none of them are very red; it takes years of keeping before the colour comes out bit by bit. This one went red starting from the scale frames; people who know call that the frame, and the redder and neater the frames, the more it’s worth.”

It swam extremely slowly, from one end of the tank to the other, turned, and swam back. Every time it turned, its body full of red frames flashed with light, as if someone had shaken out a net woven with red thread in the water.

“Red arowana,” said Ye Zhixing. “Top grade. My dad keeps it in his office, facing the door.”

“Why facing the door?”

“Feng shui,” said Ye Zhixing. “Supposed to draw in wealth.”

Chen Xi stared at the screen, watching the fish swim from this end of the tank to that end, and from that end back to this one.

“Did it?” he asked.

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer.

He looked at the red arowana in the video, at the “Great virtue bears all things” on the wall behind it, at the purple-clay tea set on the desk. He thought of that dinner, of the nod his father had given him from one seat away.

He took the phone back and locked the screen.

---

“So what does it eat?” Chen Xi asked. “A fish that precious.”

“Small fish and shrimp, sometimes pellets, sometimes centipedes,” said Ye Zhixing. “Mostly live food. Live crucian carp, live river shrimp, bought and kept in another tank for two days first, dosed with medicine to get rid of parasites, then fed to it.”

Chen Xi stopped for a moment.

“…Crucian carp? River shrimp?”

“Mm.”

Slowly, Chen Xi turned his head and looked at Ye Zhixing.

“The ones I’ve been catching in the river for a whole year,” he said, “crucian carp, river shrimp, the ones that died on me batch after batch, the ones that broke my heart—”

“Mm.”

“At your dad’s place,” said Chen Xi, “they’re fish food?”

Ye Zhixing looked at him and said nothing.

Chen Xi looked back at him.

After several seconds, Chen Xi burst out laughing. He laughed loudly, laughed until he was crouched on the floor, laughed until there were tears in his eyes.

“Holy crap,” he said, still laughing, “holy crap. My pets are your family’s pet’s fish food.”

He laughed for a long time. When he was done laughing, he stayed crouched there, wiped his eyes with his sleeve, the smile still on his face, but the smile had turned a little, like a fizzy drink left out too long, all the bubbles gone.

“…Fine,” he said. “That’s the gap for you.”

Ye Zhixing looked at him.

“That red arowana,” said Ye Zhixing, “spends all day in that tank, swimming from this end to that end, from that end to this end. The shovelnoses and silver arowanas at the bottom were put in to keep it company. My dad says arowanas have a temper; kept alone they tend to crash into the glass, so you put in a few other fish to distract it. But it never looks at them.”

He paused.

“For its whole life, all it gets to see is that office.”

Chen Xi wasn’t laughing any more.

He looked at Ye Zhixing for a while, then stood up and brushed off his knees.

“Your crucian carp,” said Ye Zhixing, “have at least seen a river.”

## 5. Spiders

“Aaah—!”

The scream came from the back-room doorway.

It was Su Tang.

She was standing in the back-room doorway, her back pressed to the wall, both hands over her mouth, her eyes wide and round, staring in front of her.

In front of her stood Gu Xuerong.

Gu Xuerong held her two hands cupped in front of her chest, closed together, as if holding a handful of water. There was something in her palms.

Two spiders.

Not big, grey-brown, with long, thin legs; one was sitting in her left palm, and one was crawling into the gap between the fingers of her right hand. She opened her fingers a crack to let it through, and it went through, came out on the back of her hand, and climbed along the back of her hand toward her wrist.

“Wh-wh-where did you get those?!” Su Tang’s voice was shaking.

“The back room,” said Gu Xuerong. “Behind that cupboard where the junk goes. They’d made a web in the corner.”

“Then why did you catch them?!”

“It’s cold,” said Gu Xuerong. “Behind the cupboard is next to the heating pipe, that’s the only warm bit. They were spending the winter there.”

“Then let them spend the winter there! Why did you catch them!”

“Old Zhou’s moving that cupboard tomorrow,” said Gu Xuerong. “Once it’s moved, their web’s gone.”

Su Tang stared at her, at the two spiders in her hands, her mouth open, and for a long time couldn’t say anything.

Gu Xuerong looked down at the spiders in her palms. The one climbing toward her wrist had reached the cuff of the grey thermal top and stopped, its two front legs feeling at the fabric of the cuff, as if hesitating over whether to crawl inside.

She blocked it lightly with a finger of her other hand and nudged it back into her palm.

---

Ye Zhixing came over and stopped between the two of them.

He looked at the spiders in Gu Xuerong’s hands for a while, then at Su Tang pressed against the wall, then back at Gu Xuerong.

“And you,” he said, “what are you planning to do with them?”

“Put them somewhere warm.”

“Where?”

Gu Xuerong thought about it.

“Under the stairs,” she said. “Behind the photocopier in the copy shop, there’s warm air blowing out.”

“You’re going to put two spiders behind the photocopier in the copy shop.”

“Mm.”

“If the owner finds out she’ll kill you.”

“She won’t find out.”

Ye Zhixing looked at her, silent for a few seconds.

“Gu Xuerong.”

“Mm.”

“Do you know that out of everyone I’ve ever met,” he said, “you’re the strangest one?”

Gu Xuerong raised her head and looked at him.

“And I’ve met a fair few people,” said Ye Zhixing. “Ones who skip class, ones who fight, ones who come first, ones who put on airs, rich ones, poor ones, ones who talk nonsense non-stop, ones who never say a word. But someone like you—” he thought for a moment about how to put it, “someone who goes behind a cupboard in winter to rescue spiders, and then wants to find them a warm spot behind a photocopier to spend the winter—”

He stopped.

“…I’ve honestly never seen one before.”

Gu Xuerong looked at him for a while.

“Well, now you have,” she said.

Ye Zhixing opened his mouth, and nothing came out.

Behind them, Chen Xi snorted with laughter again.

Su Tang was still pressed against the wall, looking from Ye Zhixing to Gu Xuerong and back, the expression on her face slowly changing from horror into something very complicated, somewhere between laughing and crying.

“…The two of you,” she said, “are both not normal.”

---

In the end the two spiders were put under the stairs.

Not behind the photocopier, but at the turn of the stairs, where the heating pipe came out of the wall, there was a small gap squeezed between the plaster and the pipe. Gu Xuerong squatted there with her palms against the wall, waiting for the two spiders to crawl across by themselves, into the gap, out of sight.

Ye Zhixing stood at the top of the stairs, watching her.

She squatted there a long time. After the spiders had gone in, she was still squatting, her palms still against the wall, as if feeling the warmth of the heating pipe.

“Let’s go,” he said.

She stood up and wiped her hands on her trousers.

“Will they live till spring?” she asked.

Ye Zhixing thought about it.

“Don’t know,” he said.

She nodded, as if that answer was enough, walked past him and went upstairs.

## 6. The Stove

It was a bit after six when Su Tang said she wanted to cook.

It was snowing that day, and that evening there wasn’t a single order. Old Zhou was in the kitchen wiping down the griddles; he finished one and picked up another, wiping even more slowly than usual, until in the end the griddles were shiny enough to see your reflection in, and he was still wiping.

Su Tang circled the kitchen doorway twice, lifted the curtain and looked in, let it fall, lifted it again.

“Uncle Zhou, can I use the kitchen?”

Old Zhou stopped the cloth in his hand and turned round. “What for?”

“To cook,” said Su Tang. “Nobody’s eaten yet, right? And nobody’s ordering tonight. There are tomatoes in the fridge, and eggs, and a piece of pork belly…”

“That pork is for my lunch tomorrow.”

“I know,” said Su Tang, her voice dropping, then forcing itself back up. “Just one meal.”

Old Zhou looked at her for a good while, twisting the cloth once in his hands.

“…Go on, then,” he said. “I’ve got to go to the wholesale market in a bit anyway, haven’t bought in tomorrow’s food yet. I’ll get another piece of pork.”

Ye Zhixing looked up from behind the counter. “You can cook?”

Su Tang gave him a look, and in that look was something rarely seen on her face, something like defiance, or like pride.

“Just you wait,” she said.

---

Ye Zhixing followed her to the kitchen doorway, didn’t go in, and leaned against the door frame.

The kitchen was small: just right for one person standing, two had to turn sideways. Old Zhou hadn’t left; he stood with his back against the fridge, arms folded across his chest, with an air of “let’s see what you can come up with”, taking up another chunk of what little room there was. The stove was one Old Zhou had picked himself, two burners, a very fierce flame. On the wall hung a row of spatulas, slotted spoons and wok ladles, polished till they gleamed. The window was open a crack, and cold air seeped in through it, mixing with the faint smell of cooking oil left over from the stove.

Su Tang tied her bun tight again, pushed her sleeves up to her elbows, took things out of the fridge and laid them on the chopping board one by one: four tomatoes, six eggs, a piece of pork belly, a few spring onions, a knob of ginger, a head of garlic.

First she boiled a pot of water.

When the water boiled, she put the tomatoes in one by one. Before putting them in, she cut a cross in the bottom of each tomato with the knife, very shallow, just breaking the skin. The tomatoes rolled in the boiling water for a few seconds, and the skin at the cross curled up, peeling back corner by corner. She scooped them out with the slotted spoon and put them in a bowl of cold water, pinched the curled-up corner and gave it a gentle tug, and the whole skin came off, perfectly intact, thin as a sheet of red paper.

“Why take the skin off?” Ye Zhixing asked.

“If you don’t, when you fry them the skin curls up into strips, and in your mouth it’s like chewing plastic,” said Su Tang, her hands not stopping. “Take it off, and the tomatoes go soft as soon as you fry them, and let out their juice.”

She cut the peeled tomatoes into chunks, not too big, not too small, and put them in a bowl. Then she cracked the eggs, six of them, into a bowl, added a small pinch of salt and beat them with chopsticks. She beat very fast, the chopsticks whirling a vortex in the bowl, and a layer of fine foam rose on the eggs.

Heat the wok, add oil. A bit more oil, she said; eggs drink up oil. When the oil was hot enough to give off the faintest wisp of blue smoke, she poured in the eggs.

A loud sizzle.

The moment the eggs hit the wok, their edges puffed up in a ring of golden bubbles. She didn’t turn them straight away; she waited two or three seconds, until the bottom had set, then pushed in from the edge toward the middle with the spatula, push, pause, push again. The egg set in big, tender yellow curds, soft, still a little wet where it hadn’t quite set.

“This is when you take it out,” she said, sliding the eggs onto a plate. “Any more and they’ll go tough.”

There was a little oil left in the wok. She tipped in the tomatoes and stir-fried them. As soon as the tomatoes touched the hot wok they began to soften, to collapse, red juice seeping out of the chunks and bubbling away in the bottom of the wok. She added a little salt, a little sugar, and pressed the tomatoes down with the spatula to squash them a bit, pressing out more juice.

Then she tipped the eggs back in.

A couple of turns, so that every piece of egg was coated in tomato juice.

Before taking it off the heat, she scattered over a handful of chopped spring onion.

All at once the whole kitchen was full of that sweet-and-sour smell, with the fragrance of egg in it.

---

Ye Zhixing leaned against the door frame, watching her.

He watched her move back and forth in front of the stove, flame on, flame off, picking up a bowl, putting down a plate, her movements quick and steady, as if this little two-square-metre kitchen were her territory. He watched the fine layer of sweat slowly beading on her forehead, her face reddened by the heat of the stove, the forearms that showed below her sleeves pushed up to the elbow.

On her forearms were a few small, round scars. Light brown, very old, one next to another.

From spattering oil.

“How long have you been doing this?” he asked.

“What?”

“Cooking.”

Su Tang was washing the wok and didn’t look up. “Since I was ten, I guess.”

“Ten?”

“My mum works at the textile mill, rotating shifts,” said Su Tang. “Sometimes days, sometimes nights, sometimes we don’t see each other for a whole week. When my grandma was around it was fine, grandma cooked. After grandma passed, it was me.”

She put the wok back on the stove and turned on the flame to boil off the water in it.

“At first my cooking was awful. Tomato and egg came out as a pan of mush, braised pork came out as charcoal. My mum would come home from the night shift and still have to eat it.” She smiled. “Every time, she said it was good.”

“And then?”

“And then I got the hang of it,” said Su Tang. “Watched cooking shows on TV, read recipes, asked the auntie at the restaurant downstairs. Tried one dish at a time. When I got it wrong, I tried again.”

She brought the pork belly over and cut it into big chunks, each one with the skin on, a layer of fat and a layer of lean, like the pages of a closed book.

“Nobody was going to teach me anyway,” she said. “So I taught myself.”

---

The pork belly went into cold water.

She put a few slices of ginger and a length of spring onion into the pot, poured in a ring of cooking wine and turned on the flame. The water slowly heated, and the chunks of pork slowly changed colour in it, from pink to greyish white, and a layer of grey scum floated up on the surface. She skimmed it off with a ladle bit by bit, until it was perfectly clean.

“Cold water,” she said, “so the blood comes out slowly. If you scald it in hot water, the outside cooks all at once, and the blood inside can’t get out, and it stews up smelling gamey.”

She lifted out the pork, rinsed it clean under warm water and let it drain.

Heat the wok, no oil. She tipped in a handful of rock sugar and turned the flame down low.

The rock sugar slowly melted in the wok, clear at first, then it began to bubble, the bubbles growing denser and denser, the colour slowly turning yellow, then light brown, then amber. She kept stirring slowly with the spatula, her eyes fixed on the wok, not blinking once.

“Caramelizing the sugar is the hardest part,” she said, very quietly, as if afraid of disturbing the sugar in the wok. “Too much heat and it goes bitter, too little and the colour’s not enough. You wait till it’s making these really, really tiny bubbles, and the colour is like…” she thought for a moment, “like soy sauce with water in it, and then the pork goes in.”

She tipped the pork in.

A great sizzle—

A big cloud of white steam surged up. She stir-fried fast, so that every chunk of pork was coated in that amber sugar. As soon as the skin touched the sugar it began to shine, a glossy red, as if lacquered.

Then the seasonings. Light soy sauce, a ring; dark soy sauce, half a ring, for colour; salt, a small spoonful. A short piece of cinnamon bark, two star anise, three bay leaves, in they went, a handful at a time. Last, boiling water, enough to cover the pork; lid on, flame down low.

“An hour of stewing,” she said. “Turn it once halfway, or it’ll stick to the bottom.”

She turned off the big flame, pressed the lid down well, leaned against the edge of the stove and let out a long breath.

Old Zhou hadn’t made a sound all this time. From the moment she started caramelizing the sugar, the arms folded across his chest had dropped, and he had straightened up off the fridge and craned his neck to look into the wok. Seeing the colour of the caramel, seeing her timing in putting in the pork, his eyebrows had moved.

“Your heat’s right,” he said. Three words, that was all.

Then he took off his apron, hung it on the hook behind the door, and fished his e-bike key out of his pocket.

“I’m off to the wholesale market,” he said. “That pork, give it a turn in forty minutes.”

“Got it.”

Old Zhou lifted the curtain and went out. When he reached Ye Zhixing he stopped for a moment, took one more look into the kitchen, said nothing, and went downstairs.

---

In the hour the pork was stewing, she also made a bowl of steamed egg.

Three eggs, cracked into a big bowl, a little salt, stirred slowly in one direction, and as soon as they were evenly mixed she stopped, without beating hard. Then she boiled a kettle of water, and when it boiled, poured it into a bowl to cool. After a while she tested it with a finger, waited a while longer and tested it again, until her finger could stay in the water for a couple of seconds longer.

“You have to use water that’s been boiled, cooled to warm,” she said. “Raw water has air in it, and it steams up full of little holes. Too hot is no good either: pour it in and the egg cooks into flakes; warm is just right, and the salt dissolves too.”

She poured the warm water slowly into the egg, stirring gently as she poured, about one and a half times as much water as egg. Once it was evenly mixed she ran the egg through a small strainer to catch the few white strands. There was still a thin layer of fine foam floating on top, so she tore off a corner of tissue and dabbed it lightly on the foam, and the foam went off with the tissue, bit by bit, until it was all gone. Then she covered the mouth of the bowl with cling film, tight, and pressed it down all round the rim with her finger.

“It has to be covered tight,” she said. “The steam on the lid drips down, and if it drips in, the surface comes out all pitted.”

When the water boiled, the bowl went into the steamer, the flame turned to medium-low, for eight or nine minutes.

“The heat can’t be high,” she said, watching the pot. “High heat and the egg puffs up inside all at once, all honeycomb, like an old loofah.”

---

By the time the pork was done, it was fully dark outside.

She lifted the lid.

A thick cloud of steam rushed out, rich with the smell of soy and meat, and filled the whole kitchen. The sauce in the pot had reduced right down, glossy red, coating every chunk of pork. The skin quivered, and gave way as soon as a chopstick touched it; the fat layer had stewed until it was half translucent, like a piece of amber.

She turned up the flame to reduce the sauce, and it bubbled in big blobs, thicker and thicker, glossier and glossier.

Last of all, she took out the few portions of griddle beef Old Zhou had marinated, heated them on the griddle, sizzling, and arranged them on a plate.

---

The meal was laid out on the biggest table in the front room.

A plate of tomato and egg, red and yellow, the sauce thick; a big bowl of braised pork, glossy red, steaming; a bowl of steamed egg, its surface perfectly smooth, like a tender yellow mirror, on which Su Tang had sprinkled a few drops of light soy sauce and a little chopped spring onion; a plate of griddle beef, still sizzling.

And a pot of rice, which she had made last.

Chen Xi was the first to pounce.

He took a piece of braised pork and stuffed it in his mouth, so hot he had to huff, and when he’d huffed, chewed twice, and his eyes went perfectly round.

“Holy crap,” he said, the pork still in his mouth, his words mushy. “Holy crap. Su Tang. You. Holy crap.”

“Can you say anything else?”

“Can’t say anything else.” Chen Xi took another piece. “This is a hundred times better than my mum’s. No, a thousand times. Don’t tell my mum.”

He shovelled in a big mouthful of rice, then a big chopsticksful of tomato and egg, then a piece of beef, eating without once raising his head.

---

Gu Xuerong ate very slowly.

She took a small piece of tomato and egg, put it on her rice, mixed the tomato juice into the rice with her chopsticks, mixed it evenly, and ate it in tiny mouthfuls. When she’d finished, she took another small piece.

The way she ate was much the same as usual, slow, as if carrying out a very serious task. But she kept eating. One mouthful, then another, without stopping.

Su Tang sat across from her, sneaking looks at her the whole time.

Watched her take a third piece of tomato and egg; watched her hesitate, then take a piece of braised pork, take a small bite, pause, and then eat the rest of it too. Watched her scoop a spoonful of steamed egg, the egg quivering in the spoon, blow on it and put it in her mouth.

“…Is it good?” Su Tang couldn’t help asking.

Gu Xuerong finished chewing the steamed egg in her mouth and swallowed.

“Mm.”

“Which one’s good?”

Gu Xuerong thought about it.

“The egg,” she said. “It’s smooth.”

The corners of Su Tang’s mouth shot up, very high, and no matter what she did she couldn’t get them back down. She lowered her head, shovelled in a mouthful of rice, and buried the smile in her bowl.

---

Ye Zhixing ate very little.

He took a piece of braised pork and ate it, took a chopsticksful of tomato and egg and ate it, and then just held his chopsticks, looking at the dishes on the table, not moving again.

He was thinking about other things. About the sand table, about the building with the red flag, about the red arowana in his father’s office swimming from this end to that.

Su Tang glanced at him.

“Not good?”

“It’s good.”

“Then why aren’t you eating?”

“…Not that hungry.”

Su Tang said “oh,” and reached out and pushed the bowl of steamed egg toward him.

“Then have this,” she said. “This doesn’t fill you up.”

Ye Zhixing looked down at the bowl of steamed egg. Its surface was perfectly smooth, a few spoonfuls taken out of it, and where they had been scooped, the cut showed the tender yellow inside, fine as pudding, the drops of soy sauce spreading over the surface into little patches of amber.

He picked up the spoon and took a spoonful.

Smooth. Very hot. Seasoned just right. With a very faint fragrance of egg, melting on his tongue, nothing to chew.

He took another spoonful.

He finished the whole bowl of steamed egg.

Su Tang watched from across the table, said nothing, and lowered her head and went on eating her own rice.

---

A bit after eight, Old Zhou came back.

Both his hands were full of plastic bags, meat, vegetables, a case of eggs, and a sack of rice was strapped to the back seat of his e-bike, a layer of snow on his shoulders. He carried the things into the kitchen one by one and put them in the fridge.

Su Tang was at the sink washing the pot the pork had stewed in. She ran a pot of hot water, poured it in, went round the inside of the pot once with a brush, and the amber marks of caramel on the sides dissolved and ran away with the hot water, and the bottom of the pot showed its original glossy black, perfectly clean, nothing left at all.

Old Zhou stood behind her, looking at the pot.

“Caramel done right,” he said, “comes off with a splash of hot water. Overdo the heat and the sugar burns, sticks to the bottom, and then it won’t wash off; you have to scrape it out bit by bit with steel wool.”

Su Tang turned round.

Old Zhou didn’t look at her. He reached over and picked a leftover piece out of the bowl of braised pork on the table, tossed it into his mouth and chewed twice.

He chewed very slowly.

“If you want to learn the griddle,” he swallowed the pork and turned away to stack the eggs in the fridge, his back to her, his voice muffled, “come in earlier in the afternoon.”

Su Tang’s eyes lit up all at once.

Old Zhou was already on the third row of eggs, and added, with his back to her: “Don’t come in slippers. The griddle burns feet.”

## 7. Small Hours

That night, Gu Xuerong got back to the dorm at nine-forty.

As usual, Lin Xiaoman was waiting for her. The pink insulated lunchbox sat on the little table by the bed, weighed down by the English dictionary. Lin Xiaoman leaned her head out from the top bunk. “You’re back! There’s sweet-and-sour pork today!”

Gu Xuerong looked at the lunchbox.

“…I’ve already eaten.”

Lin Xiaoman stopped. “Already eaten?”

“Su Tang cooked.”

“Su Tang? The one from Class 3?” Lin Xiaoman’s eyes went wide. “She can cook?”

“Mm.”

Lin Xiaoman looked at her for a while, as if digesting this. Then she said “Oh,” and then, “So this—”

“Keep it,” said Gu Xuerong. “I’ll eat it later.”

“Later meaning when?”

“…Three or four.”

Lin Xiaoman looked at her with her mouth open, as if she wanted to say something. Three or four? Isn’t that the middle of the night? Eating then? The food would be stone cold. But she looked at Gu Xuerong’s face, and it was as pale as ever, and yet somehow, somehow not quite so pale. There was a little colour on her lips. And something in her eyes too, as if someone had turned a lamp up one notch.

“…Fine,” said Lin Xiaoman. “Make sure you eat it, then.”

“Mm.”

---

Three-forty in the morning.

The dorm was very dark. Lin Xiaoman was asleep on the top bunk, breathing very evenly, now and then turning over, the bed boards creaking. The other two girls were asleep too; one was grinding her teeth, one was talking in her sleep, and tonight it seemed to be chemical equations.

Gu Xuerong sat up.

She didn’t turn on the light; she felt her way in the dark to the pink insulated lunchbox on the little table and twisted off the lid. The food was still the tiniest bit warm; the lunchbox could keep it warm, but not for that long, and all that was left was a very faint layer of warmth, like the last bit of body heat left in the bed on a winter morning.

Sweet-and-sour pork, piece after piece, coated in sauce that had already set, glistening. The rice had gone a little hard, but was still edible. Beside it were some stir-fried greens, gone limp.

She picked up the spoon and ate, one mouthful at a time.

Very slowly. Very quietly. When the spoon touched the bottom of the lunchbox, she would stop and cock her ear toward the top bunk, make sure Lin Xiaoman hadn’t woken, then go on eating.

The strip of sky outside the window was still dark. Now and then a car went by in the distance, its headlights sweeping across the wall of the building opposite, sweeping over, then back.

She ate most of the box.

When she’d finished, she put the lid back on the lunchbox, set it back on the little table and weighed it down with the English dictionary, set squarely, exactly the way Lin Xiaoman had left it.

Then she lay down.

She didn’t fall asleep right away. She lay on her side, facing the wall; the water stain on the wall shaped like a rabbit couldn’t be seen in the dark. Her hand slid into her sleeve and found the back of her other hand, found the place Su Tang had slapped that afternoon.

It had long since stopped being red, and it didn’t hurt.

Her hand stayed there a long time.

---

The next morning, when Lin Xiaoman woke up, the very first thing she did was lean out from the top bunk to look at the lunchbox.

The lunchbox was still on the little table, the dictionary on top, exactly the way she had left it the night before.

Her heart sank. She climbed down, moved the dictionary aside and twisted off the lid.

Empty. Mostly empty. All that was left was a little rice, a few greens, and on the bottom of the box a layer of set, glistening sweet-and-sour sauce.

She looked at the lunchbox for a good while.

Then she turned and looked at the lower bunk.

Gu Xuerong was still asleep. Lying on her side, facing the wall, the quilt pulled up to her chin, only the back of her head showing, and that tiny black bun. Her breathing was very light, very even; she was sleeping deeply.

Lin Xiaoman crouched by the bed and looked at her for a while.

She thought: her schedule is a bit ghoulish. So sleepy in the daytime, head down in class, head down between classes, like a phone with a dead battery.

But she ate.

She ate most of the box.

Lin Xiaoman put the lid back on the lunchbox and took it to the washroom to wash. As she washed it she hummed, hummed what she herself couldn’t have said, but hummed it very loudly anyway, and another girl washing her face in there gave her several looks.

She didn’t care.
