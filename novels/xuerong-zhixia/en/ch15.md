# Chapter Fifteen　Demand

## 1. Instant Noodles

Ye Zhixing was woken by Your Highness.

The Ragdoll was behind the sliding door to the back room, yowling again and again, long drawn-out yowls that even bent in the middle, like someone downstairs doing vocal warm-ups for opera. On the other sofa, Ye Zhixia pulled the quilt over her head and said, muffled, “It’s hungry.”

“It’s not hungry. I fed it at ten last night.”

“Then it’s swearing at you.”

He sat up and went barefoot to slide the door open. The moment it opened, Your Highness stopped yowling, squeezed out through the gap, walked across his instep to the food bowl, lowered its head to sniff, then lifted it and yowled once more.

There was still half a bowl of food in it.

“It doesn’t like that one.” Ye Zhixia was still curled up under the quilt. “Uncle He changed the brand last time, and it stopped eating.”

“How do you know?”

“Xuerong told me.”

Ye Zhixing picked up the bag of cat food and turned it over to read the ingredients, which told him nothing. He took a photo and sent it to Uncle He, asking whether the old brand could still be bought. Uncle He replied quickly: deliveries have stopped, the pet shop’s shut, wait a while. Then one more line: Your Highness will eat after missing two meals. Don’t spoil it.

He read it out to Ye Zhixia.

“Uncle He doesn’t understand Your Highness,” said Ye Zhixia.

Breakfast was instant noodles. Old Zhou’s fridge had been emptied a few days earlier and its contents taken over to Xuerong’s by Uncle He; all that was left in the kitchen was a case of braised-beef noodles, half a tray of eggs, a jar of Lao Gan Ma, and a few spring onions about to sprout. Ye Zhixing boiled the water while Ye Zhixia sat on the bar counter swinging her legs. She said she wanted the hu la tang from the place at the mouth of the lane, and that the owner had gone back to his home village for New Year and who knew if he’d come back; then she said there was a girl in her class who posted her mum’s home-baked cakes on her Moments every single day, and she had blocked her.

“If you’ve blocked her, how do you know she posts every day?”

“I click in and have a look every couple of days.”

The water boiled. He cracked an egg into each bowl, pressed the noodle blocks down, and put the lids on.

In the three minutes waiting for the noodles, he opened his computer.

There were four new emails, all from the night before. One was from a hotpot chain in the city: an ordering system signed last November, half built, and now the client said all their branches were closed, the project was on hold, and the final payment would be “discussed after reopening.” One was from a supermarket: they had been due to launch membership points this month, now they weren’t, and could some of the deposit be refunded. The other two were worded much the same, round and round with “these special times,” “postpone,” “we hope you understand.”

He answered them one by one. Where refunds were owed, he refunded according to the contract. Where they weren’t, he listed the parts already delivered in a table and attached it, and kept the wording very polite.

City Matters was fine; if anything City Matters was busier than before. But more than half the company’s money came from its other lines: ordering, membership and booking systems for restaurants, supermarkets, driving schools and gyms in the county and the city. All of those places were shut now.

He pulled over a sheet of paper and did the sums. Ye Zhixia leaned across from the other end of the bar to look.

“Bro, what are you working out?”

“How many more months of instant noodles the two of us can afford.”

“And how many is it?”

“This month’s covered. Next month’s tight, but that’s covered too.” He drew a line under the figure. “Last year, before I handed in that report, I left three months of wages in the company account and never touched a penny of it. Back then it was Dad I was guarding against. I didn’t expect to get both at once.”

“And after the three months?”

“After the three months, your brother’s going to have to come up with something else.”

“Stop thinking for a minute. Your noodles have gone to mush,” said Ye Zhixia.

He turned the paper over, face down on the table.

The noodles really had gone soggy. Ye Zhixia ate two mouthfuls, ladled in a big spoonful of Lao Gan Ma, ate until she was dripping with sweat, said between mouthfuls that these were the worst instant noodles she had eaten in her life, and in the end drank the soup as well.

By noon Your Highness still hadn’t touched the bowl. A little after two in the afternoon, while Ye Zhixia was having a nap, it walked over by itself and finished the half bowl.

## 2. Homework

No. 8 High’s online classes stopped on a Monday.

The county education bureau had issued a notice: until school officially reopened, no school was to teach new material, and online work would focus on revision and self-study. The notice was forwarded to the class group after nine on Sunday evening, and Zheng Xin added a line after it: Please plan your time sensibly, homework will be posted over the coming days.

The homework went up all day.

Maths was two papers plus three chapters of the workbook. One paper for physics, one for chemistry; for biology, copy out the key points, three times each. For Chinese, an essay, four classical passages to translate, and a book report. Even the politics teacher, who had dropped offline halfway through a lesson and never come back, was back, with forty multiple-choice questions in a file called “New Microsoft Word Document (3).”

No lessons, but the questions came first.

At three in the afternoon, it was Lin Xiaoman who started the voice call in “Back Street Cats”.

Her way of starting a voice call was to send “help” first, then a photo. The photo was of a physics problem, with arrows drawn all round it in pencil, the arrows fighting each other. Then the call rang.

Chen Xi was the first to join, chewing something.

“Which problem.”

“Number seven. The slope one.”

“Hang on a minute, I’m eating dumplings.”

“Then why did you join?”

“My mum made too many. She always makes too many.” Chen Xi’s words were mushy. “Pork, prawn and chive. Anyone want some, I’ll get my dad to bring them— my dad can’t go out, the estate won’t let anyone out. Never mind.”

In the background a woman’s voice, very close, asked who he was talking to. Chen Xi said classmates. The woman said then tell your classmates to come and eat too. Chen Xi said Mum, she’s in a village. The woman said oh, well you eat then, eat plenty, the vinegar’s on the table.

Su Tang was second to join, and the moment she joined there was a shriek. Not hers; someone behind her.

“Su Yu! Give the tablet back to your brother! That’s for your brother’s online classes— no, there aren’t online classes any more, it’s still your brother’s!” Her voice went far away, then came close again. “Sorry, sorry, my brothers. Two of them. Fighting over the tablet.”

“Fighting over it for what?” Lin Xiaoman asked.

“One wants to watch cartoons, one wants to play Honor of Kings,” said Su Tang. “There’s only one tablet in the house. My dad’s phone screen is cracked, my mum’s phone she wants for her TV dramas, my phone— my phone is in my hand right now, talking to you lot. So they’re fighting.”

“How old are your brothers?”

“One’s twelve, one’s six.”

“And the twelve-year-old can’t beat the six-year-old?”

“He can, he doesn’t dare,” said Su Tang. “If he hits him, my dad will sit him down and reason with him, and reasoning takes an hour. He’d rather get bitten.”

The six-year-old must have heard, and yelled from behind her: “Sis, I didn’t bite him!”

Gu Xuerong joined without making a sound. Her little avatar lit up in the group call, and the microphone icon under her name was grey, on mute.

Ye Zhixing was last to join. He was on the computer, and the internet upstairs at the cat café was better than in the basement, so his sound was very clear, clear enough for everyone to hear Ye Zhixia telling off Your Highness in the background.

“What’s it done now?” Chen Xi asked.

“It’s sitting on my test paper.” Ye Zhixia’s voice came closer. “My English paper. It’s sitting on it, I can’t pull it out, and it just looks at me.”

“So pick it up.”

“It won’t let you pick it up. It’s Your Highness.”

Lin Xiaoman read out number seven again.

Chen Xi had finished his dumplings and started explaining. He explained problems the same way he did in the classroom, no padding; first he asked whether she had drawn the force diagram, and how many forces she had drawn. Lin Xiaoman said five. Chen Xi said, a block sitting still on a slope, you’ve drawn five forces, where did the extra two come from. Lin Xiaoman said, one is the sliding-down force, and one is centripetal force.

Chen Xi’s end went quiet for two seconds.

“The sliding-down force is a component of gravity. You’ve drawn gravity, and then drawn that as well, you’ve counted it twice,” he said. “Centripetal force— where’s the circular motion here?”

“…The question has the word ‘around’ in it.”

“‘Passes around a fixed pulley.’ It’s the rope that goes around.”

Lin Xiaoman said “ah,” followed by the sound of an eraser rubbing back and forth on paper, rubbing very hard.

Chen Xi explained it from the beginning. Halfway through, Lin Xiaoman said hang on, let me write it down. Chen Xi waited while she wrote. When she’d written it, he carried on. When he’d finished, he asked if she’d got it, and she said she had.

“Then say it back to me.”

She said it back, and got half of it right.

Chen Xi explained it again.

When he’d finished this time, Lin Xiaoman said: “Then I’ll learn this by heart.”

“You can’t learn it by heart,” said Chen Xi. “Change the angle, change the mass, and what you’ve memorized is no use.”

“Then I’ll learn the changed ones by heart too.”

At Chen Xi’s end there was the sound of chopsticks being set down on the rim of a bowl.

“…Fine,” he said. “Learn it. When you’ve learned it I’ll set you three variations, and you do them. If you get them wrong I’ll explain again.”

“OK.”

Su Tang cut in at this point to say she couldn’t do the last long question on the second maths paper either, and could Chen Xi explain that in a bit too. Chen Xi said sure, at the group-class rate. Su Tang said you said last time no discounts for classmates. Chen Xi said right, no discounts for classmates, so ten yuan. Su Tang said then I’ll treat you to a meal instead. Chen Xi said meals don’t count. Su Tang said you’re so stingy. Chen Xi said this is called principle. Ye Zhixia said from the background, Brother Chen Xi, you’re such a poser.

“The biology thing,” Lin Xiaoman said suddenly, “have you copied it out? Three times each key point.”

“No,” said Chen Xi. “Copying three times is useless.”

“The teacher said three times.”

“The teacher said three times, but she’s not going to count them notebook by notebook.”

“What if she does?” said Lin Xiaoman. “I’m already on chapter two.”

Nobody argued with her about it. Su Tang said Xiaoman you’re amazing, Lin Xiaoman said I’m not amazing, my hand just aches, and then she really did shake her hand, shook it so hard her phone shook with it, and the call whooshed a few times.

Gu Xuerong’s microphone stayed grey the whole time.

She was in the basement with her maths workbook open on the table: a few problems done on the first page, the rest blank. She wasn’t doing homework. She was listening. Chen Xi explaining, Su Tang’s two brothers fighting in the background, Lin Xiaoman scrubbing with her eraser, Your Highness yowling, Ye Zhixia telling it off, all mixed together in her earphones.

She turned the volume up one notch.

---

When the problems were mostly done, Ye Zhixing spoke up.

“There’s something I want to tell you.”

“You all know what happened with my family, so I won’t go over it again,” he said. “The shop and the company are all mine now, just me, and whatever’s lost or made is on my head. City Matters is fine, busier than before. But the other parts of the company, the systems for restaurants and supermarkets—those places are all shut now, nobody wants a new system, and the ones who’d signed are pushing things back. To put it bluntly, I’m currently a boss who has to count his instant noodles by the tub. Money might be tight for a while.”

The call went quiet for a moment. At Su Tang’s end even her brothers stopped squabbling; whether she had clamped a hand over their mouths or they had sensed something, nobody knew.

“How tight?” Chen Xi asked.

“This month’s fine. Wages go out as normal,” said Ye Zhixing. “After that, we’ll have to see. So I want to think about where to go now, while it’s not that tight yet. Any ideas you’ve got, just say them. Nobody gets docked for a bad one.”

The first to speak was Gu Xuerong.

Her microphone icon lit up, and stayed lit for several seconds before any sound came.

“Maybe you could make games.”

Everyone waited for her to go on.

“Everybody’s stuck at home right now,” she said. “Apart from playing games, there isn’t anything else to do.”

“Eh—” Su Tang drew it out. “Make games? That’s way too simple~ I mean, that’s thinking about it too simply.” She corrected herself at once. “Everyone plays the same few, Honor of Kings, PUBG, all the boys in my bigger little brother’s class play them. Something a little workshop like us makes, would anyone play it?”

Having said it, she added: “Though what Xuerong said is right too, everyone really is bored right now…”

“Not that kind of game,” said Ye Zhixing. You could hear he was thinking.

At Chen Xi’s end there were chopsticks again; he had probably picked up another dumpling.

“I don’t know,” said Chen Xi. “Maybe you could take on custom mini-programs for businesses. Everything’s moving online now: restaurants want online ordering, supermarkets want community group buying, schools want clock-ins. There’s work out there.” He swallowed. “But I don’t think you’re in a position to.”

“What do you mean?” Ye Zhixia asked for her brother from the background.

“What I said,” said Chen Xi. “Custom work for businesses, the big jobs need tenders, licences, past cases, a corporate account. The small jobs don’t need any of that, but the owners of the small jobs are like you right now, they’ve got no money either. You finish the job, they give you an IOU, three months’ payment terms at least, and whether you ever get the money is another story. What you’re short of isn’t work, it’s cash. Take on a pile of jobs you can’t get paid for, and you’re just using your own cash to front other people.” He paused. “And besides, you’re a high-school kid running a company. Normally people see you’ve done well with Town Matters, they’re happy to sign. At a time like this, people want someone they can rely on. In their eyes, you’re not that.”

Nobody picked it up.

“…Brother Chen Xi,” said Ye Zhixia, “you’re really harsh.”

“I’m right.”

“I know you’re right,” said Ye Zhixia. “That’s why it’s harsh.”

Ye Zhixing laughed once, very short, audible over the call.

“He’s right.”

---

For the next half hour, nobody really talked about anything that mattered.

Lin Xiaoman said her dad had killed a chicken today, the chicken had got away, he’d chased it all round the yard and finally caught it behind the woodpile, and there were chicken feathers stuck all over him. Su Tang said her mum had cried at a TV drama again yesterday, one about doctors, and her dad had said next to her it’s all acting, and her mum had said I know it’s acting, and carried on crying. Chen Xi said his dad had been at home for twenty-something days and had tightened every screw in the flat, even the one on the back of the remote control, and once he’d tightened it the battery cover wouldn’t open, and now to change the channel you had to poke it with a toothpick. Ye Zhixia said Your Highness was sitting on her test paper again, maths this time.

For a while nobody said anything, and there were only the sounds of everyone’s homes: the TV at Su Tang’s, the chickens in Lin Xiaoman’s yard, the extractor fan at Chen Xi’s. At the cat café there was nothing, only the hum of the electric heater.

Then Ye Zhixia said: “Bro, make Rice Cake.”

“What?”

“Rice Cake. People are still sharing that Rice Cake video,” said Ye Zhixia. “Someone posted it in my class group yesterday, with the caption ‘me and my diet plan’.”

Su Tang said “ah”: “That one! I saw it on Douyin too, loads of people in the comments asking which shop the cat’s from.”

“I mean make a game,” said Ye Zhixia, a little impatient. “Rice Cake on a diet, as a little game. The kind you just tap and play, you open it right in WeChat. All the girls in my class play those.”

There was no sound from Ye Zhixing’s end.

“Why aren’t you saying anything?”

“I’m thinking.”

“Then think,” said Ye Zhixia. “Your Highness, get off.”

Su Tang’s end dropped out first. She shouted “Su Yu, what are you doing,” and then she was gone. Two minutes later she sent a text: the little one pulled out my charger!!! my phone’s dead!! going to get it back!!

Lin Xiaoman’s mum called her from the yard to feed the pigs, so she left too.

Chen Xi said he was going to wash up, and left.

That left Ye Zhixing and Gu Xuerong, two avatars hanging there, one lit, one grey.

“That game thing,” said Ye Zhixing. “Once I’ve thought it through properly I’ll tell you. You brought it up first, so you’re in on it.”

The microphone lit up for a moment. “…Mm. Don’t think about it for too long, then.”

One after the other, they left the call.

## 3. The Licence

That evening, Chen Xi sent Ye Zhixing a message on his own.

> if youre doing mini games
> ads only no in app purchases
> in app purchases need a publishing licence you wont get one and you cant wait for one

Ye Zhixing replied:

> i know

> i know you know
> im worried youll rush

Ye Zhixing replied “mm,” then typed another line:

> that class of yours you can list it in the tutoring section on town matters free

> no need
> my group has enough people
> if i list it youd have to handle my refunds i dont want to get tangled up in accounts with you

Ye Zhixing took a screenshot of these lines and sent it to Big Liu, the head of tech at the company, with one line after it: meeting tomorrow morning.

Big Liu was twenty-six, from the county, had done two years of outsourcing work in Zhengzhou, come back the year before last to get married, and had a baby last year. More than half the code for the company’s lines was his. When the epidemic came and the office closed, he worked from home, at a desk that was his family’s sewing-machine table, with the baby asleep on the bed behind him. During the meeting the baby cried three times, and each time Big Liu said “hang on,” muted himself, and came back humming a song Ye Zhixing had never heard.

Ye Zhixing laid out the idea: make WeChat mini-games, start small, a week or two each, as fast as they could, put them out the moment they were done; if one got players, keep building it, add levels, add skins, add a leaderboard; if nobody played, drop it and move on to the next. No in-app purchases, ads only. Rewarded videos, banners, interstitials.

“You need a thousand unique visitors before the ad programme switches on,” said Big Liu. “Fifty-fifty split.”

“I know.”

“What’s the first one?”

“Rice Cake.”

There was a two-second silence at Big Liu’s end, then he laughed. “That fat cat? My wife shared that video.”

“That one.”

“Right,” said Big Liu. “Cocos can publish straight to WeChat mini-games, I’ve done one before. Someone needs to draw Rice Cake.”

“One of the street promoters at the shop can draw,” said Ye Zhixing. “I’ll have her draw at home.”

“And the gameplay?”

“Cat treats fall from the top, Rice Cake runs left and right to catch them. It can only eat three a day; once it’s had three, it has to dodge the rest,” said Ye Zhixing. “Same as the rule on the whiteboard at the shop.”

“And if it can’t dodge?”

“It gets a size fatter. Fat enough, game over.”

“How do revives work? Share to a group to revive? That converts well.”

“No. That counts as forced sharing, it’ll get us banned,” said Ye Zhixing. “Watch a rewarded video to revive.”

“Fine, you’re the boss,” said Big Liu. “Who’s testing? If I write it and test it myself, I won’t find anything. Whatever you write yourself, wherever you click, it works.”

By then, Ye Zhixing had already thought it through.

“I know someone,” he said. “Seventh on the whole Nightmare Raid server. She took out that game’s age check herself, and kept a log afterwards: which line she changed, what she changed, why, and what might break because of it, all in more detail than the cat café’s ledger. She never takes the proper route in any game. The places at the edge of the map where there’s no path, corners, underwater, the bottom of cliffs, wherever nobody else goes, she has to go and have a look.”

“…Is that a tester you’re getting, or a little empress?” said Big Liu.

“Both.”

---

Uncle He phoned at noon the next day. Ye Zhixing had sent him a long message the night before, and instead of replying in writing he called.

“Those two at the cat café are on part-time agreements, signed with the shop,” said Uncle He. “To move them to the company, you first end the shop agreement, then sign again with the company. That’s not hard, e-signatures will do, press a fingerprint on the phone.”

“Mm.”

“Su Tang’s sixteen, and Gu Xuerong’s sixteen too,” said Uncle He. “Under eighteen means they’re juvenile workers. The company has to register them with the labour department, and they need a medical check before they start. The hospitals aren’t doing those now; once things open up, I’ll take them to catch up. And there are rules about the work: heavy, high up, toxic, none of that.”

“One’s testing, one’s customer service. Both at home, on their phones.”

“Then that’s fine,” said Uncle He. “What wages do I put down?”

“Gu Xuerong two thousand five hundred. Su Tang by the hour, eighteen an hour.”

There was no sound at Uncle He’s end. Ye Zhixing waited.

“Young master,” said Uncle He. “Gu Xuerong’s a night owl.”

“I know.”

“Don’t let her test till three in the morning.”

“…I know.”

“Good,” said Uncle He. “I’ll draft the contracts this afternoon and send them to you to look at.”

## 4. Contracts

Ye Zhixing called Su Tang first.

When she answered, her voice was very low, and there was an echo behind her, drip, drip, like she was in a washroom.

“Where are you?”

“In the toilet,” said Su Tang. “It’s the only place at home with a door that locks. Go on.”

He told her. Customer service for Town Matters, remote, paid by the hour, eighteen an hour, the contract moving from the shop to the company.

At her end, first came an “eh.”

“Me? Customer service?” she said. “C-can I do that? I type really fast! No, I mean—” Her voice suddenly dropped. “You’re not doing this because… I mean, because of my family…”

“No,” said Ye Zhixing. “The epidemic section gets forty or fifty requests for help a day, and Xiaowen answers them on her own, till midnight. Last week an old woman posted that her gas cylinder was empty, and Xiaowen answered three hours late. At the shop you talk to customers better than anyone.”

“…Oh.”

“We really are short of people.”

“Oh,” said Su Tang again, and this time her voice brightened. “Then I need to ask my mum and dad! Wait a sec! Just a sec!”

She put the phone down on the washbasin and ran out. Ye Zhixing heard the toilet door open and close, someone singing on the TV outside, a little boy shouting “Sis, are you done yet,” Su Tang shouting “No!”, and then her talking to someone, through the door, too muffled to make out.

Seven or eight minutes later she came back, a little out of breath.

“My dad says signing a contract is a good thing,” she said. “He says before I sign, send him the contract, he wants to read it clause by clause. Sorry, he’s like that, he reads the whole manual when he buys a rice cooker.”

“He should.”

“My mum says…” Su Tang paused, as if choosing which part she could say. “My mum says it’s better than waiting tables somewhere. And then she said again that I still have to go to teachers’ college.” She laughed first. “She’s been going on all day. Their mill has stopped work, they only pay the minimum wage now, and my dad’s factory is about the same. The auntie downstairs from us works at the tax bureau, she’s been at home every day, and her wages haven’t lost a cent. So my mum says, see, see, people with a permanent government post.”

“Mm.”

“She’s not complaining,” Su Tang said at once. “She’s just… saying. My mum and dad are fine, their social insurance is all paid up, they just haven’t got a permanent post.”

“I know.”

That evening, Ye Zhixing sent the contract over. A little after nine, Su Tang sent a photo: under the light in her living room, her dad in reading glasses, holding the phone far away, a pencil in his other hand, making notes on a sheet of white paper. On the paper was written “Party A”, “Party B”, “probation period?”, “Clause 7 confidentiality what does it mean”.

Then one line after it: he’s been reading it for forty minutes.

Half an hour later:

> my dad says its fine!! he says the contracts very properly written, he says your companys a proper company
> he also asked how old you are
> i said sixteen
> he went quiet for ages
> then he said the young will surpass us
> im dying

---

He called Gu Xuerong twice.

The first time nobody answered. He knew she didn’t necessarily look at her phone in the daytime, and the signal in the basement was bad. The second time it rang five times, and she answered.

“Hello.”

“That game from this afternoon,” he said. “I’ve decided to do it.”

He went through what had been said at the meeting: a mini-game, Rice Cake, catching cat treats, Big Liu, Cocos, revive by rewarded video. When he got to the word “testing,” he put it another way.

“We need someone to test. That means playing the game over and over, finding what’s wrong with it, writing it down. Remote, you can do it from where you are. Your cat café contract moves to the company, and from now on instead of the accounts, you do this.”

There was no sound at the other end.

“Two thousand five hundred a month.”

Still no sound. He heard the compressor of the little fridge in her basement start up with a hum.

“Testing, that kind of work,” said Gu Xuerong. “What do people outside normally pay for it?”

He hadn’t expected her to ask that first.

“If you outsource it, it’s by the person-day,” he told her honestly. “One person, one day, two or three hundred. If you count ten days a month—”

“So two thousand five counts as cheap.”

“It is cheap. I’m not going to lie to you.”

“So in other words, I’m the cheap one.”

“Right. For now, you’re the cheap one.”

“Cheap is good,” said Gu Xuerong. “My dad said it once: on a building site, the last one to get laid off is always the cheapest one.”

Ye Zhixing was thrown for a second.

“…That’s probably the one thing your dad’s got right in his whole life,” he said. “But that’s not how it’s counted with me. If you find more wrong with it than the people outside who charge two or three hundred a day, I’ll have to raise you next month. And if I don’t, Big Liu will be the first one to object.”

She went quiet again, not for long this time.

“Let’s leave it at that for now, then,” she said.

Then she said, “One more thing. Don’t pay my wages into that card.”

“Why can’t it go onto the card?”

“My dad took me to open the card,” she said. “In middle school, when the school wanted fees paid, he took me. It has his phone number on it. When money goes into the card, his phone rings.”

“And the second he sees it, he’ll come asking for it. Right?”

“He will,” said Gu Xuerong. “He owes people money out there.”

Ye Zhixing didn’t ask how much, or who to. He said they’d use WeChat for now, and he’d ask Uncle He about the bank.

When he’d hung up, he sent Uncle He a message. Uncle He replied twenty minutes later:

> Over sixteen, with a labour contract and proof of income, she can go to the bank with her own ID and open one herself, no guardian needed.
> When the banks open, I’ll take her.
> The new card will have her own number on it.

He forwarded it to Gu Xuerong unchanged.

She replied with one word: “OK.”

## 5. Enzyme Drink

A little after eleven at night, Su Tang’s phone was charged, and she got on MC.

Gu Xuerong was already in. She wasn’t building; she was putting up a fence beside the little wooden house by the river, setting the posts one by one, enclosing a neat square of ground with three sheep shut inside.

“What are you doing?”

“Penning sheep.”

“Why pen sheep?”

“The last ones ran off,” said Gu Xuerong. “Beds need wool. We’ll need more.”

Su Tang said “oh,” got out some wooden fence posts too, and helped set them beside her. As she set them, she started talking. When she talked her hands usually didn’t stop, the same as when she brushed cats at the shop while chatting to customers.

She said she’d got her charger back off Su Yu, and Su Yu had cried, and when he’d finished crying he’d come running over wanting her to pick him up. She said her dad had read the contract for over an hour, and after he’d finished he was still in the living room telling her mum “the young will surpass us,” and her mum had asked him what it meant, and he had explained for ages, and her mum had said oh, and gone back to her TV drama. She said her bigger little brother, Su Lei, had secretly asked her whether the brother who ran a company hired first-years in middle school, he wanted to earn money to buy a phone too.

“What did you say?”

“I said they don’t hire primary school kids,” said Su Tang. “He said he’s in first year of middle school. I said first year of middle school is still primary school. He’s furious, he’s still not speaking to me.”

The fence was finished. The three sheep wandered round and round inside it, and one pressed its face against the fence, looking at them.

Su Tang’s character crouched outside the fence for a moment, stood up, then crouched again. When she crouched and stood up in the game, it meant she was thinking something over; Gu Xuerong knew that by now.

“Um…” said Su Tang. “This afternoon you said your dad owes people money. I-I’m not trying to pry, if you don’t want to say—”

“Enzyme drink,” said Gu Xuerong.

“…What?”

“A kind of liquid. In bottles, yellow, they said drinking it cures illness. Lowers blood pressure, lowers blood sugar, weight loss, beauty, cures everything,” said Gu Xuerong. “He sold it with that auntie. A few years ago. First you pay out of your own pocket for stock, a big batch, then sell it to other people, and they buy stock and sell it on further down. The more you sell, the more money the people above give you.”

“Isn’t that…”

A sheep baa-ed inside the fence.

“At first it sold. To relatives, to the people on the building sites, to the parents of Tiantian’s kindergarten classmates. Then it stopped selling. It piled up at home, most of all in the kitchen, case after case, from the floor up to the roof beam. My bed was in between the cases,” said Gu Xuerong. “At night when I slept, I could smell it, sour, a bit like vinegar gone off.”

Su Tang’s character stopped crouching, and stood still.

“Then it went out of date,” said Gu Xuerong. “He had me take it to the ditch behind the house and pour it away. Unscrew bottle after bottle and pour. It took a week. For more than a month afterwards that ditch smelled of it, and the neighbours came round to curse.”

“…How much money did you pour away?”

“Don’t know. He borrowed money to buy the stock. He’s still paying it back.”

She set the last fence post, closing a gap.

“He owed money before too,” she said. “Grandma said. Grandma said when he was young he got in a fight and stabbed someone with a knife, didn’t kill him, but paid them a lot of money, sold the house even. Grandma said that was when my mum saw him for what he was.”

“Your grandma told you that?”

“Not told me. Said it at the table,” said Gu Xuerong. “They talked whether I was there or not. My uncle’s wife said things too. She said it very flatly, like reading out accounts, item by item, no lead-in at the front, no summing up at the end.

“This auntie now,” she said, “is the third.”

Su Tang’s character stood where it was for a good while.

Gu Xuerong thought she had frozen.

Then Su Tang said: “Xuerong, a sheep’s got out.”

“What?”

“On your side, that corner, there’s a gap.” Su Tang’s voice was suddenly urgent. “One’s got out! It’s heading for the river! Quick quick quick—”

Gu Xuerong turned round, and sure enough, a sheep was ambling out of the corner of the fence, next to the gap she had just filled— she had filled the wrong block— and walked down to the river and lowered its head to eat grass.

The two of them gave chase.

The sheep wouldn’t come; a rope wouldn’t hold it. Su Tang said lure it with wheat, Gu Xuerong had no wheat, Su Tang said then go and cut grass, grass drops seeds, no, seeds won’t do, it has to be wheat, Gu Xuerong said then what do we do, Su Tang said then push it, and the two of them pushed the sheep, one behind the other, one push and it moved one block, one push and it moved one block. Halfway there, Su Tang accidentally pushed it into the river, and the sheep floated in the water baa-ing, and Su Tang laughed on voice chat until she could hardly breathe, saying sorry sorry sorry, I didn’t mean to, this poor sheep.

In the end it was Gu Xuerong who went into the water and shoved it back up, block by block, from behind.

By the time the sheep was back in the pen, it was nearly one.

“I should sleep,” said Gu Xuerong.

“Mm! Then go to sleep!” said Su Tang. “Tomorrow I start work, my first day as customer service, I’m so nervous.”

“You’re not nervous.”

“I am nervous!”

“You can talk to anyone.”

“That’s because…” Su Tang got halfway and didn’t finish. “Never mind, go to sleep, good night!”
