# Chapter Sixteen　Flexible

## 1. Interviews

The first interviewee’s cat got into the shot.

It was a tabby, which emerged from the bottom left of the frame and walked across the keyboard, leaving a long string of “ggggggggggggg” in the code editor on screen. The man on the other end was twenty-eight or twenty-nine, from Hangzhou, had worked three years at a company making HTML5 mini-games, and had a very good CV. He lifted the cat down, said sorry about that, and then said: “Mr. Ye, I’ll be straight with you. Remote is fine, twenty-two thousand a month, with social insurance and housing fund paid on the Hangzhou base. If you don’t pay those, twenty-five thousand.”

Ye Zhixing wrote the figure down on paper, and when he’d written it, drew a circle round it.

“We’re in a county town here.”

“I know,” said the man. “That’s why I’m asking that much. Can you find anyone who knows Cocos where you are?”

They couldn’t. Everyone knew that.

The second was from Zhengzhou, and his price was reasonable, twelve thousand. Big Liu asked him three questions. He answered the first well, stumbled over the second, and for the third said “one moment, let me think,” after which there came from the call a very faint sound of typing, and that rustle of a Baidu results page being scrolled. Big Liu muted his own microphone and sent Ye Zhixing a WeChat message: he’s searching.

Ye Zhixing replied: I can hear.

The third was a local, twenty years old, who had done half a year of front-end at a training school in the city and sent a photo of his completion certificate, gold characters on red. Ye Zhixing had him share his screen and write a button: click once, the number goes up by one. He wrote for twenty minutes, and got it done; click once, and the number went up by two.

“…Is that a bug?” he asked.

“What do you think?” said Big Liu.

“I’ll go and have another look.”

The fourth didn’t show; half an hour after the agreed time, a message came saying the internet at home was down. The fifth showed up, and the first thing he asked was “Is your company listed?”

Nine interviews in one week.

On Friday evening, Ye Zhixing sat at the bar counter upstairs at the cat café, wrote the nine names on a sheet of paper, and crossed them out one by one. When he got to the last one, the pen ran dry; he switched to a red one and finished the line.

Big Liu was on the video call holding his baby, who had just finished his milk and was hiccuping with his head on his father’s shoulder.

“Boss Ye, maybe just go with the Hangzhou guy,” he said. “He’s expensive, but he can really do the work.”

“Twenty-five thousand a month,” said Ye Zhixing, “and the mini-game hasn’t made a cent yet.”

“Then the Zhengzhou one. So he searches, who doesn’t search when they code.”

“He searched in the interview.”

“…Fair enough.”

The baby let out a very loud hiccup, and Big Liu patted his back and said “good boy.”

“Actually, the few of us we’ve got now,” said Big Liu, “it’s not like we can’t do it. We’re just slow. I’m writing the game on my own and keeping an eye on the Town Matters back end too, Xiaowen’s training Su Tang, the two street promoters can’t go out right now, so they’re at home drawing and recording sound effects. If we’re really going to run several lines at once, there aren’t enough of us.”

“I know.”

“So what kind of person are you looking for?”

Ye Zhixing thought about it.

“Cheap ones. It doesn’t matter if they’re no good now. The kind who’ll be good after a year of being brought along.”

Big Liu laughed, so hard the baby on his shoulder bounced. “Then you’re not looking for an employee, you’re looking for an apprentice.”

“More or less.”

“Apprentices are hard to find,” said Big Liu. “The ones who can learn don’t need a job, and the ones who need a job can’t necessarily learn.”

Ye Zhixing turned the sheet of crossed-out names over and wrote one word on the back: wait. Having written it, he found it funny himself, scrunched the paper up and threw it into the cardboard box of cat-litter bags under the bar.

## 2. Snow

In mid-March it snowed again.

Not a heavy one. It fell all night, and in the morning there was a thin layer on the ground that turned to black water the moment a car went over it. There was nobody on Back Street. On the copy shop’s roller shutter was a sheet of A4, printed: Closed during the epidemic, for anything urgent please phone. Under the phone number someone had added a small line in ballpoint: what counts as urgent.

In the epidemic section of City Matters, the requests for help were fewer by the day. At the start of the month there had been forty or fifty a day; now there were ten or so, mostly people asking about medicine, about when they could go out, about how to hand in their children’s homework. The supermarkets had opened, and each household could send one person out every two days, with a pass from the neighbourhood committee, for two hours at most.

The last batch of supplies for the estate where Ye Zhixing volunteered went out on Wednesday. The woman from the neighbourhood committee gave every volunteer a certificate of merit, printed on A4, with a red stamp on it and the names handwritten; on his, the “zhi” in Ye Zhixing had been written as a different “zhi,” the one that means “wise.”

He took a photo of the certificate and posted it in the group.

> ye wise-xing hahahahahahaha
> Chen Xi: wise is good, classier than your real name
> Lin Xiaoman: i want a certificate!! our village didnt give out certificates
> Su Tang: comrade ye wise-xing!! thank you for your service!!
> Ye Zhixia: i’ve already stuck it up over the litter box

---

On Chen Xi’s side, business was a little too good.

The online classes had stopped, the homework had been handed out, and nobody could do it. Forty-odd people in a class, forty-odd people facing the same set of papers, and the last long question, no more than three people in the whole class could do it, and one of them was Chen Xi.

His QQ group classes went from one a day to three a day: maths in the morning, physics in the afternoon, questions in the evening. Questions were charged per problem, five yuan each. It wasn’t only Class Seven coming any more; there were people from Class Eight, Class Three, and two from Year Two who got someone to bring them into the group, and the first thing they said was “hello, senior,” and Chen Xi said I’m Year One, and the guy said hello, junior then, and Chen Xi said that’s not right either, call me Mr. Chen.

“Ten yuan a lesson,” Lin Xiaoman worked it out for him in the group, “forty people a lesson, four hundred. Three lessons a day, twelve hundred. Plus the questions.”

“Not that much,” said Chen Xi. “Some people come in and don’t pay.”

“So what do you do?”

“Kick them.”

“…That’s still several hundred a day.”

“Mm.”

“Chen Xi, you’re rich.” Lin Xiaoman sent a kowtowing emoji. “Tens of thousands a month.”

“Not that much,” said Chen Xi. “It stops when school starts.”

“Why stop?”

“Once school starts, who’s going to pay to hear me explain things.”

That was true. But what he didn’t say was something else. Last Wednesday, the physics teacher had posted in the class group, “A number of students have said the homework is quite hard; I can use my spare time to give one-to-one tutoring to students who need it, message me privately for details.” Less than an hour after it went up, the message was withdrawn. The next day, Zheng Xin forwarded a document from the county education bureau to the group, with a very long title, the gist of which was that serving teachers were strictly forbidden from giving paid tutoring in any form, online or offline, and that anyone found doing so would be dealt with severely.

The physics teacher never posted in the group again.

Chen Xi saw the “message withdrawn” notice, saw the document, said nothing, and that evening added another class.

He wasn’t a teacher. The document didn’t apply to him.

---

Lin Xiaoman was still copying.

Biology finished, she was copying chemical equations, five times each this time, a rule she’d made herself. She had filled one physics mistakes notebook and started another. Of the three variations Chen Xi set, she could now get two right; the third she still couldn’t, the third always came at it from some angle she had never seen.

“Stop memorizing,” Chen Xi said on the call. “Think about it: who’s giving this force to whom.”

“I did think.”

“And what did you get?”

“…I couldn’t work it out, so I memorized it.”

Chen Xi went quiet. On the call there was only the sound of his chopsticks tapping a bowl, ting, ting, ting, three times.

“Then write down the places where you can’t work it out,” he said. “Every time you can’t work it out, write one line: which step you got stuck on. When you’ve filled a page, take a photo and send it to me.”

“Do I have to pay for that?”

“No.”

“No discounts for classmates.”

“This isn’t a class,” said Chen Xi. “This is me wanting to know where you get stuck.”

Lin Xiaoman’s end was quiet for a moment, then came the sound of pages turning.

“OK,” she said. “Then I’ll write it.”

## 3. The Phone Call

The call came to the cat café’s landline.

The cat café’s landline number was on the opening-hours sign at the door, and on the shop’s page on Town Matters; normally it didn’t ring twice a month, and when it did it was mostly people asking whether Rice Cake was still there, or whether they could bring their own cat to play. Since the lockdown it hadn’t rung at all.

A little after three that afternoon, it rang. Ye Zhixia was lying across the bar counter doing maths, closest to it, and reached out and answered.

“Hello, Back Street Cats—”

“Where’s your boss?” A woman’s voice, very sharp. “Put your boss on.”

Ye Zhixia looked at her brother and covered the mouthpiece. “It’s for you. Some auntie, really fierce.”

Ye Zhixing took it.

“Speaking.”

“You’re the one called Ye?” said the woman. “Gu Xuerong works for you, right? I’m her mum.”

Ye Zhixing didn’t correct her.

“What can I do for you?”

“Plenty,” said the woman. “She’s a child, working for you, how much are you paying her? Where’s the money? Is it all going to her? She’s a minor, what does she know about money. We’re her guardians, the money should come to us. We’ll keep it safe for her.”

“Her wages,” said Ye Zhixing, “by the regulations can only be paid to her.”

“What regulations?”

“Article Six of the Interim Provisions on Wage Payment,” said Ye Zhixing. Uncle He had told him this a couple of days earlier; he had asked Uncle He to send him the original text and read it twice. “The employer shall pay wages to the worker in person.”

There was a moment’s silence at the other end.

“…And if she can’t collect it?” The woman’s voice changed key. “Don’t the regulations also say that if the person can’t collect it themselves, a relative can collect it for them? I’m her relative.”

Ye Zhixing was a little surprised. She actually knew that clause.

“She can collect it herself,” he said. “And she hasn’t authorized you.”

“She’s a child, what does she know about authorizing—”

“Auntie,” said Ye Zhixing, “she’s sixteen.”

Another voice came down the line, a man’s, some way from the receiver, slurred, too indistinct to make out, but Ye Zhixing had heard that tone before, at the door of that single-storey house in the urban village. The woman said something to the man, then came back to the phone.

“Right, so you won’t pay up,” she said. “I’m telling you, once the lockdown’s over, we’ll come to that shop of yours—”

“You’re welcome,” said Ye Zhixing. “Book ahead before you come. Shop rules: customers can’t pick up the cats without asking.”

He hung up.

Ye Zhixia was still lying across the bar counter, her pen not moving.

“Bro,” she said, “that was Sister Xuerong’s stepmum?”

“Yeah.”

“Will she call again?”

“She will.”

She did call again. Three times the next day, five times the day after. On the third evening it was a different number, the man, who started cursing the moment the call connected and cursed for a full minute and more; Ye Zhixing put the receiver down on the bar counter, went to change the cats’ water bowl, and when he came back the other end was still cursing. He picked it up and said: “Finished? If you’re finished I’m hanging up.”

Afterwards he blocked both numbers.

The landline couldn’t block numbers, so he unplugged it.

Ye Zhixia asked: “Should we tell Sister Xuerong?”

Ye Zhixing thought for a moment. “She knows.”

“How do you know she knows?”

“They called her first,” said Ye Zhixing. “When they couldn’t get through, they called here.”

## 4. Brushing

Your Highness hadn’t been outside all winter, and its fur had grown like a scarf trailing on the floor.

Ye Zhixia dug two combs out of the storeroom, a slicker brush and a steel comb. She handed the slicker brush to her brother and kept the steel comb for herself.

“Use that first to loosen the undercoat,” she said, “then I’ll go through from the head with this. Be gentle, Your Highness doesn’t like pain.”

“Doesn’t like pain?” said Ye Zhixing. “It drew blood on the Devon yesterday.”

“The Devon started it.”

Your Highness lay on Ye Zhixing’s lap, belly up, four feet stuck in the air, with an air of do what you like to me. Ye Zhixing’s first stroke brought out a clump of grey-white down; the draught from the electric heater caught it and stuck it in Ye Zhixia’s hair.

Rice Cake was waiting beside them. It didn’t need combing, but seeing Your Highness get combed, it had to squeeze its way into Ye Zhixia’s arms and butt her hand with its head. Ye Zhixia had no choice but to give it a couple of strokes with the steel comb as well.

Calico appeared from somewhere, jumped onto the back of the sofa, and rubbed against the back of Ye Zhixing’s head from behind.

“They think you’re a cat tree,” said Ye Zhixia.

“I am one.”

Brushing Rice Cake, Ye Zhixia asked as she brushed: “Bro, do you like Sister Xuerong?”

Ye Zhixing’s hands didn’t stop.

“Where did you hear that.”

“I didn’t hear it anywhere, I worked it out,” said Ye Zhixia. “You took her a fridge, you found her a job, her stepmum calls and you have a row with her—”

“I didn’t have a row.”

“You hung up on her.”

“That’s not a row, that’s ending a call.”

Ye Zhixia rolled her eyes. Rice Cake rolled over on her lap and switched to the other side of its belly.

“Between people,” said Ye Zhixing, “it’s not only like or don’t like.”

“Then what is it?”

He picked the clump of down off the slicker, rolled it into a little ball, and squeezed it in his hand.

“When I see her,” he said, “I think of some things from before.”

“That thing three years ago?”

Ye Zhixing didn’t pick that up. He put the little ball of down on Your Highness’s belly; Your Highness glanced at it and didn’t move.

“So when you’re good to her, is that because of her, as a person, or because of that stuff from before?” Ye Zhixia asked.

“I can’t really say. Probably a bit of both,” he said. “When I can’t say what something is, I generally just get on and do it. Do it for long enough and you find out for yourself.”

“Zhixia,” he said, “someone like me, you tell me, do I really have the right to save anyone?”

The comb in Ye Zhixia’s hand stopped.

“…What are you on about,” she said. She bent her head to brush Rice Cake, brushing very hard, and Rice Cake meowed and turned to glare at her. She brushed more gently. “What are you on about. You’ll always be the best brother.”

“That’s a different thing.”

“It’s not a different thing,” said Ye Zhixia. “You just are.”

Ye Zhixing didn’t argue with her.

Your Highness’s belly was done, and he turned it over to do its back. Your Highness wasn’t keen, wriggled a couple of times, and in the end lay down, its tail slapping his thigh, again and again.

“So now you’re out, what are you going to do?” Ye Zhixia asked. “Just live at the shop?”

“First get the local stuff up and running,” said Ye Zhixing. “City Matters, the mini-games, the company’s other lines, whatever can hold, hold it. Give it a couple of years, and once I’ve got something in hand, head for a big city.”

“Which big city?”

“Haven’t decided. Wherever there are the most chances.”

“And me?”

“You go to school.”

“I mean,” said Ye Zhixia, “what can I help with?”

Ye Zhixing glanced at her. There was still Your Highness’s fur stuck in her hair, wisp after wisp, grey-white; she didn’t know.

“You don’t need to help with anything,” he said. “You just be all right. Eat when you should eat, play when you should play, and get into high school. Everything else, your brother’s here. I’ll deal with it. It’ll be fine.”

“There you go again.”

“What?”

“You said that last time too,” said Ye Zhixia. “Three years ago, you said it was fine too. And then you stood outside on your own all night.”

The slicker in Ye Zhixing’s hand stopped on Your Highness’s back.

“…Mum and Dad don’t really like me much either,” said Ye Zhixia. She said it very fast, as if trying to finish before she regretted it. “Dad only cares whether you’re useful, and when you’re not, he yells at you. I’ve never been useful, so he can’t even be bothered to yell at me. Mum just cries, and when she’s done crying she does whatever Dad says. In that house you’re the only one who’s good to me.”

“So?”

“So stop carrying everything on your own,” she said. “I’m fourteen, I can cook, I can do accounts, I can clip cats’ claws. I can help mind the shop, I can help answer Town Matters messages. Can Sister Su Tang keep up on her own? She’s answering till midnight.”

Ye Zhixing looked at her.

After a good while, he reached out and picked the wisp of cat fur out of her hair, picked one wisp, and there was another, and picked again.

“Your hair’s full of Your Highness,” he said.

“…Don’t change the subject.”

“Town Matters,” he said, “I’ll ask Xiaowen tomorrow which part she can hand over to you. Not the help requests, the ones asking about opening hours. An hour a day, after your homework.”

Ye Zhixia’s eyes lit up.

“Is it paid?”

“No,” said Ye Zhixing. “You’re not sixteen, you can’t sign.”

“So I work for nothing?”

“You eat my food and live under my roof.”

“That’s what you’re supposed to do.”

“Then count it as your board.”

Ye Zhixia thought about it, decided she didn’t seem to be losing out, and said nothing more, bending her head to go on brushing Rice Cake. Rice Cake was already asleep, purring very softly.

## 5. Blocks

A little after midnight that night, when Su Tang found Gu Xuerong in MC, she was sitting on the roof of the little wooden house.

The roof sloped, and she was sitting on the ridge, her two blocky legs hanging down one side. In the sky was a square moon. In the woods across the river hung a few bee nests; the two of them had just been over to collect honey, lit a campfire, filled three glass bottles, and Su Tang had drunk all three in one go and announced that it was sweet.

When they got back, Gu Xuerong climbed onto the roof, and Su Tang climbed up too and sat down beside her.

Two block people sitting on the ridge of a roof, looking at a square moon.

“Xuerong.”

“Mm.”

“…Can I ask you some things?”

“Ask.”

For a long time there was no sound from Su Tang. Gu Xuerong thought she had frozen again and was about to speak when Su Tang began, her voice lower than usual, slower than usual, as if moving the words out of her mouth one at a time.

“The braised pork day,” she said. “When you moved the pot up in front of the phone so I could see the colour. Your sleeve caught on the rim of the pot and slid up a bit.”

Gu Xuerong’s block person didn’t move.

“I saw,” said Su Tang. “I didn’t say anything then, I was afraid if I said something you’d turn the video off. But I kept thinking about it afterwards. For days and days.”

“…”

“I want to ask you,” said Su Tang, “why do you… still shut yourself in so tight? Your arm, what happened? Why would you do that? And, later on… have you got any plans?”

She asked it all in one breath, and when she had asked it, sniffed first herself.

Gu Xuerong didn’t answer on voice.

After a while, a line popped up in the chat bar at the bottom left of the screen.

**<Hibernation> who knows**

Another line.

**<Hibernation> have i shut myself in**

**<Hibernation> haha**

**<Hibernation> i know**

**<Hibernation> but i cant get out yet**

Su Tang looked at the lines and made no sound.

The chat bar stayed still for a long time. The moon moved one block west. Across the river, the bees flew back to their nest and crawled in one by one.

**<Hibernation> why do that**

**<Hibernation> maybe i thought about it too**

**<Hibernation> ending all of it**

On voice, Su Tang made a small sound, very short, and clamped it back down herself.

**<Hibernation> plans i never thought about**

**<Hibernation> i think there used to be things i wanted**

**<Hibernation> then they all broke**

**<Hibernation> once theyre broken you stop thinking about them saves trouble**

**<Hibernation> dont look at the arm**

**<Hibernation> its not nice to look at**

**<Hibernation> just leave me be**

That last line stayed in the chat bar, and after ten seconds or so slowly faded and disappeared.

For a long time there was no sound from Su Tang. Gu Xuerong heard a little sniffing, very suppressed, one, then another, and the rustle of a tissue being pulled from its packet.

“…No,” said Su Tang.

Her voice was hoarse, and thick with her nose.

“I won’t leave you be,” she said. “You say leave me be, and I just leave you be? Then all those meals I brought were for nothing. Braised pork lessons for nothing. Honey collected for nothing.”

“You drank the honey.”

“I still collected it!” said Su Tang, and sniffed again. “Xuerong, I don’t know how to say it. My mum says I’m bad with words, I can’t talk properly about serious things, I just chatter. I’m no good at it. But you listen, I’m only saying it once.”

“Mm.”

“Whatever happens,” said Su Tang, “wherever you go later, whatever you do, whatever you turn into, we’re best friends. Forever. Let’s make a promise, OK?”

For a good while there was nothing in the chat bar.

**<Hibernation> what if the promise breaks**

Su Tang was thrown for a moment.

“…Then we promise again!” she said. “If it breaks, we promise again. However many times it breaks, we promise that many times.”

**<Hibernation> then it doesnt count as a promise**

“It counts!” said Su Tang. “If I say it counts, it counts!”

The chat bar was empty again.

The square moon was still moving west. Gu Xuerong’s block person moved on the ridge, turned its head, and looked at Su Tang beside it. A block person’s face is flat; its two eyes were two little black squares, with no expression.

**<Hibernation> ok**

**<Hibernation> promise**

Su Tang let out a sob, and this time didn’t clamp it down, and cried properly. Crying, she said “wait a sec,” “I’m getting a tissue,” “we’re out of tissues,” “I’ll use my brother’s towel,” and then on the call came her brother’s sleepy voice: “Sis, why are you using my towel…”, “Go back to sleep!”

Gu Xuerong listened from her end.

By the time Su Tang came back, her nose was so blocked her voice came out muffled.

“Xuerong,” she said, “your bee.”

“What?”

“Behind you, on the roof, there’s a bee. It followed us back.”

Gu Xuerong turned round. There really was one, a little yellow-and-black-striped block, circling and buzzing over the roof behind her. It must have followed them over after the campfire went out.

“Is it going to sting me?”

“It won’t sting you. It’s just lost,” said Su Tang. “Bees go back to the nest at night, it can’t find its home.”

Gu Xuerong looked at the bee.

“So what do we do.”

“Don’t know,” said Su Tang. “The guide didn’t say.”

The bee circled a few times and landed on the roof, right beside Gu Xuerong’s block person, and stopped flying.

Neither of them did anything about it.

He sent the screenshot to Big Liu.

Big Liu replied with three words:

> taking an apprentice?

Ye Zhixing replied:

> trying him out
