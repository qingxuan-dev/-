# Chapter Eight　Cleaning Duty

## 1. The Rota

Class Seven’s cleaning rota was stuck to the back door of the classroom.

A sheet of A4, the table drawn with a ruler by the class monitor: Monday to Friday across the top, sweeping, mopping, wiping the board, emptying the bins, cleaning the windows down the side, two names in each box. It had been put up with four strips of clear tape, and after a month all four corners had lifted; one, scraped by the back door opening and closing, had gone fuzzy.

Gu Xuerong’s name was in the Wednesday column, paired with Lin Xiaoman: “Sweeping.”

Cleaning duty came in two shifts. The morning one meant arriving at six-twenty and sweeping the classroom before morning reading; the evening one meant staying after evening study to clear up the day’s scraps of paper, chalk dust and snack wrappers, empty the bins, switch off the lights and lock the door.

Gu Xuerong had never once turned up.

She couldn’t get up for the morning shift. She didn’t sleep until two or three at night, sometimes later, and at six-twenty in the morning her body was still sunk somewhere very deep. The alarm by her pillow rang and rang; she reached out and turned it off, her hand dropped, and that was that. She wasn’t there for the evening shift. The moment evening study ended she headed for Back Street, where the second floor’s accounts were waiting for her.

The first two times, Lin Xiaoman swept for her.

Lin Xiaoman took two brooms, one in each hand, and pushed back and forth across the classroom as if rowing a boat. When she had finished she clapped the dust off her hands and, in the Wednesday box on the rota, put a little tick after each of the two names, pressing hard.

The third time, Lin Xiaoman was off sick with a fever.

On Wednesday evening nobody swept Class Seven’s floor. The next morning someone from the discipline office came round to check cleanliness, stood at Class Seven’s back door for a while looking at the heap of chalk ends under the lectern, the sunflower-seed shells on the floor and a trodden-flat milk carton, wrote a few words in a notebook, and docked two points.

And that was how Class Seven lost this month’s rotating red flag.

---

The homeroom teacher was called Sun, and taught politics.

He was forty-five or forty-six, not tall, hair combed back very flat, forehead very shiny. He always wore a white shirt, its tails tucked meticulously into his trousers, and his belt buckle was gold, polished enough to see yourself in. When he spoke he liked to clasp his hands behind his back and lean forward, as if looking down at a document.

He had said many times at class meetings: a class is a collective, and the honour of the collective comes before everything.

He had also, one weekend, called six boys from the class to his newly bought flat to move furniture. From the ground floor to the sixth, no lift, a solid-wood dining table carried by six of them that got stuck three times on the turn of the stairs. When it was done he gave each of them a bottle of mineral water and said this was “social practice” and would count towards their all-round quality assessment.

One of those six boys was Chen Xi.

## 2. The Office

First period on Thursday morning, Mr. Sun stood at the lectern and didn’t teach politics.

He slapped a notice of docked points down on the desk. Not loudly, but with a sharp crack, and two girls in the front row hunched their shoulders.

“Gu Xuerong.”

The room went silent. Every head turned towards the second row by the window.

Gu Xuerong had just lifted her head from her desk. There was a red line pressed into her forehead where the button of her cuff had dug in, a few strands of hair squashed up into tufts, her eyes not yet fully open, sleep still floating in them.

“Stand up.”

She stood up. Her chair legs scraped a long screech across the concrete floor.

“The rota. Wednesday. Your name.” Mr. Sun picked up the notice with two fingers and held it well away from him, as if the paper were dirty. “Since the start of term, four cleaning duties. How many have you turned up to?”

Gu Xuerong said nothing.

“I asked you: how many have you turned up to?”

“…None.”

“None.” Mr. Sun repeated it and nodded, putting the notice down. “Forty-seven people in this class, and something forty-six of them can manage, you can’t. Why? Are you special? Everyone knows your health isn’t good, and everyone made allowances for you during military training. But sweeping a floor—is that going to kill you?”

Someone in the back laughed quietly, then stopped at once.

Gu Xuerong stood there. Her hands hung at her sides, sleeves covering half of each hand, the fingers of her right hand pinching at her left cuff, again and again. She was looking at a small patch of floor in front of the lectern. On it was a piece of chalk, white, broken in two.

Ye Zhixing sat in the row behind her. He couldn’t see her face, only the back of her neck and that tiny black bun. The loose strand of elastic on her hair tie was sticking up again, in a little loop.

His hand clenched inside his desk.

“I’ll make myself clear today.” Mr. Sun clasped his hands behind him and leaned forward. “From today, Class Seven’s cleaning duty is yours alone. For one week, morning and evening, sweeping, mopping, windows, bins, all of it, by yourself. At midday break you’ll also sweep the corridor and our outdoor area. If you can’t do it—”

He let the sentence hang there, waiting for the gap to settle over the room.

“If you can’t, then please have your parents come to the school, so we can discuss whether you’re suited to continue at No. 8 High.”

“Mr. Sun.”

Ye Zhixing had stood up.

Every head in the class swung round to the back. Mr. Sun’s eyes narrowed.

“Ye Zhixing.” His voice dropped, slower than before. “Are you going to tell me again that I don’t have the right?”

Ye Zhixing stood there, his jaw clenched tight. He opened his mouth—

“Don’t.”

The voice came from the front. Gu Xuerong didn’t turn round. She was still looking at the broken chalk.

“Don’t,” she said again, very quietly. “I’ll call him.”

---

Gu Jianguo arrived at ten in the morning.

He had come over from the building-materials market on the north side of town, where he carried cement: fifty-kilo bags, two or three hundred of them a day. He hadn’t had time to change. He wore a white vest washed yellow, a grey long-sleeved work jacket over it with the cuffs rolled to the forearm, the flesh of his forearms in lumps, tanned the colour of soy sauce, veins coiled over them like old tree roots. His trouser legs were covered in grey-white cement stains, and his canvas army shoes had split at the sides and been sewn up with black thread.

He was around fifty, hair cropped so short you could see his scalp, his temples already white. His face was square, with high cheekbones; the corners of his eyes drooped, and the corners of his mouth drooped too, so that he always looked a little aggrieved.

He had his cigarettes out before he even reached the discipline office door, a pack of Hongtashan, opened, seven or eight left inside. He pulled one out and offered it to Mr. Sun with both hands, bending at the waist, bending deep.

“Mr. Sun, Mr. Sun, sorry for the trouble, sorry for the trouble.”

Mr. Sun didn’t take the cigarette and waved it away. Gu Jianguo wasn’t embarrassed in the least. He tucked the cigarette behind his ear and rubbed his hands on his trousers, rubbing off a layer of dust.

“The child doesn’t know any better, she’s caused you trouble.” His voice was low, with a heavy accent, the end of every sentence sinking. “Our family’s not well off, her mother passed early, there’s nobody to look after her, I’m out working all day and haven’t been able to… It’s my fault for not raising her right, my fault.”

When he said “her mother passed early,” Gu Xuerong, standing in the corner of the office, moved her eyes.

Her mother hadn’t passed. Her mother was in the city, living with another man in a flat with a lift. They had a son, who was in Year Two of primary school this year.

She said nothing.

“This cleaning duty, she has to do it.” Mr. Sun sat behind his desk and tapped the desktop with a finger. “This is no small matter. If a person won’t even take on their share for the collective, what will become of them in society? Our school has its limits too. If someone won’t mend their ways, then we advise them to leave.”

“Yes, yes, yes,” said Gu Jianguo, nodding again and again. “She should be punished, she should. However you say to punish her, Mr. Sun.”

“Take her home for today and let her reflect for a day. From tomorrow, as I said, a week of cleaning duty. A three-thousand-character self-criticism, due Monday.”

“Yes, yes, yes.”

Gu Jianguo bowed again. As he bent, he glanced back at Gu Xuerong.

The glance was very short, quickly withdrawn. When he turned back, it was the same drooping, slightly aggrieved, honest man’s face again.

But Gu Xuerong had seen it.

She knew that glance far too well.

## 3. Back Street

From the school to the dormitory, Back Street was the shortest way.

Not yet eleven in the morning, and Back Street wasn’t properly awake. The braised-meat stall wasn’t set up; there was only a damp patch of ground with a few plastic crates turned upside down on it. The mahjong parlour’s door curtain was rolled halfway up, dark inside, and someone was sweeping, the broom scraping the concrete, swish, swish. The copy shop’s glass door was open, the copiers humming. The curtains on the second floor were drawn, forest green, perfectly still.

Gu Jianguo walked ahead with long strides, his soles slapping the ground, slap, slap.

Out of the school gate, across the road, into Back Street, past the copy shop, to the first corner, until the school wall could no longer be seen behind them.

He stopped and turned round.

“You fucking—”

He didn’t shout it. He forced it out through his teeth, kept very low, low as a stone sinking to the bottom of the water.

“I carry three hundred bags of cement a day, carry them till I can’t straighten my back, to earn those few coins to pay for your schooling. And you go and shame me like this at school? Won’t even sweep a floor? Who do you think you are, a princess?”

Gu Xuerong stood in front of him, two steps away.

Her schoolbag was on one shoulder, the strap slipped down to her elbow. She didn’t pull it back up.

“Talk! Gone dumb?”

“You’re just like your mother.” Gu Jianguo’s voice was creeping up, bit by bit. “Ungrateful, the pair of you. Can’t be tamed. Your mother was exactly the same, lazy, useless at everything, and in the end she just walked off and left me with a money-losing girl like you—”

The owner of a breakfast stall across the street poked her head out, looked, and pulled it back in.

“How much have I spent on you? Have you added it up? School fees, living money, you running off to the hospital every few days, drips, medicine, which of those doesn’t cost money?” He took a step forward, a step that brought him very close, so close that his smell of cement and sweat suddenly covered everything. “I’m telling you, if you can’t make it at school, then stop going. If you can’t make it, get married early. Married at eighteen. I’ve already asked around about the bride price. Back home a girl gets at least a hundred and eighty-eight thousand these days. With a body like yours, knock a bit off, a hundred and twenty, thirty thousand at least—”

Gu Xuerong looked at him.

There was no expression on her face. Not the kind of nothing that comes from holding something in, but real nothing, like a wall that everything slides off and nothing sticks to. She watched his mouth open and shut, watched the white froth at the corners of it, watched a vein swell on his forehead, watched the Hongtashan tucked behind his ear, which trembled as he talked and was about to fall.

She was wondering when the cigarette would fall.

She could barely even hear what he was saying any more. She had heard those words many times: on the phone, at the dinner table when she went back for New Year, on the night she was twelve and had a fever of thirty-nine degrees and he got so fed up with her crying that he locked her outside. “Money-losing girl,” “just like your mother,” “can’t be tamed,” “get married”—she had known them by heart for a long time, known them by heart until they weren’t words any more, just sounds, mixed in with the swish of the broom on Back Street, the hum of the copiers, the horn of a tricycle in the distance, a buzzing like rain.

“What are you staring at? You’ve got the nerve to look at me?”

Her right hand was in her sleeve, gripping her left wrist through the fabric, very tightly.

## 4. Chen Xi

“Uncle.”

A voice cut in from the side.

“Pretty loud, yelling at your own daughter in the middle of the street.”

Gu Jianguo’s head whipped round.

Outside the internet café with no sign on Back Street stood someone. Skinny, long neck, school jacket unzipped and hanging open over a black T-shirt with a skull on it. In his hand was a bottle of iced tea, beaded with condensation that was dripping off it.

It was Chen Xi.

He had slipped out in first period. Maths, which he didn’t understand and didn’t want to listen to. He had told his deskmate he was going to the toilet, gone downstairs the moment he was out of the classroom, and climbed the stretch of wall behind the canteen that had no glass on it. He had played games in the internet café for two hours and come out to buy a drink, and as he pushed the door open he heard the yelling outside.

He recognised Gu Xuerong.

He also recognised the words that had just come out of this man’s mouth.

“Who are you?” Gu Jianguo looked him up and down. “What’s it got to do with you?”

“Nothing to do with me.” Chen Xi unscrewed the iced tea and took a swig. “Just annoying to listen to. Uncle, she’s your daughter, not your livestock. At school you’re all bows and scrapes to the teachers, and the moment you’re out the gate you take it out on her. You really know how to pick the soft target, don’t you.”

Gu Jianguo’s face flushed red in an instant.

He looked at Chen Xi, then at Gu Xuerong, then at Chen Xi again, his eyes slowly narrowing. Something surfaced in those drooping eyes, murky and thick.

“Oh—” he drew the word out. “So that’s it. So that’s why she doesn’t do anything proper at school. She’s been carrying on with someone outside.”

He turned to Gu Xuerong and jabbed a finger at Chen Xi:

“This is the one you’re keeping on the side, is it? Eh? So young and already up to no good, exactly like your mother, the minute she sees a man—”

Chen Xi threw his iced tea on the ground.

The bottle bounced once on the concrete and rolled away, red tea spilling all over the ground.

His punch was very fast and very straight, and it landed on Gu Jianguo’s cheekbone.

Gu Jianguo’s head jerked to one side.

Only once.

Then it came back. There wasn’t even much sign of pain on his face, just a red patch on the cheekbone. He looked at Chen Xi the way you’d look at a cat that had pounced on you.

Chen Xi froze for half a second.

Half a second was all it took. Gu Jianguo reached out and grabbed him by the collar, gripping hard, his fist jammed under Chen Xi’s chin, and lifted. Chen Xi was one metre seventy-eight, and he was hoisted until his heels left the ground. He tried to pry the hand off; it was like a vice and didn’t budge.

“You little shit,” said Gu Jianguo. “You dare raise a hand to me?”

His other hand balled into a fist and slammed into Chen Xi’s stomach.

Chen Xi folded up like a shrimp.

There was nothing fancy about that punch; it was just heavy. The heaviness of twenty years of carrying cement, coming up from the waist, through the shoulder, through that vein-coiled arm, all the way into the fist. Chen Xi’s mouth hung open with no sound coming out, and only after two seconds did he drag in a breath, a breath that sounded like cloth tearing.

Gu Jianguo let go of his collar. Chen Xi dropped to his knees, one hand braced on the ground, one hand clutching his stomach, his face grey-green, his forehead against the concrete, in the puddle of iced tea he had just thrown.

Gu Jianguo raised his foot.

“—Abiao!”

Chen Xi yelled it from the ground. His voice was hoarse, with a hint of tears in it, but it was loud, loud enough for the whole of Back Street to hear.

“Abiao! Beanpole! Get the fuck out here!”

The thick plastic curtain of the internet café was flung aside.

---

Four of them came out.

In front was one with yellow hair, dyed like a bundle of autumn straw, an inch of black already grown out at the roots, wearing a basketball vest washed out of shape, with a half-peeled temporary tattoo on his arm: a dragon, only the tail left. He was called Abiao, seventeen, had quit school in the second year of middle school, and now kept order in the internet café for the owner and slept at night on the long sofa at the very back.

Behind him came a tall skinny one, tall as a washing pole, whom everyone called Beanpole. A chubby one with a round face and a silver stud in one ear that glinted in the sun. And a smallest one who looked fourteen or fifteen at most, only up to Abiao’s shoulder, still holding a game controller he hadn’t had time to put down.

None of them were from No. 8 High. Most had stopped school halfway through middle school and drifted around this back street, drifted for two or three years, until they had become part of the street, like the internet café, the braised-meat stall and the crooked telegraph poles.

Abiao glanced at Chen Xi on his knees, then at Gu Jianguo.

He didn’t ask a single thing.

---

The fight was nothing like as good-looking as on TV.

Nobody struck a pose, nobody shouted a slogan. Abiao threw himself at Gu Jianguo from the side and grabbed him round the waist; Beanpole locked one of his arms from behind; Chubby pinned the other; the smallest one hesitated, stuffed the controller in his pocket, and threw himself at one of Gu Jianguo’s legs.

Gu Jianguo was very strong. He shook himself once and Beanpole was thrown back two staggering steps, then lunged in again. He shook again, Chubby’s ear stud got yanked and he yelped with pain, and didn’t let go.

The four of them, bit by bit, pressed him down. Like a few ants dragging a beetle much bigger than themselves.

In the end he went down. On his side, his face against the concrete of Back Street, against the patch where the iced tea had spilled, the red tea soaking half his face.

He struggled underneath them, panting, swearing in ugly words, each one filthier than the last. The four of them held him down. None of them hit him. They just held him down, held him tight.

Chen Xi got up off the ground.

He was still clutching his stomach, his face still grey-green, a trail of spit at the corner of his mouth, or blood; he wiped it off with his sleeve. He walked over and crouched in front of Gu Jianguo, very low, low enough to look that face pressed against the ground in the eye.

“Old man,” he said, breathing hard. “Still putting on a show for me, are you.”

He grinned, a very ugly grin, a bit of red on his teeth.

“I’m the best fighter round here.”

On top of Gu Jianguo’s back, Abiao snorted out a laugh.

“You, the best fighter?” he said. “The way you were on your knees just now, you looked like my gran’s dog.”

“Shut up,” said Chen Xi. “Let me keep a bit of face.”

---

Gu Xuerong hadn’t moved from where she stood.

From the moment her father started cursing her, to Chen Xi’s iced tea smashing on the ground, to her father being pinned down, she had stood at that corner, at that distance of two steps, and not moved once.

Her schoolbag still hung from her elbow, the strap cutting into her sleeve.

She looked at her father’s face pressed against the ground. She had been looking at that face for sixteen years: had seen it flushed red with drink at the dinner table, seen it creased all over with smiles for relatives at New Year, seen its eyebrows lift on the phone as it said the words “bride price.” She had never seen it from this angle. From above, looking down at it pressed into a pool of red iced tea, watching its mouth curse, spit and tea mixing together, watching its drooping eye roll up and find her.

The Hongtashan finally fell from behind his ear, landed on the ground, rolled half a turn, and soaked in the tea, the paper slowly turning brown.

She had been waiting for it to fall the whole time.

Now it had.

---

“That’s enough.” Chen Xi stood up and brushed the dust off his knees. “Let him go.”

The four of them looked at each other, let go one by one, and stepped back two paces.

Gu Jianguo got up off the ground. He got up very slowly: first one arm braced, then his knees, and only then did he stand. His work jacket was covered in dust, half his face was wet, there was a swelling on his cheekbone, but apart from that he didn’t seem to be hurt anywhere. He stood there panting, looking at these young men half a head shorter than him.

He looked at Chen Xi, then at the other four, clenched his fists, and unclenched them. Across the road, the woman from the breakfast stall had poked her head out again, still holding a spatula.

His eyes turned back to Gu Xuerong.

“You just wait.”

His voice was very low, low enough that only she could make it out.

“I don’t believe you’ll never come home.”

He bent down, picked up the soaked cigarette, looked at it, and threw it away. He turned and walked off towards the end of Back Street, still with long strides, his soles slapping the ground, slap, slap, only dragging a little more than when he came, a slight limp in his left foot.

At the end of the street he stopped for a moment without looking back. Then he turned the corner and was gone.

Abiao spat after him, “Ptui,” then looked at Chen Xi. “Who was that?”

“No idea,” said Chen Xi.

“No idea, and you went for him?”

“Didn’t like the look of him.”

Abiao looked at him for a while, then at the girl standing at the corner, said “Ah,” and asked nothing more.

---

Gu Xuerong was still standing in the same place.

Chen Xi walked over and stopped a metre in front of her. The corner of his mouth was split, a thin cut oozing threads of blood; he hadn’t noticed.

“You all right?”

She looked at him.

The way she looked at him gave Chen Xi the creeps a little. It wasn’t fear, it wasn’t gratitude, nor what you’d expect from someone who had had a fright. Her eyes were very level, very still, like a pool where nothing you threw in would make a ripple.

After a few seconds, she spoke.

“You’re bleeding.”

Chen Xi paused, reached up to the corner of his mouth and found a bit of red. “…Oh. It’s nothing.”

She looked down and took a packet of tissues from the side pocket of her schoolbag. The very cheapest kind, fifty fen a packet, a faded peony printed on the wrapper. She pulled one out and held it out to him.

The hand holding out the tissue was trembling.

A very fine tremble, like a leaf in the wind, that you wouldn’t notice unless you looked closely. Chen Xi noticed. When he took the tissue his fingertips touched hers, cold as if just out of the fridge.

He said nothing and pressed the tissue to the corner of his mouth.

“…Thanks.”

## 5. Group Chat

Ye Zhixing was in chemistry.

The chemistry teacher was balancing equations on the board, tapping with the chalk, explaining the gain and loss of electrons in redox reactions. The classroom was stuffy. The windows were open, but there was no breeze, and the plane leaves outside hung perfectly still.

Gu Xuerong’s seat was empty. Her maths book from the morning still lay open on her desk, the scrap of paper with the parabola on top of it, one corner fluttering in the draught from the ceiling fan.

The phone in his desk buzzed.

It was the class group chat. The group was called “Year One Class Seven (No Teachers),” forty-odd people, mostly used for posting homework answers and stickers.

Chen Xi had posted:

> @Ye Zhixing something happened check your DMs

It was followed by a friend request. The verification message was just two words: Chen Xi.

Ye Zhixing looked at the group message for two seconds. People quickly replied in the group: “What happened?” “???” “Chen Xi, what have you done now?” Chen Xi ignored them, and the message was soon pushed up out of sight by the stickers that followed.

He accepted the friend request.

A voice message came through at once. He put an earbud in his right ear, propped the phone upright behind his textbook, held the message down and listened to it all.

Fifty-nine seconds.

Then another. Forty-two seconds.

Then a photo: the corner of Back Street, a pool of red liquid on the ground, a sodden cigarette soaking in it.

Then a line of text:

> we took her to get food dont worry shes ok

Ye Zhixing put the phone face down on his desk.

At the front, the chemistry teacher turned round, looked at the class and said, “This problem. Anyone want to come up and do it?”

Nobody raised a hand.

Ye Zhixing stood up.

The chemistry teacher’s eyes lit up. “Ye Zhixing? Good, come on up—”

“I need the toilet.”

He went out of the back door.

---

The toilets were at the end of the corridor. He didn’t go in. He stood at the window outside them, where a layer of dust had settled on the sill; someone had written “FUCK” in the dust with a finger, and someone else had crossed it out with another.

He rang Uncle He.

“Young master.”

“Uncle He.” His voice was very level, so level it didn’t sound quite like him. “Last month, the homeroom teacher of No. 8 High, Year One Class Seven, had six students come to his place one weekend to move furniture. Sixth floor, no lift.”

There was silence on the line for a while.

“Do you know which estate?”

“No,” he said. “There’s a student called Chen Xi, you can ask him. He should have photos on his phone. They took a group photo after the move, Mr. Sun told them to, said he’d post it on his feed as proof of social practice.”

Another silence.

“All right,” said Uncle He. “I understand.”

“Uncle He.”

“Yes.”

“Don’t tell my dad.”

For a while there was no sound at the other end, only someone warbling away on the opera channel on the TV.

“Young master,” said Uncle He, “if the master knew about something like this, he’d handle it the same way.”

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer. He looked at the crossed-out word on the windowsill.

“…Don’t tell him yet.”

“All right.”

The group exploded.

> ??? for real
> why tho
> is it the furniture thing
> omg someone actually reported it
> who was that brave

Chen Xi posted a sticker in the group: a cat wearing sunglasses.

Ye Zhixing locked his phone, opened his chemistry book, copied the redox equation nobody had done onto scrap paper, and began to balance it.

## 6. Rice Noodles

The clay-pot rice noodle shop was at the other end of Back Street, next to a shoe-repair stall.

The shop was tiny, its frontage only two metres wide. On the glass door, in red cut-out vinyl, it said “Clay-pot rice noodles · Mini hotpot · Pick your own,” half the metal radical of one character missing. When you pushed the door open, a wave of heat smelling of chilli oil and bone broth hit you in the face; if you wore glasses, they fogged up instantly.

Inside were six small square tables, their tops covered in patterned plastic, a few spots burnt through by cigarette ends. On the wall hung an old television showing a costume drama, the volume turned very low; a woman was crying, crying without a sound.

Against the wall stood a row of chiller cabinets, their glass doors coated with white frost. Inside, compartment by compartment, were all kinds of things, each threaded on bamboo skewers, the end of each skewer painted a colour: red one yuan, yellow eighty fen, green fifty fen. Lamb rolls, beef rolls, fish balls, prawn balls, beef balls, pissing beef balls, fish tofu, crab sticks, luncheon meat, baby cabbage, lettuce, tofu skin, dried bean curd sticks, enoki, kelp knots, potato slices, lotus root slices… packed to the brim, the cold leaking out through the gaps in the glass doors and curling round your calves.

The owner was in her forties, wearing an apron printed with a cartoon pig, and the pig was laughing. She was cracking sunflower seeds behind the counter, and when she saw Abiao she didn’t even look up. “You lot again. No tabs today.”

“Who’s asking for a tab,” said Abiao. “Brother Xi’s treating us today.”

“When did I say I was treating?”

“Didn’t you just say you’re the best fighter round here?” said Abiao. “The best fighter not treating everyone, how would that look?”

Chubby and Beanpole doubled up laughing. The smallest one laughed along too, laughing so hard he nearly dropped his game controller into someone else’s pot.

Chen Xi swore, took his wallet out of his pocket, opened it, looked, and shut it again.

---

They pushed two tables together and squeezed six people round them.

Each person had a small aluminium pot, with a little solid-fuel burner beneath it, its blue flame licking the bottom. The broth was the shop’s own, in three kinds: bone broth, tomato, and numbing-spicy. The owner said she fried her spicy base herself, a dozen-plus ingredients: beef tallow, ciba chilli, Sichuan pepper, star anise, black cardamom, bay leaves, and one thing brought from her home village, mountain pepper oil, which nobody else had.

The dipping-sauce bar was beside the chillers, a row of small stainless-steel bowls: minced garlic, coriander, spring onion, bird’s-eye chilli, sesame paste, oyster sauce, sesame oil, black vinegar, fermented tofu juice, crushed peanuts, and a big jar of the owner’s own chilli oil, with a thick layer of crisp-fried chilli flakes and sesame seeds sunk at the bottom, a layer of glossy red oil on top, a few black Sichuan peppercorns floating in it.

Abiao mixed a bowl of dipping sauce for everyone. He mixed with great care: two spoons of garlic, one of coriander, the chilli oil scooped together with the flakes underneath, and finally a ring of black vinegar on top.

“My secret recipe,” he said. “Everyone who tries it says it’s good.”

He set a bowl in front of Gu Xuerong.

“Can you eat spicy?”

Gu Xuerong looked at the bowl of fiery red sauce.

“…Yes.”

“Good,” said Abiao. “Doesn’t matter if you can’t, just dip less.”

---

Gu Xuerong sat at the far end, against the wall.

The little pot in front of her was bone broth; Chen Xi had ordered it for her. When he was choosing skewers he had asked what she wanted, she had said anything, and he had grabbed a handful from the chiller: two skewers of beef rolls, two of fish balls, one of baby cabbage, one of tofu skin, one of lotus root, and finally, after a moment’s thought, one of pissing beef balls.

The bone broth came to the boil, bubbling. A white broth with a few goji berries and two slices of ginger floating on top. She put the beef rolls in and watched them change colour bit by bit in the broth, from pink to greyish-white, their edges curling slightly.

Everyone else had already started eating.

Abiao ate the fiercest, one ball per mouthful, hissing at the heat, and still talking. He told them how last month in the internet café a middle-schooler had called him an “orphan,” and he hadn’t got angry, he had set the kid up on a machine and let him play all night. Beanpole said he actually was an orphan; Abiao said bollocks, my gran’s still alive; Beanpole said your gran brought you food just last week; Abiao said that’s because my gran loves me. Chubby laughed the whole time beside them, ear stud glinting, mouth too full of potato slices to speak.

The smallest one talked least. He kept his head down and ate. He ate fast, as if afraid someone would take it, chopsticks never stopping, cheeks bulging. Halfway through he looked up at Gu Xuerong, then at the few barely touched skewers in her bowl, and asked quietly, “Sis, aren’t you eating?”

Gu Xuerong looked at him.

There was still a bit of dust on his face, rubbed on when he was holding Gu Jianguo down. A patch behind his ear too.

She picked up a fish ball and put it in her mouth.

The fish ball was very hot. She held it in her mouth without chewing and waited for it to cool. The bone broth tasted plain: salty, with a little of the heat of the ginger. She bit into the fish ball; it was hollow, and a small gush of hot broth came out and scalded the tip of her tongue.

She ate one.

Then another.

She tried Abiao’s dipping sauce too. Garlic and vinegar hit first, then the heat, rising very slowly, inch by inch from the back of the tongue, and finally a fragrance she couldn’t name, a little like lemon and a little like fresh grass, that stayed in her mouth a long time.

“Well?” Abiao leaned over to ask.

“…It’s good.”

“Mountain pepper.” Abiao was very pleased with himself. “Told you.”

---

Chen Xi sat opposite her.

The corner of his mouth had stopped bleeding, leaving just a dark red scab. The punch to his stomach still hurt; every time he leaned forward to pick something out of the pot, his brows knitted. He ate very little, a few mouthfuls and then a stop, leaning back in his chair, watching the bone broth bubble.

He didn’t ask her what had just happened.

Nor why her father had cursed her, what those words meant, what was really going on in her family, or what she was going to do now.

He only, when the things in her bowl were nearly gone, got up and went to the chiller and brought back a few more skewers, and put them on her plate. One of luncheon meat, one of crab sticks, one of kelp knots.

“Eat more,” he said. “You’re too skinny, a breeze would blow you away.”

Gu Xuerong looked at the skewers.

“…On the day of military training, you said I had to apply for a fee waiver,” she said. “Lin Xiaoman told me.”

Chen Xi froze.

He opened his mouth, and his face slowly reddened, all the way to the roots of his ears.

“That—” He scratched the back of his head. “That day I was just running my mouth. I was just… talking rubbish. I didn’t know you really were…”

He didn’t finish. He lowered his head and stirred his chopsticks around in the pot, stirring for ages without picking anything up.

“Sorry,” he said.

So quietly it was almost drowned out by the bubbling of the pot.

She reached out with her chopsticks, took a piece of luncheon meat from the skewers he had pushed over, and put it in her mouth.

---

When it came to paying, the six of them had eaten a hundred and thirty-seven yuan’s worth.

Chen Xi took out his wallet. There was a hundred and five yuan in it: one fifty, two twenties, one ten, one five. He took it all out and held it in his hand, and his face went red again.

Abiao fished a crumpled twenty out of his pocket, screwed into a ball, and tossed it on the counter. Beanpole produced ten, Chubby two coins, and the smallest one searched every pocket he had for ages and came up with a handful of ten- and fifty-fen pieces; he counted them: one yuan seventy.

Still a little short.

Gu Xuerong reached into her schoolbag.

“Don’t.” Chen Xi pressed down on her wrist.

It was her left wrist he pressed, through the sleeve of her school jacket. He didn’t press hard, but her whole arm went rigid at once, as if something had stung it. Chen Xi felt it, let go immediately and drew back a little.

“…I said I’m paying,” he said. “Don’t you move.”

Behind the counter, the owner watched this crowd for a while, then gathered the pile of money towards her and counted it.

“A hundred and thirty-two seventy,” she said. “Call it done.”

“You’re knocking off five?”

“Call it done.” The owner picked up her sunflower seeds again. “Bring more people next time. Business is slow.”

## 7. Catching Up on Sleep

Gu Xuerong got back to the dormitory a little after one in the afternoon.

There was nobody in the room. Lin Xiaoman was still in class, and so were the other two girls. The room was so quiet you could hear someone downstairs chopping something, thud, thud, thud, one blow after another, very rhythmic. The four girls’ washing on the balcony swayed slightly in the wind, and its shadows on the floor swayed with it.

Lin Xiaoman’s bunk was a mess, the quilt balled up, an open comic by the pillow, half a packet of crisps lying on its pages.

On the little table by Gu Xuerong’s bed sat the pink insulated lunchbox. On its cloth cover, the rabbits were smiling.

She went over, picked up the lunchbox and opened the lid.

It was empty. Washed very clean, a few drops of water still inside.

Under the lunchbox was a sticky note in Lin Xiaoman’s handwriting, round and bubbly, like a string of bubbles:

> My fever went this lunchtime!! I’ll get your dinner tonight!! What do you want?? (You’re NOT allowed to say anything)

After it she had drawn a crying face, then a smiling face, the two faces side by side as if they were arguing.

Gu Xuerong read the “no saying ‘whatever’” twice.

She peeled it off, folded it in half and put it in the pocket of her school jacket.

Then she lay down.

She didn’t take off her uniform or her shoes. She just lay on her side on the bed, facing the wall. There was a water stain on the wall shaped like a crouching rabbit; she had noticed it long ago and never told anyone.

Her hand went into her sleeve and found her left wrist. It didn’t move; she just rested her hand there, pressed against it.

The sliver of sky outside the window was white. The sun was behind the clouds, and the light was diffuse, coming in through the window onto the floor, onto the edge of the bed, without a single shadow.

She closed her eyes.

Downstairs, the chopping stopped.

She fell asleep.

She didn’t dream.

---

When she woke it was twenty past four in the afternoon.

The light outside had changed colour, from white to a very pale gold, slanting in through the window and cutting across her face. She squinted at the beam for a while, at the dust floating in it, mote by mote, slowly turning.

She got up and washed her face.

The water from the tap was cold. She cupped her hands and splashed her face twice, then left her hands in the water a while longer, until her fingertips were cold right through, before turning it off.

The face in the mirror was still white, but not as white as that morning. The two blue patches under her eyes were still there, but her lips had a little colour now, not much, like a watercolour brush lightly touched to wet paper.

She changed her hair tie and tied her hair up again.

As she went out, she took the folded sticky note out of her pocket, looked at it once more, and put it back.

## 8. The Second Floor

The street lights on Back Street weren’t on yet.

The copier in the copy shop was still running. The owner was sitting on a little stool at the door; seeing her, she smiled and waved. “Xuerong, you’re here. It’s busy up there.”

She went upstairs.

The stairs were wooden, twenty-one in all, and she counted every one. At the fifteenth she smelled pickled cabbage and beef drifting down from above, mixed with a hint of curry.

She pushed the door open.

Su Tang was crouched under the windowsill combing the Ragdoll. The Ragdoll was swearing and Su Tang was swearing back, keeping her voice low: “Your Highness, one more word out of you today and you can forget about treats, Your Highness.” In the open space by the window three middle-schoolers lay on their stomachs with feather wands, the calico turning somersaults between them. Behind the kitchen’s quilted curtain Old Zhou’s griddle was sizzling, and he was humming a song, the same one, still out of tune.

On the grey sofa against the wall sat Ye Zhixing.

On the big screen in front of him was Nightmare Raid’s login screen: the rainy corridor, the half-open door at the end, the bit of red light leaking through the gap. He had a controller in his hands but wasn’t playing, just holding it, looking at the screen.

He heard the door and turned his head.

When he saw it was her, his fingers moved on the controller.

“You’re here.”

“Mm.”

She walked over and sat down beside him. The sofa springs creaked.

He handed her the other controller. She took it; it was warm, from being held for a long time.

Neither of them mentioned what had happened that day.

He didn’t ask what her father had said after he came, didn’t ask what had happened at the corner of Back Street, didn’t ask whether she was all right, didn’t ask whether she was going back to the dormitory tonight. She didn’t ask him what had happened with Mr. Sun, didn’t ask what Chen Xi had told him, didn’t ask where he had gone after he walked out of chemistry that morning.

On the screen, the rainy corridor began to stretch ahead.

“Left or right?” he asked.

“Right.”

“Last time you said right, something dropped from the ceiling.”

“Not this time.”

“How do you know?”

“I know.”

So he went right.

---

In the third round, Su Tang came running over from the windowsill with a receipt in her hand.

“Xuerong, that table just now, two curries and a lemon tea, QR, thirty-six.”

Gu Xuerong put her controller on her knees, picked up the ledger from the arm of the sofa, opened it to today’s page, picked up her pen and wrote it down.

Time, four fifty-one. Two curry beef rice, one lemon tea, QR, thirty-six yuan.

Her writing was still very small, but every stroke was steady. The loop of the 6 was drawn perfectly round.

She put the ledger back on the arm of the sofa and picked up her controller again.

On the screen, Ye Zhixing’s character stood in the middle of the corridor, not moving, waiting for her.

“Why aren’t you going?”

“Waiting for you.”

“…Let’s go.”

The two characters walked on together. At the first corner a hand reached out of the wallpaper; Ye Zhixing dodged right and got past it. He was a little pleased with himself and had just turned his head to say something when the screen went dark—something dropped from the ceiling and swallowed his character whole.

“…Didn’t you say not this time?”

“I said I wouldn’t.”

Her character stood beside the spot where his had fallen, and didn’t go on. She waited for him to respawn.

Ye Zhixing turned to look at her. She was watching the screen, the corner of her mouth turned up a little, a very little, as if something had floated up from under the water, touched the surface lightly, and sunk again.

He looked for a while, then turned back and went on playing.

---

A little after six, Chen Xi sent a message.

> she ate two fish balls a beef ball a piece of luncheon meat a skewer of kelp and half a skewer of beef

Ye Zhixing looked at the message for a while.

He replied:

> thanks

And added:

> put iodine on your lip

Chen Xi sent a sticker: a cat wearing sunglasses.

Ye Zhixing put his phone face down on the arm of the sofa.

Beside him, Gu Xuerong was doing the accounts again. Su Tang called out a sale, and she wrote it down. Outside the window the street lights came on one after another, and the plane leaves under them were so yellow they were translucent. Old Zhou’s griddle was still sizzling, and the Ragdoll was still swearing.

She finished the entry, put the cap on her pen, slid it back into the spine of the ledger, and picked up her controller again.

“Again,” she said.
