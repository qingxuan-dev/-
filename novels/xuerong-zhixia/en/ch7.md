# Chapter Seven　Business

## 1. Odd Jobs

It was on her third weekend visit that Su Tang brought it up.

There were people in the shop all afternoon, not many, three or five at a time, coming and going. Most were middle-schoolers from nearby who were still laughing at the arrow on the cardboard sign as they came in, saying it looked like a cross. Su Tang came earliest. She dropped her canvas bag on the shoe rack by the door, crouched under the windowsill as if she had been doing it for years, took a comb out of her bag and started combing Rice Cake.

She had brought the comb herself: pink plastic handle, two teeth missing, the gap like a missing front tooth. She combed very slowly, from the forehead back along to the tail, pausing after each stroke until Rice Cake’s eyes had narrowed again before making the next. By the time she reached its back, the whole cat had sagged, collapsing into a flat orange pancake, purr rolling in its throat one after another, like a pot about to boil.

She couldn’t bring herself to throw away the fur she combed out. She rolled it into little balls and set them on the floor beside her. By the end of the afternoon she had five or six in a row, all about the same size, like a row of uncooked rice dumplings.

Ye Zhixing sat behind the counter and watched her all afternoon.

He noticed that Su Tang working was not the same as Su Tang talking. Talking, she was a gust of wind, sentences tumbling out in strings without a breath between them. Working, she was very quiet, lips pressed together, shoulders low, her hands as gentle as if she were unwrapping something that mustn’t rustle. When a little boy reached out to tug the calico’s tail, she didn’t get up and didn’t shout. She just held the comb out to one side and said, “Could you hold this for me? My hand’s tired.” The boy took the comb and forgot all about the calico’s tail.

Towards five, the customers had all gone. Su Tang gathered the row of fur balls into a tissue, wrapped them up, dropped them in the bin, stood up and rubbed her palms on her jeans.

She came over to the counter and didn’t speak right away.

Her fingers hooked the strap of her canvas bag, let go, hooked it again. The badges pinned to the bag knocked lightly against each other, tick, tick.

“Can I ask you something?”

“Go on.”

“Do you… need anyone?”

Ye Zhixing was taken aback.

“I can work,” said Su Tang. She was speaking far more slowly than usual, each word set down in her head first and checked before it came out. “Sweeping, feeding the cats, combing, washing up, anything. I’m free after school, till nine even. Weekends too.” She paused, and her voice dropped. “…It doesn’t have to be much money.”

Once that last part was out she seemed to feel it hadn’t come out right herself. Her face slowly reddened, right to the roots of her ears. She looked down at the list of cats’ names pressed under the glass of the counter.

“How old are you?”

It was Uncle He who asked.

He had just come up with this week’s cat food, a bag in each hand, and was standing in the doorway with a plane leaf on the shoulder of his jacket. There was no telling how long he had been listening.

Su Tang turned round and her back went straight, like a pupil called on by the teacher.

“Sixteen. Just turned, last month.”

“Do you have your ID card?”

She took it out of the innermost pocket of her canvas bag. As she unzipped the pocket, Ye Zhixing saw the cat sticker lying inside, his sticky note still on the back—“Su Tang, sticker, twelve yuan”—its corners already curling a little.

She handed the card over with both hands. Uncle He took it, looked closely at the date of birth, then raised his eyes to her. He looked at her a little longer than he had looked at the card, eyes slightly narrowed, with that smile of his that gave nothing away.

“Does your family know?”

“They do,” said Su Tang. “I told my mum. She said as long as it doesn’t get in the way of school, and it’s not somewhere like an internet café, and I’m not out too late, anything’s fine.” She paused and added, “She said if you’re worried, you can ring her.”

She reeled off a number, fluently.

Uncle He wrote it on the back of his hand with his pen, gave her back the card and turned to Ye Zhixing.

Ye Zhixing was thinking.

Not about whether to take her on; he had decided that before she even asked. He was thinking about how much to pay.

He suddenly realised he didn’t know. A sixteen-year-old, working three hours in the evening: what was an hour worth? His sense of money had always had a gap in it. At one end was pocket money, at the other the string of figures in the company account that he rarely opened to look at properly, and the stretch in between, the part ordinary people dealt with every day, he had never really touched. He knew what a graphics card cost, what a planted aquascape cost, but not what a bowl of noodles cost in the county town, or what an hour’s work was worth there.

“How much do you want?”

Su Tang opened her mouth and named a figure.

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer straight away. He looked over towards the sofa.

Gu Xuerong was sitting at the end of the sofa with the ledger open on her knees, pressed together, copying in the day’s takings. Her head was down, her pen never stopping, half her face hidden by her hair. She looked as if she weren’t listening at all.

After a few seconds she spoke, eyes still on the ledger:

“The evening shift at the bubble-tea shops in town is twelve an hour.”

The figure Su Tang had named was eight.

The room went quiet for a moment. Su Tang’s face went redder still, hot, and she lowered her head, her fingers twisting the bag strap into a knot.

“Fifteen, then,” said Ye Zhixing.

Su Tang’s head snapped up. “…Fifteen?”

“Lots of cats, hard work,” he said. “And you’ll get scratched.”

Su Tang stood at the counter and looked at Ye Zhixing, then at Gu Xuerong on the sofa. Gu Xuerong had already turned to the next page, as if she hadn’t said a word. Su Tang’s mouth opened, closed, opened again, and in the end nothing came out; she just bowed to each of them. The first bow was too deep, and her bun nearly hit the edge of the counter. The second was for Gu Xuerong, who didn’t look up, so she bowed to the top of Gu Xuerong’s head.

“That’s settled, then,” said Uncle He. “I’ll write up a simple agreement and you can take it home for your mum to sign. You leave before nine, no exceptions. I’ll have Uncle Chen drop you on his way.”

“There’s no need, I live just across the river, it’s ten minutes’ walk—”

“After nine there are no lights along that stretch by the river,” said Uncle He. “He’ll drop you.”

He put down the cat food, brushed the leaf off his shoulder and went downstairs.

Su Tang stayed where she was. After a good while she turned, went back under the windowsill, crouched down and picked up the comb again. Rice Cake had fallen asleep. She said something very quietly to the sleeping cat. Ye Zhixing didn’t catch it; it sounded like, “I’m going to be here every day now.”

---

Nobody sat down to divide up the work. After a few days it simply fell into place.

Su Tang looked after the customers. She greeted them, poured water, explained to newcomers what the writing on the wall meant, and whenever she saw someone reach to pick up a cat she would go over, all smiles, crouch beside them, hold out her own hand for the cat to sniff first and then say, “He doesn’t really want to be picked up right now. See how his ears are turned back?” She never made anyone feel awkward when she stopped them. Often the person she had stopped would crouch down with her and wait for the cat to come over on its own.

Gu Xuerong looked after the accounts.

She never offered any opinion on the arrangement. When Ye Zhixing asked her if it was all right, she said yes. When he asked how her pay should be worked out, she said you told me before, I set my own hourly rate. When he asked what rate she had set, she said she hadn’t decided yet.

She never did decide. And Ye Zhixing never asked again.

## 2. Ninth Period

No. 8 High had ten lessons a day.

Forty-five minutes each. First period started at seven-twenty in the morning, tenth period ended at five-forty in the afternoon, and lunch and the midday rest added up to an hour and a half. Then evening study, seven to nine. The timetable was stuck on the back wall of the classroom, a sheet of A3 packed so tightly with boxes that one look made you want to lie down.

Ninth and tenth period were marked on the timetable as “Self-study.”

For the first two weeks of term everyone sat there obediently. By the end of September, as soon as the bell went for ninth period, people began leaking out of the back door. At first one or two, bent double, creeping along the wall and only straightening up to run when they reached the stairs; then three or five at a time, not even bothering with their bags, phones in pockets and gone; then some packed up at the end of eighth period, and when the bell went they stood up and walked out through the front door, as unbothered as if they were going to the toilet.

The head of year caught them in the corridor twice, collared a whole string of them, made them stand outside and write eight-hundred-character self-criticisms. The day after the self-criticisms were handed in, they walked out as usual. After that the head of year didn’t bother much. He sat in his office marking homework until five, his face grey, the bags under his eyes hanging down to his cheekbones.

Ye Zhixing was in the first batch to go. He didn’t crouch or hug the wall when he left. He walked out of the back door with his bag on, and nodded to the student on duty as he passed. The duty student knew who he was and didn’t dare stop him.

Where he went, at first nobody knew.

Later someone on Back Street saw him push open the door beside the copy shop and go upstairs. After that, once ninth period started, the wooden staircase often went clunk, clunk.

The people who came were never many, but they never stopped. Three, five, group after group. Girls who came together to fuss over the cats, dropping their bags at the foot of the sofa and lying flat on the floor; two boys who came to play games, round after round, until it was dark and they remembered evening study; a girl in glasses who always came alone, ordered something to drink and sat at the little round table by the window doing her homework, writing a bit, looking up at Briquette on the cat tree, writing a bit more. She never touched the cats and the cats didn’t much touch her, but she always stayed a full hour before she left.

The copy-shop owner downstairs noticed that before going up, the students sometimes stopped in to print a piece of homework or copy a test paper. She put a little whiteboard by her door and wrote on it in big letters: “Homework printing 50 fen a page.”

By the end of the month she smiled whenever she saw Ye Zhixing, layers of creases at the corners of her eyes, and once she pressed a bag of her family’s home-dried persimmons on him.

## 3. The Kitchen

At the start of October, two days before National Day, the food licence came through.

Uncle He went to collect it. When he got back he put the certificate in a plain wooden frame and hung it on the wall behind the counter. Once it was up he stepped back two paces to look, then stepped forward and nudged the frame half a centimetre to the left, then stepped back again.

“We can sell food now,” he said. “What would the young master like to sell?”

What Ye Zhixing had originally had in mind was drinks. Milk tea, juice, a few little cakes, much like the cat cafés in the city.

But after a month sitting behind the counter, he had seen clearly. Most of the people who ran over after ninth period hadn’t had dinner. They stayed an hour, two hours, and evening study started at seven, so there was no time to get to the canteen in between. One would pull a squashed bun out of their schoolbag, one would tear open a packet of spicy gluten strips, and some brought nothing at all, held a cat, and while holding it their stomach would growl, and they would be the first to laugh, embarrassed.

“Meals,” he said. “Drinks too.”

Uncle He looked at him for a while. “Who’s going to cook?”

---

The cook was recommended by the copy-shop owner.

It was her husband, Old Zhou.

Old Zhou was in his early fifties: short, stocky, short-necked, with palms as thick as two chopping boards. For more than ten years he had run a fast-food place opposite the county bus station; then the station moved out to the new district, the street outside his door emptied overnight, he held on for half a year and closed. For the two years since he had stayed in the little room behind the copy shop, watching war dramas every day, falling asleep halfway through and picking up where he left off when he woke. His wife said that if he stayed idle like that any longer, idleness would ruin him.

The first time he came up to the second floor, he came up in slippers.

He stood in the doorway without coming in. He looked at the roomful of cats, then at the students on the sofa, and finally let his eyes come to rest on Ye Zhixing, looking him over from head to foot, pausing at the white sneakers he had scuffed on purpose.

“What do you want me to make?”

“What students like,” said Ye Zhixing. “Quick to serve, not too expensive, filling.”

Old Zhou said nothing. With his hands behind his back he took a turn through the inner room, stopped at the door of the small room at the back, put his head in, then looked up at the ceiling.

“This one. Turn it into a kitchen for me. Needs ventilation, needs an extractor. Cats can’t come in.”

“Okay.”

“The stove has to be good. Big flame.”

“Okay.”

“I’ve got my own griddle plates,” he said. “Used them more than ten years. Better than anything you’d buy.”

A week later the small back room was done. A thick quilted curtain hung over the doorway, and on it was a handwritten sign: “NO CATS ALLOWED!!” Su Tang had written it, two exclamation marks, with a cat drawn beside it, very fat, one ear bigger than the other, so it looked like a pig.

Old Zhou wrote the menu on a little blackboard by the door. His chalk handwriting was unexpectedly good, every stroke neat, horizontals level and verticals straight, like someone who had spent half his life writing price lists in restaurants:

> Sizzling Pickled Cabbage & Beef Rice　16
> Sizzling Black Pepper Beef Rice　18
> Curry Beef Rice　15
> Tomato Beef Rice　15
> Taiwanese Braised Pork Rice　13
> Mushroom & Chicken Rice　14
> Silky Egg & Shrimp Rice　18
> Omelette Rice　14
> Yangzhou Fried Rice　12
> Extra egg　2
>
> Lemon Tea　6
> Iced Yuanyang　7
> Sour Plum Drink　5
> Fresh Milk Tea　8
> Hot Cocoa　8

Ye Zhixing stood in front of the blackboard for a long time.

“Why so much beef?”

“Sliced beef’s easy to do, quick, and students love it.” Old Zhou wiped his hands on his apron. “Eat first, then talk.”

He ate. The sizzling pickled cabbage and beef came first.

When the iron plate was set down it was still crackling, a ring of tiny oil sparks jumping around its edge. Underneath was a layer of pickled cabbage, stir-fried right through, gone golden and a little charred at the edges, its sourness rising sharply enough to make your back teeth clench. The rolls of sliced beef were laid over the cabbage, cut so thin the light came through, and in the heat of the plate their edges curled up bit by bit, juices running down into the cabbage, then following the curve of the plate to the other side, under the bowl of white rice upturned on the corner of the plate. On top lay a fried egg with a runny yolk, its white crisp and lacy at the edge. One poke with the chopsticks and the yolk ran out, golden, slowly, covering a slice of beef.

He finished the whole portion, asked for another bowl of plain rice, stirred the sauce left on the plate into it and ate it clean, and finally went at the charred layer of cabbage on the plate with a spoon, scraping until the iron squeaked.

Old Zhou stood by the curtain watching him scrape. He said nothing. The corners of his mouth turned down, but his eyes had a bit of a light in them.

Ye Zhixing put down his spoon.

“Buy a meal, get a cat treat free,” he said.

Old Zhou glanced at him and snorted, but didn’t object. He turned and went back into the kitchen. The curtain swayed behind him for quite a while.

---

Once the meals went on sale, the smell of the second floor changed.

Before, it had been cat food, new wood and a faint hint of litter. Now there was pickled cabbage, curry, black pepper and caramelised sugar too, wafting out from behind the quilted curtain wave after wave, into the front room, out of the window, drifting along Back Street, past the plane trees, as far as the braised-meat stall. The stall owner sniffed, swore under his breath and fanned harder.

Business was almost absurdly good, but never quite chaotic.

From a little after four every afternoon, three or five people at a time, a group in and a group out. Those eating sat at the little tables along the wall, those fussing over cats in the open space by the window, those gaming under the big screen, none of them in each other’s way. At mealtimes Old Zhou’s griddle plates came out one after another, the whole room filling with their sizzle as they were carried through, and the cats would all lift their heads at once and look towards the kitchen.

Some people ordered delivery on their phones too. Not many, three or five orders a day, mostly middle-schoolers from the nearby estates who had a Yangzhou fried rice sent to the school gate before evening study. Su Tang did the deliveries on a borrowed electric scooter with a foam box strapped to the back, and before setting off she would press each lid down twice, carefully.

“Do you know the way?” Ye Zhixing asked her.

“I grew up round here.” Su Tang put on the helmet. It was a size too big and tilted to one side once fastened. “I could do it with my eyes closed.”

“Don’t close your eyes.”

“…It’s a figure of speech!”

## 4. The Whiteboard

Everyone had a cat treat in hand, and everyone wanted to feed a cat.

So the cats entered the best days of their lives, and the most dangerous.

Rice Cake was the first to have a problem. It had always been an actor, and now at last it had a stage. Every time someone came in holding a treat, it would drop silently from the windowsill, go to meet them, sit itself neatly at their feet, two white paws together, tail curled round to cover its toes, face tilted up, eyes opened wide and round, perfectly still. It looked as if nobody had fed it for three days, as if the whole world owed it something.

In one afternoon it could con a dozen or more treats.

A week later Uncle He took it for a check-up. The vet put it on the scales, looked at the number, looked again, and looked up at Uncle He:

“What have you been feeding it?”

When they got back, there was a whiteboard beside the counter.

On it was a chart, one row per cat, a row of little boxes after each name. When a cat ate a treat, it got a tick in its row; three ticks a day at most, and once it was full, no more. At the very front of Rice Cake’s row Su Tang had written in red marker: DIET. And after it, a crying face.

The rule was set. Whether it could be enforced was all down to Su Tang.

Su Tang stood by the windowsill with a whiteboard marker gripped in her hand, like a goalkeeper. When someone with a treat headed for Rice Cake, she would intercept them with a smile, bend down and lower her voice as if letting them in on a secret: “Rice Cake’s full for today. Why don’t you go and feed the calico? She’s got two boxes left, she’s really cute too, she holds the food with her paws when she eats.”

The person she had stopped would usually be disappointed for a second, then really would turn to find the calico. The calico refused nobody, and when she ate, she did indeed hold the treat with her paws.

Rice Cake sat on the windowsill watching treat after treat pass in front of it into other cats’ mouths. Its ears slowly drooped. It turned round, back to the whole room, and the tip of its tail tapped on the sill, once, once, very slowly.

---

As well as guarding the treats, Su Tang groomed and bathed the cats.

Uncle He later switched to three self-cleaning litter boxes, round and fat, like three white space capsules. When a cat had finished, the box slowly turned itself once and sifted the clumped litter into a drawer underneath; you only had to empty it once a day. It saved a lot of work. But grooming and baths, no machine could do.

Grooming was every evening.

Su Tang sat under the windowsill with her back to the wall and did them one by one. The shorthairs were easy, a few strokes and done. The long-haired Ragdoll took the most work: a day without combing and its fur knotted, behind the ears and under the legs, and while she combed it swore from start to finish, even worse than the day it arrived. Su Tang would comb and quietly swear back at it: “Your Highness, go on, swear again. Your Highness, you’re not sleeping tonight. Your Highness, look at the state of your fur, and you call yourself Your Highness.” As the swearing went on, the Ragdoll’s voice got smaller, until all that was left was a little gurgle in its throat, eyes half closed, the whole cat gone soft on her lap. Her own voice softened along with it and turned into humming.

Baths were at weekends. Eleven cats, three or four each weekend, taking turns.

In the back room a space was kept for a big plastic tub, a hairdryer and a pile of old towels. When Su Tang bathed a cat she pushed her sleeves up to the elbow, tightened her bun, and took a deep breath, like someone about to dive in to save a drowning person.

The Devon was the most afraid of water. The moment it went in the tub, it climbed up the person holding it, all four paws hooked into her T-shirt, all the way up to her shoulder, where it clung dripping and wouldn’t come down, big ears flattened to its head, crying like a little lamb suffering the greatest injustice in the world. Su Tang didn’t pull it off. She let it stay there, holding its bottom up with one hand, the shower head in the other, rinsing its back a little at a time, talking to it the whole time: “There, there, nearly done. See, it doesn’t hurt, you’re just a bit wet, a bit wet won’t kill you.”

When it was done she came out of the back room with a towel-wrapped Devon over her shoulder, like wearing a scarf that cried. Her arms had a criss-cross of new red lines on them, thin ones, two of them beaded with a little blood.

Ye Zhixing saw, dug some iodine swabs out from under the counter and held them out.

“Here.”

“It’s fine, they’re not deep.”

“Put it on.”

She took them and sat on the arm of the sofa, the Devon still shivering in the towel on her shoulder. With one hand she held the Devon, with the other she dabbed at her arm with a swab, dab, hiss, and after each hiss she smiled at the Devon: “Look how you’ve scratched me.”

At the end of the month, when he paid her, Ye Zhixing gave her a fair bit more than they had agreed.

Su Tang held the envelope, standing at the counter. She took the money out and counted it, then counted it again. The second time, her fingers slowed.

“There’s too much.”

“Baths are extra,” said Ye Zhixing.

“We never said baths were extra.”

“Now we have.”

Su Tang looked down at the money in her hand for a long time. The old phone on the counter lit up and went dark again.

She folded the money, slid it back into the envelope, put the envelope in the innermost pocket of her canvas bag, next to the cat sticker, zipped it up, and pressed it with her hand to make sure it was shut.

This time she didn’t bow. She looked up at Ye Zhixing. Her eyes were a little wet, but she didn’t let them get any wetter, and she said, very seriously, one word at a time:

“Thank you.”

Then she turned round and went to groom the Ragdoll. The Ragdoll saw her and started swearing again.

## 5. The Accounts

More customers meant more accounts.

For the first few days, Gu Xuerong kept them in great detail. Every transaction went down: the time, the number of people, what was sold, how much was taken, cash or QR code, split into columns ruled with a ruler. Her writing was very small, line after line, neat as ants marching in columns. Sister Mei looked and said, this is more detailed than what I did at the shopping centre.

But once the meals went on sale, it stopped working.

In one afternoon, thirty or forty people came and went. People buying meals, buying drinks, buying standees, buying a blind box, not liking what was inside and coming back for another; packet after packet of cat treats going out. Su Tang would call from the front room, “One pickled cabbage beef with extra egg, QR,” and Gu Xuerong would write it down; “Two lemon teas, cash,” and she would write that down. When the calls came faster, her pen couldn’t keep up. It would stop on the paper, waiting, and by the time Su Tang called the next one she had forgotten what the last one was.

She was also getting more and more tired in the daytime.

She was the kind of person who couldn’t sleep at night. Ye Zhixing had worked this out gradually from the times she replied to messages: if he sent her something about the game at past one in the morning, she replied in three seconds; if he sent one at seven in the morning, she didn’t see it until the end of second period. In lessons she spent longer and longer with her head on her desk, and didn’t move when the lesson ended either, forehead against her arm, her right hand gripping her left cuff very tightly, as if she were asleep but her hand wasn’t.

Ye Zhixing poked the back of her chair with his pen cap. One poke, she didn’t move; two, she didn’t move; on the third she slowly sat up, turned round and looked at him. There was a mist over her eyes, as if she had just swum up from somewhere very far and very deep and hadn’t quite broken the surface yet.

“Want to play?”

She looked at him for a while.

“…You play,” she said. “I’ll watch.”

She had never said “I’ll watch” before.

---

One evening Sister Mei came to go over the accounts. After half an hour she looked up from the ledger.

“Xuerong, love, this—” She turned the ledger round and pointed at a line with a finger painted in pale pink polish. “This cat treat column says thirty-eight. Thirty-eight packets?”

Gu Xuerong leaned over to look. She looked for a long time, so long that Sister Mei thought she hadn’t heard.

“…Thirty-eight yuan.”

“So the money’s gone into the quantity column.” Sister Mei turned back a page. “Here too. And here, this lemon tea’s been entered twice.”

“Mm.”

“That’s the third one today,” Sister Mei said. Her tone wasn’t harsh. If anything it was coaxing, the way you might tell a child at home, look, your shoelace has come undone again.

“Mm.”

Gu Xuerong took the ledger, crossed out the entries and rewrote them alongside. The hand she wrote with was trembling slightly, a very fine tremble you wouldn’t see unless you looked closely, but the numbers came out crooked. She stared at the crooked 8, and as she stared it seemed to turn into a shape she didn’t recognise. She crossed it out and wrote it again. Still crooked.

She just sat there, the tip of her pen pressed to the paper, pressing out a small dot of ink that slowly spread.

Ye Zhixing sat on the sofa beside her, phone held up in front of him, a game on the screen. He hadn’t pressed anything for a long time.

He wanted to say: why don’t you leave the accounts, let Sister Mei do them.

The words got as far as his lips, and he remembered the last page of her scrap pad. On it was one line, copied out stroke by stroke that day on the second floor: “As long as it’s clear, that’s fine.” Copied so carefully, so hard, that it stood out in relief on the back of the page.

So he didn’t say it.

He put his phone down, got up, went behind the counter and poured a cup of hot cocoa, and set it on the arm of the sofa by her hand. The cup was hot; he put a tissue under it as he set it down.

“Old Zhou made too much,” he said. “If you don’t drink it, it gets poured away.”

Gu Xuerong glanced at the cocoa and didn’t move. Only after he had walked away, and Sister Mei had bent her head to sort the till receipts, did she reach out and pick up the cup in both hands and hold it against her chest. She didn’t drink it. She just held it, letting the steam creep up over her face little by little.

---

A few days later, in the early evening, when the shop was busy.

Gu Xuerong was bringing a parcel up from downstairs. A small cardboard box with a new batch of scratching boards in it, not heavy. She carried it up the stairs, and halfway up, she stopped.

One hand gripped the rail, the other arm held the box against her side, and she stood there, going neither up nor down. The stairs were wooden, worn into hollows by decades of feet, and she was standing in one of the hollows. Two girls squeezed past her, chatting and laughing on their way up; one of their schoolbags knocked her shoulder, and she swayed. The hand on the rail tightened, and her knuckles went white at once.

Her face was white too. Not her usual white: a sinking kind of white, as if something were draining out of her face bit by bit. Her lips had lost their colour, and a film of very fine sweat had come out on her forehead.

Su Tang happened to be coming down to fetch delivery bags. At the foot of the stairs she looked up and saw.

She didn’t shout, and she didn’t run. She was up in a few strides. With one hand she took the box from under Gu Xuerong’s arm and put it on a step; the other hand was already in her own canvas bag, rummaging, and came out with a sweet. The cheapest kind of fruit boiled sweet, the kind you get a whole handful of for a yuan, orange flavour, a ring of little oranges printed on the clear wrapper.

She tore the wrapper open with her teeth, put the sweet in Gu Xuerong’s palm and closed her fingers over it.

“Keep it in your mouth,” said Su Tang. “Don’t chew. Let it melt slowly.”

Gu Xuerong looked down at the sweet in her hand. Orange, translucent, lit by the stairwell light like a small piece of solidified light. She looked at it for a few seconds as if she didn’t quite understand what it was, and then, slowly, she put it in her mouth.

Su Tang helped her sit down on the step.

She didn’t leave. She crouched on the step below, her back to Gu Xuerong, facing the bottom of the stairs, as if she were waiting for someone. When people came up she lifted her face and greeted them, “Welcome, it’s upstairs on the left,” her voice as bright as ever. Her back hid most of Gu Xuerong, so that from the bottom of the stairs all you could see was a bun and an ordinary girl crouching down.

The sweet melted slowly in her mouth, so sweet it was almost bitter.

After five or six minutes, some of the colour had come back into Gu Xuerong’s face. She pulled herself up by the rail and bent to pick up the box on the step.

Su Tang got there first and picked it up.

“I’ll carry it. You go ahead.”

Gu Xuerong looked at her. Su Tang smiled at her, eyes curving into two slits, and asked nothing.

---

That night, after closing, Ye Zhixing asked Su Tang at the door:

“You always carry sweets in your bag?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“My name’s Tang, isn’t it? Sweets.” Su Tang laughed first at her own joke, twice, then suddenly stopped. She looked down at the stairs and scuffed the toe of her shoe on the worn shiny patch of the step.

“…My grandma used to be like that,” she said. “She’d be standing there and then she just wouldn’t move, face white as paper, and wouldn’t answer when you spoke to her. When I was little it made me cry, lots of times. After that I carried sweets every day, and when she looked wrong I’d pop one in her mouth.”

She was quiet for a moment.

“Then it became a habit. After she was gone, I still carried them. I can’t stop.”

Ye Zhixing stood by the door frame and didn’t reply. Downstairs, the copy shop’s shutter was half down, and a line of light came up from underneath it onto Su Tang’s sneakers.

“She needs to eat more.” Su Tang shouldered her bag, went down two steps, then turned back. “…I mean Xuerong.”

## 6. The Cat Boss

The fame started with a video.

It was filmed by a girl in her last year of middle school who had come with her mother on a weekend to eat, and happened to catch Rice Cake performing. She held her phone up at the windowsill and recorded forty seconds: Rice Cake sitting perfectly upright, face tilted up at a boy holding a treat, eyes round as two glass marbles; Su Tang lunging in from the left of the frame, pointing with her whiteboard marker—“Rice Cake’s full for today!”; Rice Cake’s ears drooping at once, and then, slowly, slowly, the cat turning round, giving the camera its back, the tip of its tail tapping on the sill, once, once, very slowly, very mournfully.

The caption on the video read: The saddest cat in the county, put on a forced diet.

In two days it had passed half a million views.

In the comments people asked where it was, people posted “Hang in there Rice Cake,” people said “the girl stopping the cat is so fierce and so cute.” Someone recognised the boy behind the counter at the edge of the frame. Only half his face was in the shot, in profile, head down, his school collar hanging loose, an order slip in his hand. The commenter wrote underneath: Isn’t that the Ye kid from No. 8 High?

After that, more and more people recognised him.

Everyone in the county town got to know that there was a cat café on Back Street, the cats put on shows, the food was really good, and the owner was a high school student whose family was in building materials. At weekends parents began bringing their children; people rode electric scooters over from the next town; university students home for the holidays came specially to film Rice Cake. People still came in groups, three or five, seven or eight, never enough to burst the door, but from afternoon to night the wooden staircase was hardly ever quiet.

---

An editor from a county WeChat account rang to say she wanted to do a feature. She already had the headline: “County Town Sixteen-Year-Old Opens Cat Café, Earns… a Month.” She said the figure could be filled in after the interview.

Ye Zhixing listened to her read out the headline on the phone.

“No.”

“No, no, wait, Ye, listen to me, this is great publicity for your shop, our readership—”

“You can interview the cats,” he said. “Rice Cake’s the most photogenic.”

He hung up.

From then on, whenever a customer raised a phone camera towards the counter, he would quite naturally turn sideways, or lower his head, open a drawer and look for something. The movements were smooth, smooth as a habit practised many times, so that it just looked as if he happened to be busy.

Once, a boy who looked like a university student walked straight up to the counter with his phone out, the lens almost in his face, and asked, grinning, “Boss, can I get a photo with you? You’re kind of famous now.”

Ye Zhixing stood there and didn’t move.

For those two seconds there was nothing on his face. Not displeasure, not nerves, just blank, like a screen whose plug has suddenly been pulled.

Then he reached out and gently pushed the boy’s phone aside, towards the windowsill.

“Take one with him,” he said. “He’s the boss.”

The boy paused, turned and saw Rice Cake on the windowsill, laughed, and went over to take the picture.

Ye Zhixing turned and went into the back, and stood for a while outside the kitchen’s quilted curtain. Behind it the griddle sizzled, and Old Zhou was humming a very old song, badly out of tune, one line taking three turns and never finding its way back.

He stood outside the curtain and listened to most of the song.

---

At school the wind had changed.

At the start of term he had been “that Ye kid from the building-materials family who skipped and got a demerit”; now he was “the boss of that cat café on Back Street.” People said hello to him in the corridor. Girls from other classes who passed him on the stairs whispered to their friends, laughed together, and then looked back.

Chen Xi came over between classes and slung an arm over the back of his chair, his tone nothing like it had been on that day at military training:

“Brother Ye. Boss Ye. Seeing as we go way back, how about a discount?”

“Your cap’s on backwards.”

“…I’m not wearing a cap today.”

“Then your shirt’s on backwards.”

Chen Xi looked down at his shirt. It wasn’t. He swore, and everyone around burst out laughing. This time, half of those laughing were laughing towards Ye Zhixing, with a touch of currying favour, a touch of joining in.

Ye Zhixing smiled briefly too, and turned to look out of the window.

In the second row by the window, Gu Xuerong was asleep on her desk. Sunlight fell on the back of her head and turned the tiny black bun dark brown. She didn’t wake.

---

That Saturday, Ye Zhixia came again.

This time she didn’t go to see the Ragdoll, and didn’t ask for a computer. She stood at the foot of the stairs for a while, watching people go up and down, watching the little whiteboard outside the copy shop that said “Homework printing 50 fen a page,” watching the warm air drifting out of the second-floor window, smelling of curry and pickled cabbage.

Then she went up and found her brother by the counter.

Ye Zhixing was checking the day’s delivery slips, turning them over one by one. Ye Zhixia didn’t disturb him. She leaned back against the counter and watched the room with him:

A family of three eating at a small table against the wall, the child’s legs too short to reach the floor, swinging in the air; two girls lying in the open space by the window, a feather wand each, the calico leaping back and forth between them; Su Tang with her whiteboard marker, patrolling between the windowsill and the cat tree like a conscientious sheepdog; the kitchen’s quilted curtain lifting and falling as Old Zhou came out carrying a sizzling iron plate, steam rolling upward under the lights.

On the grey sofa in the corner, Gu Xuerong sat with the ledger open on her knees, her pen stopped on the paper, dozing. Briquette was curled at her feet.

“Bro.”

“Mm.”

“You’re kind of famous now.”

“Mm.”

“People in my class are passing round that video of yours.”

Ye Zhixing didn’t look up. He turned over a delivery slip.

Ye Zhixia tilted her head and looked at him for a while. Under the counter light his profile looked a little hard, his jaw set, though the fingers turning the slips were very steady.

She didn’t say any more. She went to the kitchen curtain and asked Old Zhou for a sizzling pickled cabbage and beef rice with extra egg, and an iced yuanyang.

When the food came she poked open the yolk with her chopsticks and watched the golden yolk run slowly down into the cabbage. She watched it a while without eating, and then suddenly said something. Not loudly; it might have been to herself, or to the iron plate:

“This time it’s yours.”

Ye Zhixing’s hand paused on the delivery slips.

Then went on turning them.

## 7. The Lunchbox

Nine in the evening, closing time.

Old Zhou turned off the flame, scrubbed the griddle plates three times with steel wool, dried them, wiped on a thin layer of oil, wrapped them in newspaper and put them in the cupboard. Sister Mei finished the accounts, called “See you tomorrow!” from the door and clicked down the stairs in her heels. Uncle Chen drove Su Tang back across the river. Uncle He was the last to leave, and before he went he glanced at the sofa in the corner.

Gu Xuerong was awake now. She sat up, closed the ledger, put it in the counter drawer, and put the pen back in the pen pot, nib down.

She went downstairs with Ye Zhixing.

Back Street at night was a different place from Back Street by day. The braised-meat stall had packed up, leaving only a damp patch of ground with a few duck bones on it, two stray cats nosing through them. The mahjong parlour was still open, tiles clattering through the door curtain. Moths circled under the street lights, round and round, bumping into the lampshades with very soft little thuds.

They reached the mouth of the alley. Uncle Chen’s car wasn’t back yet.

“Where are you going back to?” Ye Zhixing asked.

She looked at him.

“The dormitory.”

“…Okay.”

He didn’t ask which dormitory. She didn’t say.

She walked towards the building with the gap-toothed white tiles. After a dozen or so steps she looked back and saw him still standing at the mouth of the alley. The street light shone straight down on him, pressing his shadow very short, huddled round his feet.

She turned back and walked on.

---

Lin Xiaoman was waiting for her.

She waited every day.

The No. 8 High canteen served dinner from six, and after six-forty there was nothing left but some stone-cold stir-fried greens and shredded potato. Every day, the moment the bell went for ninth period, Lin Xiaoman ran for the canteen, first in the queue, and got two portions: one to eat herself, one packed into a pink insulated lunchbox. She had brought the lunchbox from home. It had a cloth cover printed with rabbits, and on the lid, in marker pen, three characters: “Xuerong’s.” Written very big, with a heart drawn after them. Washing-up had rubbed half the heart away, leaving only a curved edge, like a crescent moon.

She brought the lunchbox back to the room and put it on the little table by Gu Xuerong’s bed, with a thick English dictionary on the lid to stop it cooling too fast. Then she began to wait.

Nine-twenty, nine-thirty, nine-forty. Every time footsteps sounded in the stairwell, she poked her head out from the top bunk.

Nearly ten, the door opened.

“You’re back!” Lin Xiaoman scrambled down from the top bunk, jumped from the second rung, and lost a slipper as she landed. She hopped over on one foot, slid the lunchbox out from under the dictionary and unscrewed the lid. Still warm, a little steam rising: tomato and egg, shredded potato, and a piece of red-braised pork, which she had spent ages wheedling out of the serving lady.

“There’s braised pork today,” she said. “The serving lady’s hand shakes, so I told her my classmate was ill, and then she gave me a piece. Look, it’s got fat and lean.”

Gu Xuerong sat on the edge of the bed and looked at the lunchbox.

The truth was she had been smelling pickled cabbage and curry all afternoon in the shop. Old Zhou sometimes saved her a bowl, and she always ate two mouthfuls and put it down. But Lin Xiaoman’s lunchbox, she would eat.

She picked up the spoon and ate, mouthful by mouthful. She ate very slowly, eating a little, stopping a little, staring blankly at some particular strand of potato, while Lin Xiaoman sat beside her, knees against knees, chattering on: who in the class had had a row with who today, over an eraser; how the canteen lady’s hand had shaken especially badly today, a whole ladle shaken down to three strands of veg; how the physics teacher’s wig had been blown crooked by the wind through the window and he hadn’t noticed, and taught half a lesson like that while the whole class held in their laughter.

All things that were nothing at all.

Now and then Gu Xuerong said “mm.” Sometimes she made no sound, and only the corner of her mouth moved.

Lin Xiaoman saw that corner move and was very pleased, and talked all the harder.

Gu Xuerong could get through half a lunchbox. Sometimes a bit more, sometimes a bit less. She ate the braised pork, very carefully, the layer of fat too.

Lin Xiaoman watched from beside her, and didn’t say “eat some more,” or “one more mouthful.” However much she ate was fine. As long as she ate, that was fine.

---

When she had finished, the two of them squeezed together on the lower bunk.

The other two girls in the room were already asleep. One was grinding her teeth, creak, creak, in duet with the ceiling fan; one was talking in her sleep, apparently English vocabulary, mumbling “abandon, abandon.”

Lin Xiaoman turned her phone screen down to its dimmest.

“One round.”

“Of what?”

“That thing of yours, nightmare whatever.”

“Didn’t you say it scares you?”

“It does scare me.” Lin Xiaoman kept her voice down, perfectly reasonable. “So you have to protect me.”

Lin Xiaoman was genuinely terrible, terrible through and through. As soon as her character stepped into the rainy corridor she began to shake, shaking so hard the whole bed trembled. At the first corner a hand reached out of the wallpaper and she cried “Ah!” out loud, clapped a hand over her own mouth, eyes wide, and looked at the other two beds. Nobody woke. She let out a breath, turned back, and cried out again at the next corner.

Her character never survived past the third corner.

So Gu Xuerong took her through it again and again.

“There’s something there.”

“Where, where?”

“Behind that door on the left. Don’t open it.”

“I opened it.”

“…”

“I’m dead.”

“Mm.”

“You laughed!” Lin Xiaoman leaned in, the tip of her nose almost touching Gu Xuerong’s face, voice low. “Did you just laugh?”

“No.”

“You did! I saw! The corner of your mouth, here,” she reached out a finger and traced the corner of her own mouth, “here, it went up!”

“The light’s too dim, you saw wrong.”

“I didn’t see wrong!”

Gu Xuerong didn’t deny it again. She took the phone from Lin Xiaoman’s hand and started a new round for her.

---

Lin Xiaoman was somewhere in the bottom dozen of the class. In the last physics quiz she had got thirty-one, and when the paper came back she laughed first, saying it was four marks better than last time. Her parents both worked in factories in Guangdong and came back once a year, for those few days at New Year. On the phone they always asked the same two questions: “Have you got enough money?” “Don’t get too close to boys.”

She didn’t expect anything very big of Gu Xuerong. She didn’t need her to do well in exams, didn’t need her to get close to anyone, didn’t need her to pour her heart out about anything.

As long as she came back every night, ate a bit, played a round with her, and smiled once in a while—even if she wouldn’t admit it—that was enough.

She didn’t know what was under those two long sleeves. She had seen that Gu Xuerong didn’t push her sleeves up even to wash her face, had seen that on the very hottest days of summer she would let the back of her shirt soak through with sweat rather than unbutton her cuffs. She had never asked, and didn’t intend to.

She just felt that Gu Xuerong was like a glass set near the edge of a table. The glass itself was fine, no chips, no cracks; it was only that it had been put in the wrong place, too close to the edge, so that anyone walking past and stirring up a breeze might knock it off.

She didn’t want to be that breeze.

And she didn’t know how to push the glass further in. Push too hard, and she was afraid it might tip over instead.

So every day she got two portions, every day she waited, every day she played a round of the game. It was the way she could think of that was least likely to touch the glass.

---

Round after round, Lin Xiaoman’s eyelids grew heavier.

Past midnight, her head nodded lower and lower and finally came to rest on Gu Xuerong’s shoulder, and she was asleep, phone still gripped in her hand. On the screen her character stood in the middle of the corridor, not moving, while something hanging from the ceiling slowly wrapped it up.

Gu Xuerong didn’t move.

She eased the phone out of Lin Xiaoman’s hand, quit the game, locked the screen and put it by the pillow. Then she just sat there with Lin Xiaoman leaning on her, listening to the ceiling fan creak, creak, and the girl in the next bed still reciting “abandon.”

She wasn’t sleepy. She was rarely sleepy at this hour.

Outside the window, the sliver of sky squeezed between the two buildings was turned a murky orange by the street light. Far off, a motorbike went by, its sound coming from very far away and going very far away, until it couldn’t be heard at all.

On her shoulder, Lin Xiaoman stirred and mumbled something. It sounded like “braised pork.”

Gu Xuerong looked down at her for a while.

Then she reached out and tucked a strand of hair that had stuck to Lin Xiaoman’s forehead with sweat back behind her ear.
