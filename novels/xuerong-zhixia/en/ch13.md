# Chapter Thirteen　The Urban Village

## 1. A Good Woman

The woman in the magenta down jacket came back several more times.

The second time was in early December, at noon on a Tuesday. She brought a bag of sugar tangerines, a carton of milk, and a knitted wool scarf, bright red, knitted very thick. She stood at the door of Class Seven and called “Rongrong” into the classroom, loud enough for half the floor to hear.

She wound the scarf round Gu Xuerong’s neck herself, twice round, tied it in a knot at the front, stepped back two paces and looked her over, her eyes narrowed to two slits. “Oh, isn’t that lovely. Red really suits you, look at your face, the minute it’s on you’ve got some colour.”

Then she turned round and started putting sugar tangerines on the desks around them, handful by handful.

“Eat, eat, a relative of ours grows them, they’re ever so sweet.”

The third time was in mid-December. She brought an insulated pot of stewed chicken soup. She set the pot on Gu Xuerong’s desk and twisted off the lid, and steam came billowing out, and the whole classroom smelled of chicken soup. She ladled some into a small bowl, blew on it, and went to hold it to Gu Xuerong’s lips.

Gu Xuerong turned her head away.

The woman’s hand stopped in mid-air, the soup in the bowl sloshed, and a little spilled onto the back of her hand. She let out a hiss, but didn’t pull her hand back, and the smile on her face didn’t change in the least; if anything it grew deeper.

“Not drinking it? Then leave it here, have it when it’s cooled.” She put the bowl down and turned to the girls around them, lowering her voice, but just enough that every one of them could hear. “This child, she’s been like this since she was little. Same temper as her real mother, stubborn. The year I married in she was only eight, and I was good to her, and she didn’t appreciate it: the food I made she wouldn’t eat, the clothes I bought she wouldn’t wear, when I talked to her she glared at me.”

She sighed, and her eyes actually went red.

“I don’t blame her. A child without a mother, it’s hard for her inside. I just… I just want to be good to her.”

The girls around them exchanged glances.

---

The fourth time, the fifth time.

She came more and more often, and brought more and more things. Fruit, snacks, milk, chicken soup, socks, gloves, a pink thermos cup, a notebook printed with a cartoon rabbit. Every time at noon, every time when the most people were around, every time in front of the whole class.

Every time, Gu Xuerong said nothing.

She took the things, stuffed them under her desk or put them to one side. But she never said a single word to the woman. When the woman spoke to her, she didn’t answer; when the woman wound the scarf round her, she didn’t move; when the woman ladled soup for her, she turned her head away; when the woman left saying “Rongrong, Auntie’s going now,” she didn’t so much as lift her eyelids.

Voices started up in the classroom.

---

At first they were very small voices. Between classes, in the toilets, in the queue at the canteen.

“Her stepmum’s really good to her.”

“Yeah, she brings so much stuff every time.”

“That chicken soup, just the smell made me hungry.”

“Why doesn’t she say a word?”

“Maybe… they don’t get on.”

Later the voices got a little louder.

“Her stepmum’s so nice, why’s she got that attitude?”

“Right, the woman comes all that way, bringing things, bringing soup, and she doesn’t even say thank you.”

“I heard she was only eight when her stepmum married in, and she’s always been good to her, and she just doesn’t appreciate it.”

“So she’s an ingrate, then?”

Once that word was out, it was like a seed falling into very loose soil, and overnight it had grown over the whole classroom.

“Ingrate.”

“You can feed her all you like, she’ll never be grateful.”

“A stepmum is still a mum, and it’s not like she owes her anything.”

“Her real dad even came to school to apologize to the teacher, see how decent he is.”

“She’s the only one making trouble.”

---

Someone said it to her face.

A girl who sat diagonally behind Gu Xuerong. That noon, after the woman had gone, the girl turned round, looked at the untouched bowl of chicken soup on Gu Xuerong’s desk, and said:

“Your stepmum’s that good to you, what’s your problem?”

Gu Xuerong didn’t turn round.

“She brings you all that stuff every time,” the girl’s voice rose a little, “and you can’t even say thank you. Do you know how many people’s mums don’t care about them at all? You’ve got a stepmum this nice, and you’re still not satisfied?”

The classroom got a little quieter. Several people were looking this way.

Gu Xuerong sat there with her back to the girl. Her right hand was inside her sleeve, gripping her left wrist.

She said nothing.

The girl waited a while, got no answer, clicked her tongue and turned back round.

“Ingrate,” she said under her breath, to no one in particular.

---

Lin Xiaoman sat beside her listening, her face flushed bright red.

She wanted to stand up and say something; she opened her mouth, then shut it again. She didn’t know what to say. All she knew was that the time Gu Xuerong’s father came to the school, on the way back he had cursed her the whole way at the corner of Back Street, cursed her horribly; Chen Xi had told her afterwards. But she hadn’t seen it with her own eyes. And all she had ever seen of the stepmother was her beaming as she brought chicken soup, wound scarves, put sugar tangerines on classmates’ desks.

She looked at the back of Gu Xuerong’s head, at that tiny black bun.

She didn’t manage to say anything.

---

Even Ye Zhixing and Chen Xi were starting to feel a little unsure.

They both remembered that first time, the child’s down jacket in a size 120, remembered the thing the woman had said in Gu Xuerong’s ear, remembered her making a phone call the moment she was out of the school gate. But once or twice could be called an act; what about the third time, the fourth, the fifth? The chicken soup was real, the scarf was real, and the patch on the back of the woman’s hand scalded red by the soup was real too.

Could a person really keep up an act that long, and make it that convincing?

“Could it be we’re overthinking it?” Chen Xi said suddenly one time in the lab, in the middle of a problem. “Maybe her stepmum’s just… that kind of person? Nosy, likes to show off, but not bad at heart?”

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer.

He thought of the yellow stain on the cuff of that child’s down jacket that wouldn’t wash out. Thought of the look the woman had given him, from head to foot, stopping at his shoes.

But he also thought of that bowl of chicken soup. Thought of the woman’s eyes going red.

He didn’t know.

## 2. Why

That day in the lab, Mr. Wu had gone out to a meeting.

Outside it was snowing, and in the lab there was only the hum of the electric heater. The two of them had been working on one electromagnetic induction problem for nearly an hour, and neither had cracked it; scrap paper covered the whole table.

Chen Xi threw down his pen and leaned back in his chair, tipping it onto two legs with a creak.

“Brother Ye, can I ask you something? Don’t think I’m being nosy.”

“Go on.”

Chen Xi stared at the ceiling for a while.

“Why do you help her so much?”

The hand Ye Zhixing held his pen in paused.

“Gu Xuerong,” said Chen Xi. “All the things you’ve done to help her, I know about them. That lunch every day, you’re the one who got Su Tang to bring it. That time with her dad, Mr. Sun getting suspended, that was you. The keys to the cat café, the accounts, and—” he paused, “that thousand yuan you gave me, that was because of her too.”

“I’m not saying it’s bad that you help her.” Chen Xi let the chair down and sat up straight, looking at him. “I just want to know why.”

He thought for a moment, and added:

“If you like her, why not just tell her and be done with it? Going round in circles like this, with that brain of hers, she might not even notice.”

Ye Zhixing looked up at him.

Chen Xi looked back, his eyes very bright, very serious, not joking in the slightest.

After a long while, Ye Zhixing spoke.

“I don’t like her.”

“At least not the way you mean.” Ye Zhixing lowered his head and looked at the unsolved problem on the scrap paper. “It’s just… when I see her, I think of some things I did before.”

“What things?”

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer.

He picked up his pen and drew a coil on the scrap paper, drew a magnet, drew an arrow. When he’d drawn it, he crossed the arrow out.

“Brother Ye?”

“Nothing, I’ll tell you some other time,” he said. “Let’s get this problem done first.”

Chen Xi looked at him. He didn’t press any further. He picked up the pen he had thrown down, twirled it once between his fingers, and looked down at his own problem again.

Outside, the snow was still falling. The electric heater hummed.

After a while, Chen Xi wrote a line on his scrap paper and pushed it in front of Ye Zhixing.

> Rate of change of magnetic flux. You missed a minus sign.

Ye Zhixing glanced at it, stopped short, and bent his head to correct it.

Once he’d corrected it, the problem came out.

## 3. Surplus

The decision to go to the Gu house was made that Friday evening.

After the shop closed, a few of them stayed. Ye Zhixing, Chen Xi, Su Tang, and Lin Xiaoman— Su Tang had dragged her along; it was her first time in the cat café, and the moment she came in she was smitten with Rice Cake, and squatted under the windowsill for ages without getting up.

Gu Xuerong sat in the corner of the grey sofa with the bright red wool scarf draped over her knees, not wearing it, just draped there. Briquette lay at her feet.

Nobody said anything.

In the end it was Lin Xiaoman who spoke first. She got up from under the windowsill, came over and squatted down in front of Gu Xuerong.

“Xuerong.”

Gu Xuerong looked at her.

“The things people in class are saying,” Lin Xiaoman’s voice shook a little, “don’t take them to heart.”

Gu Xuerong said “mm,” and after a moment added, “Don’t your legs go numb, squatting like that?”

“I…” Lin Xiaoman bit her lip. “I don’t actually know what’s really going on. I just saw your stepmum come with things, with soup, smiling at you. And then you ignore her. And then everyone says you’re…”

She couldn’t go on.

Gu Xuerong looked at her for a while, then turned her head to the others in the room. To Ye Zhixing, Chen Xi, Su Tang.

“Do you really think,” she said, “that I’m beyond forgiving?”

The room went quiet.

Su Tang opened her mouth, wanting to say “no”, but Gu Xuerong’s eyes had already moved off her face, to the window. The curtain wasn’t fully drawn; it left a gap, and outside the gap were the streetlamps of Back Street, and the snow falling flake after flake beneath them.

“Maybe in this world,” said Gu Xuerong, “there’s no love without a reason, and no hate without a reason.”

Her voice was very flat, very soft, like reading out the stem of a problem.

“She smiles at me because people are watching. She brings me soup because people are watching. My classmates call me names because they saw the soup and didn’t see anything else. My dad bows and scrapes in front of the teachers because the teachers decide whether I stay at No. 8 High. Whether I stay at No. 8 High decides whether I can get into university. Whether I can get into university decides—”

She paused.

“How much I’m worth.”

Su Tang’s hands clenched.

“Everyone has a why for everything they do,” said Gu Xuerong. “Love has a why, hate has a why. They all add up.”

She brought her gaze back from the window, lowered her head and looked at the red scarf on her knees.

“Only I don’t add up,” she said. “I’m here without any why.”

“Maybe, to everyone, I’m just surplus.”

---

For a long time nobody in the room spoke.

Briquette rolled over at her feet and rested a paw on the top of her shoe. Rice Cake jumped down from the windowsill, landed soundlessly on the floor, came over, sat down by the sofa and looked up at her.

Su Tang was the first to stand. She went over and sat down beside Gu Xuerong, said nothing, reached out, took the red scarf off her knees, folded it and put it to one side. Then she took Gu Xuerong’s hand.

Gu Xuerong’s hand was very cold. Su Tang held it and didn’t let go.

Chen Xi leaned against the counter, head down, grinding the toe of one shoe against the floor.

Ye Zhixing stayed seated the whole time.

He looked at Gu Xuerong. At her lowered head, at that tiny black bun, at her hand in Su Tang’s.

“Surplus.”

He said the word over once in his head.

He thought of the one in a billion Chen Xi had told her about. A billion antimatter particles, a billion and one matter particles; once they’ve annihilated each other, the one that’s left over is everything.

He didn’t say it out loud.

“Let’s go there,” he said.

Everyone looked at him.

“To your home.” He looked at Gu Xuerong. “We get three days off for New Year’s, right? We’ll go back there with you and have a look.”

Gu Xuerong raised her head.

“A look at what?”

“At what’s really going on,” said Ye Zhixing. “Not to prove anything to anyone. Because we want to know for ourselves.”

“…If you want to go, then go,” she said.

## 4. The Bus

January the first, New Year’s Day.

The sky was overcast, the grey-white clouds hanging very low, like an old cotton quilt soaked in water. The snow had stopped two days before, and the snow by the roadside had been ground by tyres into black slush and frozen, all ruts and potholes, crunching underfoot.

The county town’s bus station was in the south of town. An old two-storey building, its outer walls painted pale yellow, the plaster coming off in patches. The waiting hall was very cold, rows and rows of blue plastic chairs, people sitting with bags large and small, someone cracking sunflower seeds, someone on the phone, someone asleep against the chair back with their mouth open. On the wall hung an electronic board scrolling departure times, and some of the characters had dead tubes and didn’t show in full.

The five of them stood at the ticket gate.

Ye Zhixing wore his knee-length dark grey down jacket. Chen Xi wore the army-green padded jacket with electrical tape wound round the cuffs. Su Tang wore the bright red sweater her mum had knitted, with a faded blue padded coat over it. Lin Xiaoman was wrapped up the most thoroughly: a pink down jacket with two rabbit ears on the hood, a scarf wound three times round, only her two eyes showing.

Gu Xuerong was in her school uniform.

The winter uniform, navy blue with white stripes. Under it was the grey thermal set Lin Xiaoman had given her. Nothing over it.

“You’re wearing just that? You haven’t got anything warmer?” Chen Xi asked.

“…This is enough.”

Ye Zhixing said nothing, unwound the scarf from his own neck and held it out.

Gu Xuerong glanced at the scarf. Dark grey, cashmere, very soft to the touch.

She didn’t take it.

Su Tang reached out, took the scarf, shook it open, wound it round Gu Xuerong’s neck, twice, and tucked it in at the front.

“He’s not cold,” said Su Tang. “His jacket’s thick.”

Gu Xuerong looked down at the scarf round her neck, then at Su Tang, and said nothing more.

---

The bus was a very old minibus, faded red characters printed on its white body, a layer of fog on the window glass. The seats were the dark green leatherette kind, split in several places, showing yellow foam underneath. Inside it smelled of diesel and tobacco mixed together; the heating was on, turned up high, so stuffy it made your head swim.

The five of them sat in the back row.

The bus left the county town and turned onto a county road. On both sides were harvested fields covered with a thin layer of snow, and under the snow stuck up row after row of corn stubble, blackish brown, like a field planted full of old chopsticks. Now and then they passed a village, a sign standing at its entrance with the village’s name, the characters weathered nearly illegible by wind and rain. Some villages had very new houses, two- or three-storey buildings faced with white tiles; some had very old ones, red-brick single-storey houses, their courtyard walls half collapsed, firewood piled in the yards.

The bus stopped and started the whole way. Someone waved by the roadside, it stopped; someone called out “Driver, getting off up ahead,” it stopped too. On got an old woman with two baskets of eggs on a shoulder pole, off got a middle-aged man with a woven plastic sack on his back, then on got two middle-schoolers in uniform. The conductor was a middle-aged woman sitting by the door with a black money belt round her waist, and every time someone got on she called “Move down, move down,” took the money and tore off a ticket.

An hour.

Gu Xuerong looked out of the window the whole time.

Her forehead rested against the glass, and her breath had melted a small patch in the fog, and through that small patch you could see the fields, trees, telegraph poles and villages flashing past. She didn’t say a word.

Su Tang sat beside her, holding her hand the whole way.

## 5. The Single-Storey Houses

Where they got off was an urban village.

Urban village was the name, but really it was almost outside the town. A single main road, and on both sides row after row of single-storey houses, red brick, grey tile, some with walls rendered in cement, crammed up against each other, with alleys between them so narrow only one person could pass. The alleys were dirt, frozen very hard, full of ruts and potholes, frozen wastewater and stove ash everywhere.

From the roof of every house stuck a sheet-iron chimney pipe, some straight, some with a bend, and out of the pipes came grey-white smoke that the wind scattered as soon as it blew, weaving a thin, murky haze in mid-air that covered the whole village. The air had a choking smell of coal smoke, mixed with a little of burning wood.

At the mouth of the alley was a public toilet. A little cement-block building with no door, the characters “Men” and “Women” painted on the wall in red, the “Women” half scratched out by someone with a stone. You could smell it from a long way off; even the cold couldn’t keep it down.

Gu Xuerong walked at the front. She knew the way well; she took three turns and stopped at a courtyard gate.

The gate was two iron doors, once painted blue, the paint flaking off in blotches, and on the gate was a pair of New Year couplets, last year’s, the red paper faded to pinkish white, the corners curling up and flapping in the wind.

She stood at the gate and didn’t push it.

Ye Zhixing went up and stood beside her.

“Want me to open it?”

Gu Xuerong glanced at him and shook her head.

She reached out and pushed the gate open.

---

The courtyard was tiny, three or four square metres, piled full of things. Stacks and stacks of honeycomb coal briquettes, piled against the foot of the wall half a person high, a torn plastic sheet over the top; an old e-bike, its seat split and wound round with tape; a big plastic basin of dirty water frozen into ice, a few vegetable leaves frozen into the ice. The ground was all cinders and stove ash, black patches and grey patches, soft underfoot, clinging to the soles of your shoes.

Facing the gate was a row of single-storey rooms, three of them. The door of the middle one was open, a thick padded curtain hanging over it, army green, so greasy it shone.

The curtain was lifted.

The woman stood in the doorway.

Not in the magenta down jacket. She wore an old sweater, jujube red, covered in pills, the sleeves rolled up, flour on her hands. Her hair had none of that permed gloss; it was scraped back messily and held with a big black clip. No powder on her face, no lipstick, her skin sallow, heavy bags under her eyes, the corners of her mouth drooping.

She saw Gu Xuerong.

Then she saw the four people behind Gu Xuerong.

Her face changed.

It didn’t turn into a smile. First she was taken aback, her eyes widening a little, her mouth slightly open— and then, little by little, that face sank, like a piece of iron being slowly lowered into cold water.

“So you remember where you live?”

Her voice was completely different from the one at school. At school her voice was high and sweet, like singing. Now it was low and hoarse, with an indescribable, ice-cold spite in it.

“Bringing a whole gang back with you, what for? To show me?”

---

Inside it was very dark.

The moment they went in, warm air mixed with coal smoke hit them in the face, and Lin Xiaoman coughed twice. In the middle of the room stood a sheet-iron stove with a kettle on it, steam coming out of the spout. A sheet-iron flue pipe ran up from the stove through the middle of the room, bent near the roof beam and went out through a hole in the wall. Where the sections of pipe joined, there were black rings, stained by leaking coal smoke.

The whole room was grey.

The walls were grey; they must once have been white, and the coal smoke had turned them a yellowish grey. The roof beams were black, hung with a few wisps of cobweb, and the cobwebs were grey with dust too. The windows were covered with a layer of plastic sheeting to keep the wind out, and the plastic had gone yellow from the smoke, so the light that came through was yellow too. On the table, on the cupboard, on the television, everywhere there was a thin layer of dust that left a streak if you ran a finger through it.

This room was the living room.

Against the wall of the living room stood a big bed, the quilt on it not folded, heaped in a mess, printed with big peonies washed nearly white. Beside the bed was a folding table with a few bowls on it, the leftovers of a meal, a plate of stir-fried cabbage, a dish of pickles, a layer of grease congealed on the food.

The room on the left had its door shut. On the door was a cartoon sticker, a little pink rabbit, and under the rabbit, in coloured pen, two wobbly characters: “Tiantian”.

The room on the right had its curtain half lifted, and you could see it was the kitchen. A brick stove with a big iron wok on it, a spatula resting on the edge of the wok. Beside the stove was a smaller stove, and next to it a basket of honeycomb briquettes, the coal in the basket wet, a glossy black. In the corner stood a folding bed.

A very narrow folding bed, an iron frame, a thin mattress pad on it, and on the pad a small quilt, folded very flat, all four corners neatly tucked in.

Ye Zhixing looked at the folding bed.

He knew that way of folding.

He had seen one exactly the same in the basement.

---

“Who are you?”

The voice came from outside. Gu Jianguo had come in from the courtyard with a sack of rice on his shoulder, and when he saw the people in the room, he stopped dead.

He was different from at school, too.

At school he had worn that grey work jacket, stooping, rubbing his hands, all “yes, yes, yes” and “sorry for the trouble, teacher”. Now he dropped the sack of rice on the floor, dropped it with a thud, straightened up and stood in the doorway, filling half the frame. He was very tall, very broad, the old padded coat wrapped round him bulging where his shoulders stretched it.

His eyes fell first on Gu Xuerong, then swept over the others one by one. Over Lin Xiaoman, over Su Tang, over Ye Zhixing.

And came to rest on Chen Xi’s face.

His eyes narrowed.

Chen Xi looked back at him. Chen Xi said nothing, his hands in the pockets of his padded jacket, standing next to Ye Zhixing, his chin slightly raised.

Gu Jianguo’s gaze stayed on Chen Xi’s face a long time. Stayed on the faint scar at the corner of his mouth, almost invisible by now.

Then he moved his gaze away, back to Gu Xuerong.

“Well, well,” he said. “Well, well. Got too big for your boots.”

---

“Look at you,” said the woman, standing by the stove with her hands on her hips, “look at the kind of people you go around with. The one who beat up your dad outside the school last time, it’s this one, isn’t it?” She jerked her chin at Chen Xi. “Riff-raff.”

“A girl, running around outside with riff-raff,” Gu Jianguo took over, his voice not loud, squeezing it out one word at a time, “messing about with a bunch of little thugs, and you’ve found yourself a man too. Let me tell you, don’t think I don’t know. Where do you live? You don’t go back to the dorm at night, so where do you sleep? Who with?”

Lin Xiaoman sucked in a sharp breath.

“And you bring them home, for fuck’s sake.” Gu Jianguo’s voice climbed bit by bit. “Two of them. Bring back two at once. How many times have you let them screw you? Do you know who’s going to want you after this? The bride price—”

“Are you done?”

It was Ye Zhixing.

He took one step forward and stood between Gu Xuerong and Gu Jianguo.

He was half a head shorter than Gu Jianguo, and a size slimmer. He stood there, the hem of his down jacket hanging to his knees, his face expressionless, just looking at Gu Jianguo.

Chen Xi took a step forward too, and stood beside him.

The two of them, side by side, standing in front of Gu Xuerong.

Gu Jianguo looked at them.

His hands balled into fists inside the sleeves of his padded coat, clenched tight, the knuckles standing out one by one. His chest rose and fell, he was breathing hard, and the corners of his mouth twitched twice.

He looked at Chen Xi.

He remembered this one. Remembered that corner of Back Street, remembered the iced tea spilled all over the ground, remembered four people pinning him to the ground, his face pressed against the icy concrete, pressed into that puddle of red tea.

He looked at Ye Zhixing too.

He didn’t know this one. But he recognized the down jacket, recognized the shoes, recognized the way he stood. He had seen that way of standing before, in the building-materials market, on the bosses who came to buy stock, on the people who drove cars and never carried anything themselves.

His fists slowly unclenched.

“…Fine,” he said. “Fine. You lot have got what it takes.”

He turned round, bent and shouldered the sack of rice from the floor, carried it into the kitchen and dropped it with a thud beside the folding bed.

---

The door on the left with the rabbit on it opened a crack.

A little girl poked her head out. Seven or eight, her hair in two little pigtails, round cheeks, rosy red. She wore a pink down jacket, brand new, the cuffs spotless, the zip pull a shiny little metal rabbit.

She looked at the crowd of strangers in the room and blinked.

“Mum, who are they?”

“Back in your room!” the woman said sharply. “Do your homework!”

The little girl ducked her head into her shoulders and shut the door.

Before the door closed, Ye Zhixing saw inside. A very small room, a single bed with a pink sheet, a row of stuffed toys at the head of the bed, a few certificates of merit stuck on the wall, and on the floor by the bed an electric heater, red, glowing, humming.

It was the only place in that house without coal dust.

---

Chen Xi had seen the down jacket too.

Pink. Brand new. The zip pull a little metal rabbit.

He turned his head and glanced at Gu Xuerong.

Gu Xuerong stood there, Ye Zhixing’s dark grey scarf round her neck, her winter uniform on. She was looking at the closed door, at the little pink rabbit sticker on it.

There was nothing at all on her face.

## 6. Two Cars

They stayed in that house less than twenty minutes.

On the way out, Gu Xuerong went into the kitchen and stood by the folding bed for a while. She bent down and pulled a cardboard box out from under the bed, the box thick with dust. She opened it, rummaged a little, and took out two things.

An old schoolbag. Canvas, probably blue once, washed nearly white, the strap broken once and sewn back with thick black thread, the stitches crooked; you could tell at a glance a child had sewn it.

A hardback notebook. The cover curled at the edges, all four corners worn round.

At the bottom of the box was a photo album. She picked it up, looked at the cover, didn’t even open it, and tossed it back into the box. Tossed it carelessly, no differently from tossing a used-up ball of paper.

She put the notebook into the old schoolbag, put the old schoolbag into the bag on her back, and pushed the cardboard box back under the bed.

The woman stood in the kitchen doorway the whole time, watching her, saying nothing.

Gu Xuerong shouldered her bag, walked past her, and went out through the door with the padded curtain.

She didn’t look back.

---

They didn’t take the bus back.

At the mouth of the alley, Ye Zhixing called two cars on his phone. While they waited, the five of them stood by the road and nobody spoke. The wind came across from the fields, stinging their faces. It carried the smell of that public toilet over, and carried it away again. On the roofs of the houses in the distance, chimney after chimney was still smoking, grey-white, joining into one sheet, covering the whole village.

The cars came.

Su Tang, Lin Xiaoman and Gu Xuerong took one. Ye Zhixing and Chen Xi took the other.

---

In the car in front, the three girls squeezed into the back seat.

Gu Xuerong sat in the middle. She held her schoolbag in her arms, held it very tight, both arms round it, her chin resting on it.

The car went a long way, out onto the county road, and on both sides were those fields again, planted full of corn stubble like old chopsticks.

Lin Xiaoman was the first to cry.

At first she just sniffed, and sniffing, the tears started falling, one after another, dropping onto that pink down jacket and leaving small dark circles. She wiped them with the back of her hand, and the more she wiped, the more there were.

“Sorry,” she said, her voice very hoarse. “Xuerong, I’m sorry.”

Gu Xuerong turned her head to look at her.

“When people in class were saying things about you,” said Lin Xiaoman, “I didn’t say anything. I didn’t know… I didn’t know it was like this. I thought… I thought your stepmum really was good to you…”

She couldn’t go on, and buried her face in her hands.

Gu Xuerong looked at her for a while.

“You save me food every day,” said Gu Xuerong.

Lin Xiaoman raised her eyes between her fingers.

“You save me food every day,” Gu Xuerong said again. “You didn’t do anything wrong.”

Lin Xiaoman stared at her blankly. Then she burst into even louder sobs, threw her arms round Gu Xuerong and held her very tight, burying her face in her shoulder, smearing tears and snot all over that dark grey cashmere scarf.

Held like that, Gu Xuerong stiffened for a moment, then slowly, slowly, relaxed.

She didn’t hug her back. But she didn’t push her away either.

Su Tang sat on the other side and didn’t say anything the whole time. She reached out, took the hand Gu Xuerong had wrapped round her schoolbag, and held it in her own.

Gu Xuerong’s hand was still cold.

Su Tang held it, and put it into the pocket of her padded coat, together with her own hand, tucked inside.

It was very warm in the pocket.

Gu Xuerong looked down at the bulging pocket. There was a crumpled supermarket receipt in it, pressing into her fingers.

“…Before I was ten,” she said suddenly, very quietly, “I didn’t live there.”

Su Tang’s hand tightened in the pocket.

“I lived at my grandma’s,” said Gu Xuerong. “My mum left me there and went. Didn’t give anything, didn’t leave anything. Just didn’t want me.”

Lin Xiaoman lifted her head from her shoulder.

“Grandma was old,” said Gu Xuerong. “She said, for my mum’s sake, she’d give me a mouthful of food.”

She paused, as if thinking about how the words had gone, wanting to be sure she remembered them right.

“She said it every day. When she served the rice, she said it.”

It was very quiet in the car. The driver had the radio on, and the radio was playing a very old song, the signal poor, cutting out in bursts.

“My uncle’s family had a boy and a girl,” said Gu Xuerong. “At mealtimes they wouldn’t let me at the table, so I ate on the doorstep holding my bowl. In winter we slept on one kang, and they’d roll the quilt away and say I was a nobody’s-child and didn’t deserve to be covered. If I cried, Grandma would say, what are you crying for, you’re lucky to have a mouthful of food.”

As she said all this, her voice didn’t rise or fall, like reciting a text.

“Then Grandma said she was old and couldn’t manage any more, and had my uncle send me to my dad’s.”

She looked down at the schoolbag in her arms.

“That old schoolbag is the one I had in first grade. From Grandma’s house to my dad’s house, that was the one thing I brought with me.”

“And the notebook?” Lin Xiaoman asked softly.

Gu Xuerong didn’t answer.

She rested her chin on the schoolbag again and closed her eyes.

---

In the car behind, the two boys sat one in front, one in back.

Ye Zhixing sat in the passenger seat, Chen Xi in the back. The driver was a talkative middle-aged man who started chatting the moment they got in, about the weather, about the epidemic— “Heard some kind of pneumonia’s turned up over in Wuhan, you know about that?”— about whether they’d be going home for New Year. Neither of them took him up on it, and after a while the driver lost interest himself and turned on the radio.

The car drove for a long time.

“Brother Ye,” Chen Xi said from the back. “That down jacket her sister had on, you saw it, right?”

“I saw it.”

“Brand new. Even had a little rabbit on the zip pull,” said Chen Xi. “And there was a heater on in that room, just for her sister.”

“Mm.”

“And her? She sleeps in the kitchen.”

Ye Zhixing said nothing.

“Right next to that stove,” said Chen Xi. “A coal stove. Did you see that flue pipe? The joins were all black, it’s been leaking smoke the whole time.”

“…How many years did she sleep on that folding bed?”

Ye Zhixing looked out of the car window.

Outside were those fields. The snow in the fields was white, the corn stubble black, the sky grey. A crow rose from the field, flying very low, past the car window, and away behind them.

“Now I’ve seen it for what it is,” said Ye Zhixing.

His voice was very flat, so flat it didn’t quite sound like him.

“These people are just scum.”

Chen Xi said nothing in the back.

“One face in public, another behind closed doors,” said Ye Zhixing. “At school, he’s an honest man, she’s a good stepmum. Chicken soup, scarves, she can even make her eyes go red. Shut the door, and one sleeps in the kitchen, one sleeps in a room with an electric heater. Outside, he apologizes to the teachers; at home, the first thing out of his mouth is how many times she’s been screwed, how much bride price she’ll fetch.”

His hands rested on his knees, and his fingers slowly curled in.

“A really good act,” he said. “So good the whole class believed it. So good I nearly believed it myself.”

“…Me too,” said Chen Xi.

“That bowl of chicken soup,” said Ye Zhixing, “was a show for the whole class. That child’s jacket was a show for her.”

“What do you mean by that?”

Ye Zhixing didn’t answer straight away.

“Making the whole class think she’s an ingrate,” he said, “is so she can’t stand being at school either. Making her feel she doesn’t even deserve a coat that fits is so she’ll feel…”

He didn’t finish.

“…So she’ll feel she’s surplus,” Chen Xi finished for him.

It was quiet in the car for a long time.

The old song on the radio ended and gave way to a news programme, the presenter’s voice very serious, saying something about “Wuhan”, “unknown cause”, “expert team”, the signal poor, cutting in and out. The driver reached over and changed the station, to an advert for health supplements.

“Scum,” Chen Xi swore in the back, very low, very hard. “Fucking scum.”

Ye Zhixing didn’t reply.

He looked out of the window at the fields, trees, telegraph poles and villages flashing past.

He thought of that folding bed. Thought of that small quilt folded square, all four corners neatly tucked in.

He thought of the bed in the basement. Folded exactly the same way.

## 7. January

After New Year’s Day came the finals.

The exams went on for three days. Gu Xuerong got seventy-four in maths, sixty-one in physics, fifty-eight in chemistry. Not high, but every one of them a good bit above the midterm. When Ms. Zheng handed out the report cards, in the corner of Gu Xuerong’s she wrote two words in red pen: “Keep going.”

The woman in the magenta down jacket never came to the school again.

The voices in class calling Gu Xuerong an ingrate slowly died down too. Not because anyone explained anything. Lin Xiaoman didn’t explain, Su Tang didn’t explain, Ye Zhixing and Chen Xi didn’t explain either. It was only that one lunchtime, the girl who had said it to Gu Xuerong’s face, standing in the canteen queue, heard Lin Xiaoman behind her say something to someone else.

What Lin Xiaoman said was: “She sleeps in the kitchen. Next to a coal stove. Has done for years.”

Just that one line, no beginning, no end.

The girl turned round and looked at Lin Xiaoman. Lin Xiaoman didn’t look at her, and walked off with her tray.

After that the girl never said the word “ingrate” again.

---

In mid-January, the winter holiday began.

The dorm Lin Xiaoman lived in was run privately by the landlady, who usually took in a few students as lodgers to make a bit of rent. As soon as winter holiday came, the landlady locked up, saying she was going back to her home village for New Year and nobody could stay.

Lin Xiaoman’s parents had come back from Guangdong and were going home to the village for New Year. The day she left was the eighteenth of January, a very cold morning. She had a big schoolbag on her back and a suitcase in tow, and she stood at the dorm door for a long time, looking at Gu Xuerong.

“Come home with me,” said Lin Xiaoman. “Our house in the village is big. My mum and dad are really nice, you can share my bed.”

Gu Xuerong shook her head.

“Then where are you going for New Year?”

“I’ve got somewhere to stay.”

“Where?”

Gu Xuerong didn’t answer.

Lin Xiaoman looked at her. She knew Gu Xuerong had somewhere; she had always known, she had just never asked.

She took the pink insulated lunchbox out of her schoolbag and pressed it into Gu Xuerong’s hands.

“This is for you,” she said. “Remember to eat, yourself.”

She turned and went, went a few steps, then turned back and waved at Gu Xuerong. Waved very hard, the rabbit-eared hood flapping in the wind.

---

The twentieth of January.

Gu Xuerong was in the basement, lying on the single bed, looking at her phone.

A news notification came up: in an interview, Academician Zhong Nanshan said that the novel coronavirus pneumonia could spread from person to person.

She looked at the news for a while, then swiped it away.

The twenty-third of January, ten in the morning.

Wuhan closed its outbound routes.

She looked at that news for a long time.

That afternoon, queues formed outside the county town’s pharmacies. People buying masks, buying alcohol, buying disinfectant. On the home page of Town Matters, several hundred posts sprang up overnight: who had masks, who wanted to buy alcohol, which pharmacy still had stock, which street’s supermarket had a long queue. Su Tang posted a photo in the group, the little supermarket downstairs from her building, its shelves half empty. Chen Xi said in the group that his dad’s haulage truck had been stopped at the motorway exit and couldn’t get back.

The twenty-fourth of January, New Year’s Eve.

The Spring Festival Gala went out as usual. Gu Xuerong had no television; she watched a bit of it on her phone, and halfway through, turned it off.

Outside, firecrackers were going off. Very far away, crackling, in bursts. Outside the little basement window level with the pavement, now and then a glimmer of light flashed past, someone’s fireworks reflected in the glass, gone in an instant.

The twenty-fifth of January, the first day of the New Year.

The whole province launched a Level One response to a major public health emergency.

The twenty-seventh of January, the Ministry of Education issued a notice postponing the start of the spring term.

---

The county town began to lock down.

First the villages were sealed. Village entrances were blocked with mounds of earth, with tractors, with iron barriers, nobody allowed in or out, people standing guard in red armbands, shouting through loudhailers. Then the housing estates were sealed, each estate keeping only one gate open, a table at the gate with a thermometer gun and a register, and to go in or out you had to scan a code, sign in, have your temperature taken. Then the streets were sealed: the internet café on Back Street closed, the braised-snack stall had long since stopped coming, the copy shop’s roller shutter came down with a sheet of paper stuck on it: “Closed during the epidemic.”

The cat café upstairs closed too.

Ye Zhixing said in the group that he’d sort out the cats: Uncle He lived close by and would go over every day to feed them. Su Tang said she could go, her home was just across the river, a ten-minute walk. Uncle He said no, you weren’t allowed out as you pleased now; he had a pass, he would go.

Gu Xuerong said nothing in the group.

---

It was very quiet in the basement.

It was very quiet outside too.

That little window level with the pavement used to have shoes passing it every day. People going to school, going home from work, going to buy groceries, walking dogs: leather shoes, trainers, high heels, slippers, pair after pair passing the window, sometimes fast, sometimes slow, sometimes stopping to stand in front of the window a while, and then moving on.

Now there was nothing outside the window at all.

Only snow. Falling layer upon layer onto the pavement, nobody treading it down, piling up thicker and thicker, as high as the windowsill, burying the bottom half of that little window.

Gu Xuerong sat on the edge of the bed, looking at the window.

Her phone lay beside her, the screen lit, showing a record of WeChat transfers.

From: Ye Zhixing.

On the last day of every month. The note said: “Wages for the accounts.”

She had counted. From October to January, four payments. Added together, they came to just enough for three months’ rent on this basement, with a little left over.

She had paid three months’ rent. At the beginning of January, before the finals, she had taken the cash to the landlady, an old woman who lived upstairs, who took the money, counted it twice and said, “Lass, not going home even for New Year?”

She said: “No.”

The old woman looked at her for a while, didn’t ask any more, and wrote her a receipt.

The receipt was now under the pile of workbooks on the table.

With the little money left over, she bought two bags of dried noodles, a case of instant noodles, a bottle of oil, a bag of salt, a bag of rice, and an electric kettle hung on the wall for boiling water, since the old one had broken. The things were piled under the bed against the wall, stacked neatly.

She had worked it out. Eating carefully, it would last till the end of February.

---

The evening of the twenty-eighth of January.

Gu Xuerong sat on the bed with her back against the wall.

That faded old schoolbag hung on the back of the chair, the crooked black stitching on its strap very noticeable under the light. The hardback notebook lay on the table, on top of the workbooks; since she had brought it back, she had not opened it once.

The basement bulb hung overhead, yellow, bare. The blue-and-white checked cloth was still covering the corner, bulging in one place, casting a blurry shadow under the light.

Her phone buzzed.

It was Ye Zhixing.

> still alive

She looked at the two words.

She typed a few characters:

> alive

The reply came straight back:

> have you eaten

> yes

> what did you eat

She glanced at the case of instant noodles under the bed.

> noodles

There was a pause at the other end.

> where are you

She didn’t answer.

Another message came:

> basement?

She stared at the question mark.

> mm

The other end went silent.

After a long while, so long she thought he had gone to sleep, her phone buzzed again.

> dont go out theyre not letting people out now
> if you need anything tell me
> i can find someone on town matters to bring it

She looked at the lines, her finger resting on the screen for a while.

She didn’t reply.

She turned her phone face down on the bed.

Outside the window the snow was still falling. No footsteps, no cars, no firecrackers. The whole county town seemed to have had its pause button pressed, every sound stopped in mid-air, hanging, unable to fall.

She lay down for a while.

Then she sat up again and turned the phone over, and the screen lit up, and the lines were still there.

She typed one:

> dont need anything

Sent it.

She put the phone by her pillow, reached up and pulled the thin cord overhead. The light went out.

In the dark, the little window half buried in snow let in a very faint, grey-white light: the streetlamp shining on the snow, and reflecting off the snow into the room.

## 8. A Bag of Groceries

A little after ten the next morning, someone knocked on the basement’s iron door.

Three knocks, very soft, a pause, then three more.

Gu Xuerong sat up in bed and made no sound. She sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, so long the person outside knocked three more times.

“Miss Gu.” Outside was an old, unhurried voice. “It’s me, Uncle He.”

She got up, went to the door and opened it a crack.

Uncle He stood on the steps. He wore a blue surgical mask, and above the mask a pair of reading glasses, the lenses fogged. Over his grey jacket was a black down vest, and on his arm a red armband printed with a few yellow characters, the pass the neighbourhood committee had issued. Both his hands held things, and there was a cardboard box at his feet. The steps behind him were covered in snow, with only his own line of footprints in it.

“The young master asked me to bring these,” said Uncle He. “Don’t come out, keep your distance. I’ll leave them by the door, you take them in yourself.”

He set the things down on the step by the door one by one, and when he was done, stepped back three paces, back to the top of the steps.

Two big plastic bags, one cardboard box.

“The shop’s closed, and what’s in Old Zhou’s fridge will only go off,” said Uncle He. “Meat, vegetables, eggs, rice, it’s all in there. In the box there’s a little electric pot, plug it in and you can cook, and an extension lead.” He paused. “The young master says all you’ve got is a kettle, you can’t cook anything.”

Gu Xuerong stood in the crack of the door, looking at the pile of things on the step.

“…I don’t need them. Please take them back.”

“I’ve delivered them,” said Uncle He. “If you don’t take them, they’ll freeze out here, and once they’re spoiled, they’ll be thrown away.”

When he had said this, he nodded to her, turned, and stepping in his own footprints, went up the steps one by one, and was gone.

---

She stood behind the crack of the door for a long time.

In the end she opened the door and dragged the two plastic bags and the cardboard box in, one at a time.

The bags were very heavy.

One bag was meat. A big piece of pork belly wrapped in a food bag, frozen rock hard; two boxes of fatty beef rolls, the ones Old Zhou used for his griddle rice; a bag of frozen dumplings. The other bag was vegetables: a big Chinese cabbage, five or six potatoes, a bag of tomatoes, a bunch of spring onions, a knob of ginger, a head of garlic; a tray of eggs, thirty of them, in a cardboard tray, not one broken. At the very bottom was a five-kilo bag of rice and a small bag of dried noodles.

In the cardboard box was a little electric pot, white, round, with a glass lid, the box printed with “Multi-function electric cooking pot 1.5L”.

Under the pot was a sheet of paper. It had been torn off one of the sticky-note pads from the counter at the shop, yellow, and on it was Ye Zhixing’s handwriting, as ugly as ever:

> Old Zhou’s stock, it’ll only go off.
> Keep the rice dry, don’t keep the meat indoors.
> If you can’t cook it ask Su Tang.

She looked at the sticky note for a while, then stuck it on the glass lid of the electric pot.

Her phone buzzed.

It was a transfer.

> Ye Zhixing transferred ¥500.00 to you
> for groceries

She looked at the number and didn’t tap to accept.

After a while, another one:

> if you dont take it it goes back automatically in 24 hours
> up to you

She put the phone down.

---

At two in the afternoon, Su Tang video-called.

Gu Xuerong looked at the green button pulsing on the screen. It pulsed seven or eight times, and just before it rang off, she pressed answer.

Su Tang’s face appeared on the screen. Very close to the camera, so close only half her face showed, one eye, half a bun. Behind her was a very small room, a few certificates of merit on the wall, gone yellow, and a faded “fortune” character stuck on the window.

“Xuerong!” Su Tang’s voice burst out of the phone, so loud Gu Xuerong held it a little further away. “You finally picked up! Ye Zhixing said Uncle He brought you some stuff? Did you take it?”

“…I took it.”

“Show me, let me see what there is.”

Gu Xuerong turned the phone round to point at the two plastic bags on the floor.

Su Tang said “wow” at the other end. “That much? Old Zhou’s emptied out the whole fridge, hasn’t he? Pick it up so I can see, the meat, yes, that piece.”

Gu Xuerong picked up the rock-hard frozen pork belly and held it to the camera.

“Have you got a fridge there?”

“No.”

“So is it cold in your room?”

Gu Xuerong thought about it. The basement had no heating, only a small electric heater; with it on, the room was just about bearable, and once it was off, it soon got cold, and a very thin frost formed on the damp marks on the wall.

“Cold.”

“Then that’s easy,” said Su Tang. “That window of yours, is there snow outside it?”

Gu Xuerong looked up at the little window half buried in snow.

“Yes.”

“Divide up the meat,” said Su Tang. “However much you eat in one meal, cut it into pieces, one piece to a plastic bag, tie the top tight. Then open the window and bury the bags in that heap of snow outside. It’s several degrees below zero out there now; in the snow they’ll freeze harder than in a freezer. When you want to eat some, bring one bag in and let it thaw in a basin, and the rest stay frozen outside.”

Gu Xuerong looked at the screen.

“When our fridge broke before, that’s what my mum did,” said Su Tang. “Everyone up north does it.”

“…What about the beef?”

“Same, bury it in the snow. The dumplings too.”

“And the vegetables?”

“Don’t bury the vegetables, they’ll go mushy once they freeze,” said Su Tang. “Put the cabbage and the potatoes in the coldest corner of the room, not next to the heater. Cover the potatoes with a bag or a box, keep them out of the light; in the light they go green, and the green bits you can’t eat, they’re poisonous. If the outer leaves of the cabbage dry out, don’t throw them away, leave them, they protect the inside. Eat the tomatoes and spring onions first, those two don’t keep.”

“And the eggs?”

“Don’t wash the eggs,” said Su Tang. “If you wash them they keep worse; that layer on the outside protects them. Just leave them in the tray, somewhere cool and shady.”

“And the rice?”

“Is it damp where you are?”

Gu Xuerong glanced at the rings of damp marks along the bottom of the wall.

“Damp.”

“Then you have to seal the rice properly,” said Su Tang. “Roll the top of the bag down, clip it tight, and put another plastic bag over it. If it gets damp it’ll get bugs, it’ll go mouldy, and never, ever eat mouldy rice.”

She said it all very fast, one thing after another, as if reciting something she had long since learned by heart. Gu Xuerong held the phone, squatting beside the two plastic bags, listening, and did each thing as she was told.

She put the pork belly on the table and, with the little knife Ye Zhixing had left— the knife had turned up in the cardboard box, packed with the electric pot— cut at it, stroke by stroke. The meat was frozen too hard; she couldn’t cut it, and the knife stuck in it.

“If it won’t cut, don’t force it!” Su Tang shouted from the screen. “Mind your hand! Leave it there to thaw a bit, cut it once it’s a bit softer.”

Gu Xuerong put the knife down.

“…How did you know I couldn’t cut it.”

“I heard it,” said Su Tang. “The sound the knife makes when it gets stuck.”

---

While the meat thawed, Su Tang taught her to cook.

“Let’s start with the simplest thing. Tomato and egg noodles,” said Su Tang. “That electric pot of yours, plug it in, pour in a bit of oil. Not much, just enough to cover the bottom.”

Gu Xuerong plugged it in and poured in oil. The pot’s indicator light came on, red.

“Crack two eggs, stir them, pour them in. Don’t touch them, wait till the bottom sets, then cut through it with your chopsticks into big pieces, and take them out.”

The eggs went in with a sizzle. Gu Xuerong held her chopsticks and watched the edge of the pool of egg in the pot puff up bit by bit, turn yellow, become a ring of fluffy golden edge.

“Cut it now.”

She cut it.

“Take it out.”

She took it out, into the lid of the pink insulated lunchbox.

“Cut the tomatoes into chunks, put them in, add a bit of salt. Take the spatula— you haven’t got a spatula? Then use your chopsticks, press the tomatoes a bit, press the juice out.”

The tomatoes slowly softened in the pot, red juice coming out, bubbling away. All at once the whole basement was full of that sour, hot smell, covering the damp smell from the walls.

“Add water. One bowl of water. When it boils, put the noodles in. How much noodles? Just you, one small handful, about this thick.” At the other end, Su Tang made a circle with her fingers.

The noodles went in, slowly spreading out in the red soup, softening.

“When the noodles are nearly done, put the egg back in. Scatter the spring onion. Done.”

Gu Xuerong put the egg back in and scattered the spring onion. She stood in front of the electric pot, looking at the steaming pot of red and yellow noodles, and for a moment couldn’t think what came next.

“Done?” Su Tang asked.

“…Done.”

“Show me!”

Gu Xuerong held the phone above the pot.

Su Tang looked for a few seconds at the other end, then suddenly fell silent.

“…Su Tang?”

“It’s nothing.” Su Tang’s voice was a little muffled. “It looks great. Much better than my first time. My first time I burned it.”

She sniffed.

“Eat it while it’s hot. Send me a picture when you’re done.”

---

Gu Xuerong sat on the edge of the bed, holding the inner pot of the electric cooker, eating the noodles with chopsticks, one mouthful at a time.

The noodles had gone a bit soft, the tomato soup was a bit bland, the egg had been cut too small, and there was too much spring onion.

She ate it one mouthful at a time.

When she had finished, she picked up her phone, took a photo of the empty pot and sent it to Su Tang.

Su Tang replied in a second with a sticker, a cat holding up a big thumbs-up.

Then another message:

> tomorrow ill teach you braised pork you can do it in the electric pot too

She looked at the message.

Then she opened the other chat and scrolled up, to the five-hundred-yuan transfer, still there, not expired, the “Accept” button beside it lit up.

Her finger hovered over the button for a long time.

In the end she pressed it.

> Accepted

She typed a few more words:

> took it thanks

After sending it, she put the phone by her pillow, screen down.

The electric pot was still warm, sitting on the table, the yellow sticky note on its glass lid. The steam from the pot had curled up one corner of the note, and the few ugly characters on it had run a little.
