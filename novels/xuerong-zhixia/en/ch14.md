# Chapter Fourteen　The Doorstep

## 1. The Crab

In early February, Uncle He came again.

This time he carried only a polystyrene box, the same one Ye Zhixing had carried up the stairs that day, a few chunks knocked off its four corners, wrapped round several times with wide tape. Two more layers of cling film had been wound round the outside.

As before, he set the box on the step, stepped back three paces to the top of the steps, and spoke through his mask.

“The young master asked me to bring this. The shop’s closed, and with nobody changing that tank of water in the back room every day, it couldn’t hold out.” He paused. “It’s already been dealt with. Scrubbed clean, gills, heart and stomach removed, chopped into pieces. Half is in the top layer, you can cook it today; half is in the bottom layer, frozen, bury it in the snow.”

Gu Xuerong stood in the crack of the door, looking at the polystyrene box.

“…The crab?”

“Mm.”

“The big one?”

“The big one,” said Uncle He. “Two kilos and a hundred and fifty grams.”

He took something else out of the pocket of his down vest and bent to set it beside the box.

A can of beer.

A green can, printed with a brand everyone in the county town knew. The can was ice cold, covered in fine beads of water, and as soon as it touched the step the beads froze into a layer of white frost.

“The young master says,” Uncle He looked at the can of beer, his expression a little odd, “it’s for cooking.”

He turned and went, stepping in his own footprints.

---

The polystyrene box opened onto two layers. The top layer, separated by cling film, held the crab chopped into pieces: the two big claws set apart to one side, their shells already cracked with the flat of a cleaver, showing the snow-white meat inside; the body cut into four pieces, the cut faces milky white with a faint translucent sheen; the top shell turned over beside them, and inside the shell a layer of orange-yellow stuff, set into a solid piece.

The bottom layer was frozen, rock hard, and through the cling film you could see much the same shapes.

That claw, Gu Xuerong recognized.

Blue-green, the glaze of old porcelain, the row of orange-red teeth on the inside thick as pebbles. Now it was cracked open, lying in the polystyrene box, not moving.

She squatted there, forgetting the door was still open, the cold wind pouring in.

Her phone buzzed.

> get it

It was Ye Zhixing.

> got it

> can you cook it

She didn’t reply.

> if not well video call

She typed a line:

> wasnt it your pet

At the other end, “typing…” flickered on and off for ages, but no words came.

Then a video came through.

---

The video showed a room.

Not big, like a converted storeroom, no windows, the light white and very bright. Against the walls stood two rows of metal shelving, running from floor to ceiling, level upon level, and on every level, neatly lined up, a row of off-white plastic boxes. The boxes were square, each about the size of a shoebox, one next to another, like a wall full of drawers. Into the side of every box ran a thin tube, and out of it ran another, and all the tubes joined a thick pipe under the shelving that led to a very large blue tank in the corner. The tank hummed, and you could hear water running through the tubes, very fine, very dense, like rain.

The camera moved in on one box.

On top of the box was a small lid with a few holes in it. The person holding the camera lifted the lid.

Inside was a crab. Palm-sized, a blue-green shell, its two claws drawn in front of it, crouched in a shallow layer of water at the bottom of the box; when it saw the light, it shrank back into a corner.

The lid closed. The camera moved along and lifted another. Another crab. Lifted another. The same.

One crab to a cell. Dozens of cells, dozens of crabs.

At the end of the video the camera turned round to face the person holding the phone. Half of Ye Zhixing’s face, in a mask, his eyes a little puffy, as if he hadn’t slept well.

“See,” his voice came muffled through the mask. “This thing’s called a crab apartment. One crab to a cell; you can’t put them together, put them together and they fight, and once they’ve fought they eat each other, the big ones eat the small ones, the hard-shelled ones eat the ones that have just moulted. So it has to be one to a room.”

“The big one was the one I’d kept longest, and the biggest. Once it got past two kilos it barely grew, and one more moult and it most likely wouldn’t have made it through. In that tub at the shop, with nobody changing the water every day, it was holding out even less.”

The video cut off there.

Straight after it came another message:

> dont feel bad
> most people never get to eat one of these inland one that big costs thousands
> but its not like cats or dogs ive had it over a year and it doesnt know who i am it never comes over to you
> i cant treat it the way i treat the cats
> go ahead and eat it

She thought of the way that claw had opened. All at once, snap, very wide, wide enough to fit an egg. Thought of it following her fingers, left, right, left again.

It had followed her.

But it didn’t know anyone.

She typed a few words:

> it followed my hand

For a long time there was no reply.

After about five minutes:

> it was looking for a chance to pinch you

She looked at the sentence, and the corner of her mouth moved.

> mm

> did you get the beer

> got it

> then lets start

## 2. Beer

Ye Zhixing taught her.

A video call; behind him was a white wall with a clock on it, and the clock said three-forty in the afternoon. He didn’t show his face; the camera was only on one of his hands and a notebook, on which a few lines of steps were written in very ugly handwriting, and he read them out.

“Plug in the electric pot. Oil, a bit more than usual.”

She put in the oil.

“Slice the ginger, cut the spring onion into lengths, smash a few cloves of garlic. When the oil’s hot, ginger in first, then garlic, give them a quick fry, till they smell.”

The ginger went in with a sizzle. The garlic followed, and all at once a sharp, warm fragrance rose through the basement.

“Crab in. Claws and body first, top shell last. Once they’re in, don’t rush to turn them, let them fry in the oil a while, till the shell changes colour.”

She put the pieces of crab in one by one.

The blue-grey shell hissed as it touched the hot oil. After a while, the edges of the shell began to change colour, from blue-grey to a faint orange-red, then redder and redder, reddening fast, as if someone had lit a lamp under that layer of shell. The two claws turned red too, the blue-green glaze fading bit by bit, replaced by a very bright, fully cooked orange-red.

She held her chopsticks and watched the two claws change colour, so absorbed she forgot to turn them.

“Has it gone red?”

“…It has.”

“Turn them over, fry the other side.”

She turned them.

“Now light soy sauce, one spoon; dark soy sauce, half a spoon, for colour; sugar, a small spoon. Mix it all through.”

She did as she was told. The sauce coated the pieces, and the crab turned a shining red.

“Open the beer.”

She picked up the can of beer. It had sat outside the door all morning and was painfully cold. She pulled the ring, and with a hiss white foam surged out of the can and all over her hand.

“Pour it in. Till it nearly covers the crab. You don’t have to use it all.”

The beer went into the pot with a great sizzle, and white foam surged up all at once, churning, with a faint smell of malt and a little bitterness. The whole basement filled with that smell: soy sauce, crab, and the yeasty fragrance of beer bubbling.

“Why beer?” she asked.

“Beer gets rid of the fishy smell,” said Ye Zhixing. “Things from the sea are fishy, and the bit of alcohol and the taste of the grain in beer cover it up. And the sauce you get from braising in beer is sweet, with a little bitterness, it goes with the roe.” He paused. “Uncle He said so. I asked him too.”

“…You can’t cook?”

“I can eat.”

She said nothing, but Ye Zhixing seemed to hear something, and clicked his tongue at the other end.

“Put the lid on. Braise for fifteen minutes. Turn it once halfway. At the end take the lid off and reduce the sauce, till it’s thick and clings to the shell.”

She put the lid on.

The glass lid of the electric pot quickly fogged over, and she couldn’t see inside any more; she could only hear the bubbling, and the hiss of the sauce rising at the rim of the lid and falling back into the pot.

“Ye Zhixing, what time is it there?”

“Same as where you are. Three-fifty,” he said. “We’re not exactly in different time zones.”

“Where are you?”

The camera wobbled as he switched the phone to his other hand, and a length of lamp post flashed past the edge of the picture.

“Out,” he said. “You keep braising, I’m going to hang up. Send me a picture when it’s done.”

The video ended.

---

Fifteen minutes later, she lifted the lid.

A cloud of steam rushed up, carrying a smell of beer, soy sauce and crab meat mixed together, so rich it was almost solid. The sauce in the pot had nearly reduced, glossy red, clinging to every piece of shell, and the roe in the top shell had melted into the sauce, dyeing the whole pot a very deep orange.

She picked up a claw with her chopsticks.

The claw was heavy, a real weight in her hand. Through the crack where it had been split showed a whole length of white meat, very tender, trembling at the touch of a chopstick.

She took a bite.

Very savoury. So savoury it was sweet, with a little bitterness from the beer, a little saltiness from the soy sauce, melting on her tongue layer by layer.

She ate one mouthful at a time. She finished one claw, then a piece of the body, then scraped the remaining layer of roe out of the top shell with her chopsticks and mixed it into a bowl of plain rice.

When she had finished, she took a photo. In the photo was an electric pot more than half empty, a little sauce and a few bits of broken shell left at the bottom, and beside it a top shell scraped perfectly clean, not a scrap of roe left in it.

She sent the photo to Ye Zhixing.

Then sent another message:

> it was delicious

It was a long time before he replied:

> mm

> good

## 3. Online Classes

On the tenth of February, online classes began.

No. 8 High used DingTalk. Every student installed it on their phone; every morning you clocked in at seven-twenty, and the first class started at seven-thirty. The teachers lectured at home in front of their computers, and the students listened at home in front of their phones. Eight classes a day. The four evening lessons had moved online too, but with no teacher keeping watch, you clocked in and then came or didn’t as you pleased, and within a few days hardly anyone was there.

Gu Xuerong took her online classes in the basement.

The signal wasn’t great. The basement walls were thick, the phone’s single bar of signal came and went, the teacher’s voice often broke up into bursts of electronic noise, and the picture would freeze on one slide, and stay frozen for minutes at a time. If she held the phone up under that little window, held it a bit higher, the signal was a bit better. So she held it up like that, held it for a whole class, until her arm ached.

The teaching wasn’t up to much either.

Ms. Li lectured on functions in front of her computer, and partway through she would just read out the slides; the text on the slides was tiny, and she read fast, and when she finished one slide she went on to the next, never asking whether anyone had understood. Not that she could ask: the forty-odd students were each just a little grey square, no face, no voice, only a name.

Halfway through politics class, the teacher’s connection dropped and never came back.

Zheng Xin taught English conscientiously, but lecturing alone at a computer for forty-five minutes, her voice went hoarse partway through, and she stopped three times to drink water.

Gu Xuerong listened, and none of it went in.

She wanted to listen. But all of it came out of a small, stuttering screen, a sentence at a time, as if through a very thick pane of glass, and couldn’t reach her.

Chen Xi’s classes started at eight every evening and finished at half past nine.

He ran them in a QQ group classroom: a dozen-odd from Class Seven came, plus Su Tang, twenty-something people in all. He propped his phone on a stack of books, the camera pointed at his desk, and on the desk were chopsticks, an eraser, a bottle, a bowl of water, a few marbles, and that fifty-centimetre fish tank of his— the tank now had an air pump in it, humming and bubbling, and the two crucian carp in it were doing just fine.

The way he taught was exactly the same as when he used to tutor Gu Xuerong. No formulas; things.

When he got to buoyancy, he pushed an empty bottle down into the bowl. When he got to momentum, he knocked two marbles together on the desk. When he got to reaction rates, he dropped an effervescent vitamin tablet into water, and had everyone watch the strings and strings of bubbles.

He talked very fast, but never got a word wrong. His blackboard was a sheet of white paper; he derived formulas on it, writing line after line down the page, writing very fast, his handwriting crooked, but every step was right, every coefficient balanced perfectly, and he never had to go back and correct anything. At first a few of the strong students from Class Seven tried to catch him out; after three days of listening, they stopped, and started copying his derivations into their notebooks.

He could always find a thing, something you could see and touch, and press those principles that sounded so mysterious in the textbook onto that thing, press them on so they fit perfectly.

Lin Xiaoman came every day and asked a pile of questions every day, until Chen Xi was scratching his head.

“Chen Xi, why does that bottle float?”

“I just explained! Because the water it pushes aside is heavier than it is!”

“What does pushes aside mean?”

“…It means it shoves the water out of the way! You push it down, it shoves that much water off to the side, and the weight of that water is the force holding it up!”

“Oh… then why—”

“Lin Xiaoman, let me have a drink of water.”

“Ten yuan a lesson, do you charge for the time you spend drinking?”

“…No. I’ll drink fast.”

Gu Xuerong didn’t say anything.

Her camera was off, her microphone was off, and only now and then did she type a word or two in the chat box. But she came every day, and was there every day. When Chen Xi asked partway through, “Got it?”, she typed an “mm” in the chat box.

She asked one question. Only once.

That day Chen Xi was teaching conservation of momentum, knocking two marbles together, one stopping, the other shooting off. When he finished, he asked if there were any questions. The chat box was quiet for a while, and then a line popped up:

> if two marbles stick together after they collide is momentum still conserved and where does the energy that goes missing go

Chen Xi looked at the line and stopped short.

That was something they wouldn’t learn until next term. Perfectly inelastic collisions.

He looked up into the camera, at that grey little square with the name “Gu Xuerong” on it.

“Momentum is conserved,” he said. “Kinetic energy isn’t; the missing part turns into heat, turns into sound, turns into that moment when the marbles get squashed.” He paused. “How did you think of asking that?”

There was no reply in the chat box.

After a long while, two words popped up:

> just did

Chen Xi smiled at the camera.

“Right,” he said. “Then there’s an extra lesson tomorrow. No extra charge.”

The last line was the balance.

How many months that money would last, she didn’t work out. She closed it.

## 4. MC

Su Tang and Lin Xiaoman came to find her a different way.

With the epidemic, nobody could go out, and Su Tang couldn’t really come to the basement. But every day or two she video-called. Once, the moment the call connected, Su Tang stared at the screen for several seconds and suddenly said, “Xuerong, your face has got rounder.”

Gu Xuerong was startled, and touched her face without thinking.

“It has!” Su Tang leaned in toward the screen. “Your chin’s not so pointy. And those two dark patches under your eyes are lighter. What time have you been going to bed lately?”

“…One.”

“And before?”

“Three.”

At the other end, Su Tang said “wow” and laughed until her eyes curved into two slits, as if this were her own good result.

Sometimes the calls were just her, sometimes all three of them together. She didn’t talk about anything serious, she just chatted. About what vegetables the supermarket downstairs had got in that day, about how her mum couldn’t go to work at the textile mill any more and watched TV dramas at home all day and cried halfway through; about some dish she had made at home and how it had turned out, and then she would point the phone at the pan to show Gu Xuerong.

Lin Xiaoman came even more often. She had gone back to the village, and the village entrance was blocked with mounds of earth and tractors, nobody allowed in or out. She was stuck at home day after day, going stir crazy, and could send Gu Xuerong dozens of messages a day. The chickens in her family’s yard, the dog at their gate, her dad chopping firewood in the yard, her mum steaming a whole pot of mantou, steamed too big, each one the size of two of her fists.

“Xuerong, look! This mantou is bigger than my face!”

Gu Xuerong looked at the photo. In it, Lin Xiaoman held up a big, white, plump mantou in front of her face, only her two eyes showing over it, curved with laughter.

She saved the photo.

---

Later, it was Su Tang who suggested it.

“Let’s all play MC together!”

“What’s MC?” Lin Xiaoman asked.

“Minecraft,” said Su Tang. “The one I heard about, where Xuerong fell in the lava at the shop that time.”

“Oh, I can do that one! My little cousin plays it every day!”

So the three of them started a server.

Su Tang set it up. She chose Peaceful mode, saying that way there were no monsters and you couldn’t die. “Xuerong, don’t worry, this time if you dig next to lava, I’ll be right behind you holding on to you.”

The moment Lin Xiaoman got in she started running around everywhere, ran straight into a river and floundered in it for ages, shouting “help, I can’t swim.” At the other end Su Tang laughed until she couldn’t breathe, and said you can’t drown in the game, press space and you float up.

Gu Xuerong spawned in a forest.

She stood where she was, looked around for a while, and didn’t move.

“Xuerong? Are you frozen?”

“…Don’t know what to do.”

Last time at the shop, all she had known was how to dig down. This time Su Tang wouldn’t let her dig; she said she’d teach her to “play properly” first.

“Punch a tree first,” said Su Tang. “Face the trunk, hold down the left button and don’t let go.”

Gu Xuerong held it down. Cracks spread across the trunk, and with a snap a block of wood dropped out and floated on the ground, spinning. She walked over, and the wood was sucked into her inventory.

“Open your inventory. Put the wood in that little grid, see, four planks come out on the right. Take them out.”

“…They’re out.”

“Four planks in a square, that makes a crafting table. Put it on the ground. Right-click it.”

A three-by-three grid popped up.

“From now on you make everything in this grid,” said Su Tang. “The shape you lay out is the shape of the thing you make. Two planks stacked vertically make sticks. Three planks along the top, two sticks down the middle, makes a wooden pickaxe. Use the wooden pickaxe on stone, put the stone you knock off in place of the planks, and it’s a stone pickaxe.”

Gu Xuerong laid them out one by one. She got it wrong once, put the sticks in crooked, and nothing came out. She took the sticks out, laid them straight again, and out came the stone pickaxe.

“Eight blocks of stone in a ring with the middle empty is a furnace,” said Su Tang. “Put iron ore in it, burn wood or coal underneath, and you can smelt iron. Coal and a stick together make a torch; stick it on a wall at night and it won’t be dark.”

“…And a bed?”

Su Tang paused.

“A bed needs wool,” she said. “Three wool in a row, three planks underneath. For wool you find sheep and shear them, or—”

“White?”

“Sheep are white, so if you shear them you get white wool. If you want a different colour you have to dye it,” said Su Tang. “For red you find flowers, those little red ones, poppies or roses, put them in the grid and you get red dye. Red dye plus white bone meal is pink.”

Her character turned and walked into the forest. Su Tang watched her chop trees for a while, squat beside a flock of sheep for a while, wander round and round on the grass for a while, bending to pick the tiny red flowers dotted across it one by one. She did it very slowly, very carefully; every time she made something, she opened the crafting table, laid out the grid, and when it was laid out, she looked at the thing that appeared on the right for a moment before taking it out.

“Xuerong,” Lin Xiaoman said quietly on voice chat, “she’s being so serious.”

“Shh,” said Su Tang.

When she had made all that, she walked to the riverbank, found a flat piece of ground, and began to build a house.

She built very slowly, very carefully. First the foundations, block after block of stone, laid perfectly evenly; then the walls, wooden, raised layer by layer; windows cut, a door cut; and last the roof.

Su Tang and Lin Xiaoman ran over to look.

“Xuerong, what are you building?”

“A house.”

“Who’s it for?”

Gu Xuerong didn’t answer, and went on building.

The house was finished. A little two-storey wooden building, one big window on the ground floor, two small windows upstairs. The door was on the ground floor.

Su Tang walked once round the house, came to the door, opened it and went in.

The ground floor had nothing in it. Empty. The floor was wood, the window very large. The sun in the game had already sunk behind the hills, the sky at the edge an orange-red, and the last light slanted in through the window, laid across the floor in long stretched squares.

“And upstairs?” Lin Xiaoman asked from behind.

Gu Xuerong walked to a corner of the room, where a ladder leaned against the wall, leading up to the first floor. She climbed up. Su Tang and Lin Xiaoman followed.

Upstairs there were three beds.

One red, one pink, one white. In a row, beside the two small windows.

Lin Xiaoman stood in front of the three beds for a long time.

“…These are ours?”

Gu Xuerong stood by the window with her back to them, looking out. Outside was the river, across the river a forest, behind the forest a mountain, and on top of the mountain, snow.

“Mm.”

Lin Xiaoman went over to the pink bed and right-clicked it, and her character lay down on it.

“I’m sleeping in this one!” she shouted, her voice suddenly hoarse. “I-I’m sleeping in this one!”

Su Tang stood by the white bed for a moment, then lay down on it too.

Gu Xuerong was still standing by the window.

“Xuerong,” said Su Tang, “is the red one yours?”

“Mm.”

“Then lie down too.”

“Not sleepy.”

“It’s dark, if you lie down you skip the night,” said Su Tang. “But all three of us have to lie down together; if one person doesn’t, it won’t get light.”

Gu Xuerong stood there and didn’t move.

Lin Xiaoman’s little figure had already lain down, and so had Su Tang’s. She walked over and lay down on the red bed.

The screen slowly dimmed, and slowly brightened again.

It was morning.

Outside the window the river sparkled in the sunlight, the forest was green, the snow on top of the mountain was white. Three little figures sat up in three beds and stood in that small upstairs room.

“…I’m hungry,” said Lin Xiaoman. “Can you eat in the game?”

“You can,” said Su Tang. “Farming. I’ll teach you how to farm.”

## 5. Deliveries

Ye Zhixing hardly said anything in the group these days.

He was busy. So busy that the messages he sent were always at one or two in the morning, so busy that the background on his video calls was always changing: sometimes a white wall, sometimes the driver’s seat of a minivan, sometimes a supermarket storeroom with case after case of instant noodles and bottled water stacked up behind him.

Town Matters had changed.

At the very top of the home page was a new red banner that said “Epidemic Information”, and when you tapped it, it was divided into sections: supplies requests, medicine requests, volunteer sign-up, estate notices. If someone’s family was short of medicine, they posted; if someone’s elderly relative was home alone with nobody looking after them, they posted; if an estate was sealed, and what it was short of, they posted. Once posted, someone in the back end read it, and when they’d read it, they passed the information to the neighbourhood committee, to the volunteers, to whoever could help.

None of these posts cost anything, and none of them could be pinned.

“Why can’t they be pinned?” Su Tang had asked once on a video call.

“Pinning costs money,” said Ye Zhixing. “If at a time like this whoever has money gets seen first, then it isn’t asking for help any more.”

“Then who gets seen first?”

“Whoever’s most urgent,” said Ye Zhixing. “There are people in the back end reading them one by one, sorting them one by one.”

“Who reads them?”

“Me,” said Ye Zhixing. “And the people at the company.”

The people at the company, Su Tang learned later, were four in all. One in charge of tech, one in charge of customer service, two who did the street promotion. All young people from the county town, who normally worked in a small rented office; when the epidemic came, the office closed, and the four of them stayed at home, one computer each, one phone each, reading from seven in the morning till midnight.

Later, they did more than read.

The neighbourhood committees didn’t have enough people. The county town was sealed, each estate with one gate, a table at the gate, and behind the table two or three people in red armbands, taking temperatures, signing people in, issuing passes. The people in the estates couldn’t get out; someone had to deliver their vegetables, deliver their medicine, deliver their rice, noodles and oil. The committees couldn’t cope, so they posted a call for volunteers on Town Matters.

When Ye Zhixing saw it, he was the first to sign up.

The two street promoters from the company signed up too. Uncle Chen signed up too, driving the Ye family’s black car, the boot stuffed with vegetables and medicine. Uncle He was too old, and the committee wouldn’t let him go out, so he helped with registration in the committee office, writing down item by item who had asked for what and whether it had been delivered.

---

Ye Zhixing covered the south of town.

Every morning at eight he collected supplies at the committee, carried them case after case onto the vehicle, and when they were loaded, delivered them household by household from the list. He drove a borrowed little electric three-wheeler, its cargo bed covered with a blue plastic sheet, the plastic sheet covered in snow.

He delivered fast. He knew the south of town well: which building was where, which way each stairwell door faced, which household had steps at the door, which building’s stair light was broken, he could have done it with his eyes closed. He would put the things at the door, knock three times, step back two paces, and when someone inside called through the door “got it”, he left.

Sometimes the people inside would open the door. Open it a crack, show half a face, look at him, and say “thank you, young man.” He would nod, in his mask, his expression impossible to see.

There was one old woman who lived alone, over eighty, on the fifth floor of an old building with no lift. Every three days he brought her vegetables. The first time he delivered, the old woman opened the door, looked him up and down, and said, “You’re the Ye family’s boy, aren’t you?”

He was taken aback.

“I’ve seen you on Town Matters,” said the old woman. “That video of yours, the cat one.”

The old woman passed a plastic bag out through the crack of the door, and in the bag were two boiled eggs, still warm.

“Take them,” she said. “In this cold.”

He took them. On his way downstairs, he stood in the stairwell, peeled one egg and ate it. The other he put in his pocket.

---

The twentieth of February.

On that day’s list, there was one address he read twice.

South of town, Back Street, two turns in. The side of an old building. Basement.

Supplies: one refrigerator (small, single door).

Requested by: Ye Zhixing.

In the committee office he held the list and turned it over to look at the back. There was nothing on the back. The person who had registered it was Uncle He. Uncle He sat behind the table in his reading glasses, writing something else, his head down, not looking up.

“Uncle He.”

“Mm.”

“This,” Ye Zhixing turned the list round, “did you fill it in?”

Uncle He looked up and glanced at him over the top of his reading glasses.

“You told me last week,” said Uncle He. “Said the basement had no fridge, the meat was buried in snow, what happens when the snow melts. So I bought one for you, put the order under your name, sent it along with the other supplies. The money came off that card of yours.”

Ye Zhixing looked at him.

“…Thank you, Uncle He.”

Uncle He lowered his head and went on writing.

“Young master,” he said, without raising his eyes, “go on.”

---

The fridge was a small single-door one, white, half a person tall, packed in a cardboard box wound round and round with packing straps.

Ye Zhixing lifted it into the bed of the three-wheeler, tied it down tight with rope, and covered it with the blue plastic sheet.

He rode the three-wheeler from the committee toward Back Street.

Back Street was very quiet. The copy shop’s roller shutter was down, with “Closed during the epidemic” stuck on it. The thick plastic curtain at the door of the internet café had frozen into a stiff board. The patch of ground where the braised-snack stall used to be was covered in snow, so you couldn’t tell what it had been. The curtains upstairs were drawn, dark green, not moving.

He took one turn, then another.

The streetlamps grew sparser. The buildings on both sides grew older, the little ads on the walls soaked white by the snow, peeling off layer by layer.

He stopped at the side of that old building.

The seven steps were covered in snow, without a single footprint. The iron door at the bottom of the steps was shut, its green paint blotchy and flaking. The little window beside the door, level with the pavement, was mostly buried in snow, only a strip at the very top showing, and through the strip came a little yellow light.

He parked the three-wheeler by the road, untied the rope, and lifted the cardboard box down from the cargo bed.

It was very heavy. He held it in his arms and went down one step at a time. Seven steps, and he went very slowly, testing each one with his foot first, making sure of his footing, before stepping down.

At the bottom of the steps, he set the cardboard box beside the iron door, against the wall, steady.

He stood in front of the door.

There were sounds inside. Very faint: the bubbling of the electric pot, and a woman’s voice coming from a phone, saying something about “…the periodicity of trigonometric functions…”. A recording of an online class.

He stood there a while.

He didn’t knock.

He stepped back two paces, to the bottom of the steps, and pulled his mask down a little, uncovering his mouth.

“Gu Xuerong.”

Inside, the sound of the online class stopped.

“Fridge,” he said. “It’s at the door.”

There was no sound inside.

He called out again:

“The fridge is at the door! Plug it in and wait two hours before you put anything in it!”

Then he turned and went up, one step at a time.

When he reached the fifth step, he heard the hinges of the iron door creak behind him.

He didn’t look back.

At the top of the steps he pulled his mask back up, swung onto the three-wheeler and twisted the handle. The three-wheeler slid a little on the snow, steadied, and moved off.

---

Gu Xuerong stood in the doorway.

The iron door was open a crack, and cold wind poured in through it, into her collar. She was wearing the grey thermal set with her winter uniform over it, barefoot on the cement floor by the door, and the floor was ice cold.

She looked at the cardboard box by the door.

The box was wound with packing straps, a few flakes of snow on the straps, not yet melted. On the side of the box was a picture of a fridge and a line of words: “Single-door mini fridge · Fridge & freezer”.

She raised her head and looked up the steps.

On the steps there was only one line of new footprints, one after another, running from the bottom of the steps to the very top, and then vanishing at the roadside. On the snow by the road were two tyre tracks, from a three-wheeler, winding off into the distance, lit by the streetlamps, a stretch bright, a stretch dark.

She stood there looking at the two tracks for a long time, so long the soles of her feet went numb with cold.

Then she bent down and dragged the box toward the door.

The box was very heavy. She dragged it very slowly, edging it in bit by bit, in through the door, over the threshold, to the wall, next to the corner covered with the blue-and-white checked cloth.

She took off the straps, opened the box, and lifted the fridge out of the foam. White, very new, a layer of protective film on the door.

She found the power cord and plugged it into the extension lead.

The fridge’s indicator light came on, green. After a while there was a very faint click inside the fridge, and then the sound of the compressor starting, a hum, very low, very steady.

She squatted in front of the fridge, listening to the sound.

It had always been very quiet in the basement. Before, there had only been her own breathing, the occasional bubbling of the electric pot, the crackle of the teacher’s stuttering voice in the online classes, and now and then a car passing outside the window. Now there was one more sound. A hum, very low, very steady, going on and on, never stopping.

She sat on the floor beside the fridge, her back against the wall, and listened for a long time.

Later she noticed the protective film on the fridge door was still on. When she peeled it off it made a loud ripping sound, and she jerked back in fright and knocked the back of her head on the wall.

## 6. Freezing Point

It was at the end of February that Ye Zhixing was thrown out of his home.

That evening, he had just finished the last delivery, and by the time he got home it was nearly midnight. He stamped the snow off his feet at the door and pushed it open. The living room light was on.

Ye Shilong was sitting on the sofa.

He wore that dark cashmere cardigan, that gold watch on his wrist, and on the coffee table in front of him a stack of paper was spread out. Printed paper, A4, sheet after sheet, and Ye Zhixing recognized it at a glance.

It was his partnership proposal. “Annual Advertising and Job-Posting Partnership Proposal for Ye’s Building Materials and the East and South Developments”. And copies of a few contracts, a few transfer records, a few pages of statements.

“Sit,” said Ye Shilong.

Ye Zhixing didn’t sit. He stood in the entrance hall, still in his down jacket, the snow on his soles melting into water, spreading into a small puddle on the floor.

His mother stood at the foot of the stairs, her hand on the banister, not coming down, not going up. Ye Zhixia’s door was open a crack, no light in the crack, but Ye Zhixing knew she was behind it.

“Last month,” Ye Shilong picked up the top sheet, “the east and south developments ran ads on your Town Matters, home page, long term, fifty thousand a month.”

“The building-materials company’s job posts, through your platform, charged per post, over thirty thousand a month.” Ye Shilong picked up another sheet. “And the logistics fleet, and the property management, and—”

He put the stack back on the coffee table, very gently.

“Since the epidemic, the sales office has been shut for a month. Not one person has come to look at a flat.” He looked up at his son. “The workers on the sites weren’t let go home before New Year, and now they’re all stuck in there, eating and sleeping on site, can’t get in or out. Not a single new person can get in. These ads, who are they for? These job posts, who are they hiring?”

Ye Zhixing stood there.

“The contracts are normal contracts,” he said. “The services are real, the prices are fair, the ads are all labelled—”

“I didn’t say you were breaking the law,” Ye Shilong cut him off.

His voice wasn’t loud, wasn’t fast, just as it had been that day in the study leafing through the report. The slight smile at the corners of his mouth was gone, his eyes were very calm.

“I’m saying, at a time like this, you’re still carrying things out.”

The living room was very quiet. The radiator gave a click.

“Before the epidemic, you carried things out, and I turned a blind eye,” said Ye Shilong. “I know what you’re thinking. You think this family’s about to collapse, and you want to put a bit more by for yourself. Fine, you’re my son, I let you. But now—”

“Now the banks call every day. The sites can’t stop; stop for a day and the costs of feeding the men keep running, the interest on the loans keeps running, and if the schedule slips, everything after it slips. The demolition payments for the North City, the second instalment is due next month. The loan on the west-side land falls due in March. The east and south developments can’t sell a single flat, not a cent is coming back. At a time like this, every cent in the accounts has someone watching it.”

He looked at Ye Zhixing.

“Every cent you move out of here, the banks can see, the creditors can see, the people at City Hall can see. What will they think? They’ll think the Ye family is moving its assets. The Ye family is about to do a runner.”

Ye Zhixing’s hands clenched in the pockets of his down jacket.

“Do you know what that charge is called?”

“…I know.”

“You don’t,” said Ye Shilong. “If you knew, you wouldn’t be doing this.”

He stood up.

He was a little taller than Ye Zhixing, and when he stood, the living room light was behind him, throwing his shadow over Ye Zhixing.

“The contracts are all terminated tomorrow,” he said. “Not one more cent goes over. That Town Matters of yours, from today on, has nothing whatsoever to do with the Ye family.”

Ye Zhixing looked up.

“You,” said Ye Shilong, “move out of this house.”

---

At the foot of the stairs, his mother’s hand tightened on the banister.

“Shilong—”

“Let him go.” Ye Shilong didn’t turn round. “Doesn’t he want to fly on his own? Then let him fly on his own.”

Ye Zhixing looked at his father.

He wanted to say a lot of things. Wanted to say that the hundred-odd workers on the west-side site hadn’t gone home even on New Year’s Eve, and their wives were posting on Town Matters asking whether anyone knew how the men on the site were doing, were they getting fed, was anyone running a fever, and he had seen every single one. Wanted to say you know this family’s about to collapse too, so why did you still take the North City land? Wanted to say that admission ticket of yours, that red arowana of yours, that nod you gave me at the banquet, what did any of it actually mean? Wanted to say, that business three years ago, why weren’t you afraid of people seeing that?

He said none of it.

He turned, opened the door, and walked out.

The door closed behind him. Not hard, but solidly, with a click, the latch springing into the strike plate, a perfect fit.

---

He stood at the door for a while.

The osmanthus tree in the courtyard was laden with thick snow, the snow lying along its bare branches, one by one, like a white skeleton. Under the streetlamp the snow was a bluish white.

His phone buzzed.

It was Ye Zhixia.

> bro

He looked at the word.

> where are you going

He thought for a moment, and typed a few words:

> the shop

> is there heating there

> theres an electric heater

There was a long pause at the other end.

> wait a sec

A few minutes later, the little side gate by the courtyard gate opened. Ye Zhixia, wrapped in a down jacket, slippers on her feet, a big plastic bag in her arms, ran out through it, through the snow, all the way to him.

She shoved the plastic bag into his arms.

In the bag were his quilt, his pillow, two sweaters, a power bank, and a phone charger.

“Mum told me to bring them,” said Ye Zhixia, out of breath, her nose bright red with cold. “She doesn’t dare come out.”

Ye Zhixing held the plastic bag and looked at her.

Ye Zhixia looked back at him. She wasn’t crying, but her eyes were red.

“Bro,” she said, “it was like this for you back then too.”

Ye Zhixing said nothing.

“Three years ago,” said Ye Zhixia. “Back then, you were standing outside on your own too.”

She sniffed.

“Back then I was little, I didn’t understand anything, I just watched from upstairs,” she said. “I’m not little now.”

She reached out and gave his arm one very hard slap.

“Give me five minutes.”

“What for?”

She didn’t answer, turned and ran back, her slippers leaving a line of crooked footprints in the snow.

Five minutes later, the side gate opened again.

She had changed into snow boots, with her school bag on her back, bulging, and a small suitcase in her hand, its wheels ploughing two furrows through the snow.

Ye Zhixing looked at her.

“What are you doing?”

“Coming with you.”

“Go back.”

“No.” Ye Zhixia set the suitcase down at his feet. “I told Mum. Mum said, keep an eye on your brother, don’t let him starve.”

“…She really said that?”

“More or less,” said Ye Zhixia. “She cried. And when she’d finished crying, she pushed a thousand yuan on me and told me not to tell Dad.”

She tilted her face up and looked at him. Her nose was bright red with cold, her eyes red too, but her chin was up, raised very high.

“I don’t want to stay in that house either,” she said. “He’s on the phone every day, smoking in front of that tank of golden arowana every day, the whole living room full of smoke. I can’t even get through my online classes.”

Ye Zhixing looked at that chin of hers, held so high.

He bent down and picked up her little suitcase.

“The shop’s cold.”

“I know.”

“No bed, you’ll sleep on the sofa.”

“I know.”

“The Ragdoll yowls at night.”

“If Your Highness yowls, I’ll yowl right back.”

Ye Zhixing said nothing more, and carried the suitcase off toward the mouth of the lane. Ye Zhixia, her school bag on her back, followed behind him, stepping in his footprints.

---

That night, Ye Zhixing and Ye Zhixia moved into the cat café.

Upstairs was very cold. The fan heater had been off for over a month, and the room was about as cold as outside, their breath white in the air. He switched on the electric heater, moved it next to the grey sofa, spread the quilt on the sofa and put the pillow on the armrest.

The cats were all awake.

Rice Cake jumped down from the windowsill, walked to the sofa, glanced up at him, jumped onto the sofa, trod a few circles on the quilt, and lay down. Briquette poked its head out from the top of the cat tree, looked at him once, and drew back again. Calico was the first to burrow under the quilt. The Ragdoll yowled once in the back room, not loudly, as if saying hello.

Ye Zhixia took the other sofa, wrapped in the quilt she had brought, and the moment she lay down, the Devon burrowed in, one big ear sticking out from the corner of the quilt. The Ragdoll in the back room yowled once. Ye Zhixia rolled over and said toward the back room, “Your Highness, go to sleep.” The Ragdoll yowled once more, and then really did stop.

Ye Zhixing sat on the sofa, the quilt over him to the waist, Rice Cake pressing down on his legs, Calico nosing about in the quilt by his feet.

The electric heater hummed. Its orange-red light fell on his face, half bright, half dark.

He took out his phone and opened the Town Matters back end.

Epidemic Information. New requests today: forty-seven. Handled: forty-two. Pending: five.

He read them one by one.

When he had read them, he passed the five pending ones on, to the neighbourhood committees, to the volunteers covering the north of town tomorrow.

Then he opened another page. The company’s account balance.

Without those few Ye family contracts, next month’s income would be somewhat less. But those contracts had only been signed in the last few months; before that, Town Matters had never relied on the Ye family. Job posts, second-hand, rentals, pinning, ads, one small sum after another, and the stream they added up to had always been there, and in the epidemic it had actually grown fuller: everyone in the county town who couldn’t get out was on it, looking for things, selling things, asking about things.

The four employees’ wages, the office rent, the server costs, the thousand a month to Gu Xuerong, and now, on top of that, food and drink for two.

He worked it out.

Enough.

He put the phone down.

On the other sofa, Ye Zhixia was already asleep, her breathing very light, the Devon’s big ear rising and falling with her breath.

Outside the window it had started snowing again. Very fine, very light, falling on the upstairs window with its safety mesh, falling outside the dark green curtains, without a sound.

Rice Cake rolled over on his legs, showing its belly.

He reached out and rested his hand lightly on that patch of ginger-and-white belly.

Rice Cake lifted a hind leg and kicked him.
